Limits And Traps

A reader-facing guardrail against overgeneralizing the course machinery. These are the places where the concept, theme, or equation can be precise and still be misused.

Concept-Level Traps

Concept limit

Preferences

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to think preferences are emotions. In the course they are a disciplined choice ordering, not a psychological biography.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by asking whether the preference is realistic for every human mood. The course is doing a modeling move: it asks whether the relevant choices in the problem can be treated as stable enough for strategic analysis.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is that preferences describe rankings within the modeled choice problem; they do not explain where desires come from. If the lecture question is about persuasion, learning, or changing tastes, preference is only the starting input, not the full explanation.

What breaks without it: Without preferences, a payoff table has no disciplined source. The analyst could assign numbers, but there would be no explanation of why a player ranks one result over another or why a later best-response calculation should be trusted.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when choices form cycles or depend on irrelevant framing. If a person prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, the model cannot build a coherent choice rule from that ordering.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary language can hide contradictions. People can say they like many things, but a model needs to know whether those comparisons can be used together. A preference relation is the smallest formal object that lets the course ask whether the choices are complete enough and consistent enough to support later payoff reasoning.

Mathematical intuition: The math is a compression of choice behavior. Instead of carrying the full story of each outcome, the preference relation keeps the ordering that matters for the decision. If x is preferred to y, a utility representation can assign x a higher number than y. The number is secondary; the preserved order is the point.

Where it reappears: Preferences feed utility, expected utility, bargaining surplus, bidder values, and welfare comparisons. The same primitive is quiet in later lectures because the course has already installed it as the language of individual motivation.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for the place where an economic model has to say what an actor counts as better before any strategic reasoning begins.

Concept limit

Utility

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to treat utility units as physical units like inches. Utility usually represents order, and only specific setups justify stronger numerical claims.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by treating utility points like dollars or happiness units. Sometimes dollars enter utility, but utility itself is the model’s choice index, not necessarily a literal commodity.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is scale and interpretation. Utility can support payoff comparison inside the model, but it cannot automatically compare happiness across people or survive arbitrary changes when expected-utility structure is required.

What breaks without it: Without utility, later algebra would float free from choice behavior. The course could say one outcome is preferred, but it could not easily compute expected payoffs, best responses, or surplus divisions.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when students compare utility numbers across people or across transformations as if the scale were physical. Many game-theory results need the ordering or expected ranking, not interpersonal happiness units.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because tables and equations cannot operate directly on vague rankings. Utility turns the ranking into a number while preserving the choice information the model needs. It is the handle that lets the course move from “preferred” to “has the larger payoff in this calculation.”

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is that utility preserves the shape of a ranking in a form algebra can manipulate. If higher utility means the player chooses that outcome over another, then best-response calculations can compare numbers instead of verbal preferences. The danger is forgetting which transformations preserve the relevant choice facts.

Where it reappears: Utility becomes the payoff entry in strategic-form games, the continuation value in trees, the expected payoff in Bayesian games, and the private value in auctions. It is the bridge from wanting to calculating.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for a paper turning goals, payoffs, welfare, or preferences into a number that can be compared or optimized.

Concept limit

Expected Utility

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to average dollars automatically. If people care about risk, the utility of money may not rise one-for-one with dollars.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by averaging dollar outcomes when the model needs utility outcomes, or by forgetting that the probabilities are beliefs from the player’s point of view rather than facts known after the uncertainty resolves.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary appears when probabilities or utility over risk are misspecified. Expected utility is a disciplined average, but it does not by itself prove the player has correct beliefs or risk-neutral attitudes.

What breaks without it: Without expected utility, Bayesian games and auctions would have no disciplined way to evaluate actions under unknown types or rival values. The model would either ignore risk or smuggle in an unspecified attitude toward it.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when a high payoff is extremely unlikely or a modest payoff is nearly certain. Probability changes the choice because the decision is over a lottery, not over a single known result.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because uncertainty creates bundles of possible outcomes. A player choosing now cannot optimize separately in each future state; they need one comparison for the whole risky action. Expected utility is that comparison, built by weighting each consequence by the probability assigned before the outcome is known.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is weighted bookkeeping. Each possible payoff counts in proportion to how likely that state is. The expectation symbol is not magic; it is the sum of utility in each possible world multiplied by the probability of that world.

Where it reappears: Expected utility is the arithmetic behind mixed strategies, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, auctions, and belief updating. Whenever the course says a type or action is chosen before hidden information is known, this primitive is working underneath.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for private information, lotteries, risk, or unknown types where the model has to average payoffs across possible worlds.

Concept limit

Strategic Form Games

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to read one row or one column as if the other player were irrelevant. The whole point of the table is joint dependence.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by reading a row as if it has a single value. A row has several consequences, one for each possible opponent action, so a strategy cannot be judged until the contingency is named.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is timing and information. Strategic form is clean for simultaneous or reduced games, but it hides histories and observations unless those are folded into strategies with care.

What breaks without it: Without strategic form, simultaneous games would remain verbal scenarios. Students could argue about incentives, but they would lack the structured object needed to compare rows, columns, and deviations cleanly.

Failed shortcut: The simple story fails when a move sounds good alone but earns a different payoff under another player action. Strategic form forces every action to be evaluated inside an action profile.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because strategic dependence needs a container. If each payoff depends on everyone’s action, the model needs a way to list the whole action profile and the payoff consequences together. Strategic form is the simplest object that keeps those dependencies visible and checkable.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is a function from action profiles to payoffs. The input is everyone’s action, not one action by itself. The output can be different for each player, which is why the same cell can be attractive to one player and unattractive to another.

Where it reappears: Strategic form supports dominance, iterated deletion, best response, Nash, mixed strategies, and zero-sum analysis. Later trees and Bayesian games are extensions of this representation when timing or hidden information must be shown explicitly.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for simultaneous moves, payoff matrices, or models where the exact timing is suppressed so strategic dependence can be seen cleanly.

Concept limit

Extensive Form Games

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to collapse every sequential game into a payoff matrix and lose the credibility problem.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by assuming a tree automatically means everyone observed every earlier move. Information sets matter because the same physical history and the same known history are not always the same thing.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is representational honesty. A tree can only support credible conclusions if its nodes, information sets, and payoffs capture what players actually know and can choose.

What breaks without it: Without extensive form, the course could mistake a sequential conflict for a simultaneous one. That would hide credibility, information sets, and the fact that later choices can make earlier announcements meaningful or empty.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when a threat or promise is evaluated as if it were binding from the start. In a tree, the later decision node asks whether the threat would still be chosen after the history occurs.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because time and information change what an action means. A flat list of actions cannot say who moved first, what was observed, or which histories a player can distinguish. The tree structure preserves those facts so later credibility and belief questions have a place to attach.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is recursive structure. A node summarizes a history, an action selects a branch, and a terminal path gives payoffs. The tree turns time into an object that backward induction and subgame perfection can operate on.

Where it reappears: Extensive form connects directly to backward induction, subgame perfection, bargaining, one-shot deviation, and PBE. It is the representation that lets credibility and belief updating become mathematical rather than rhetorical.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for histories, decision nodes, information sets, or any claim where the order of moves changes what behavior can be believed.

Concept limit

Dominance

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to delete a strategy because it feels unlikely. Dominance is a universal comparison, not a hunch.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by deleting strategies that are merely unattractive under their favorite prediction. That is not dominance. A strategy that is bad against one opponent move but useful against another is belief-dependent, not dominated.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is strength. Dominance is decisive when it applies, but many live strategies are not dominated. Those cases need beliefs, best responses, or equilibrium instead of more aggressive deletion.

What breaks without it: Without dominance, every listed strategy would look equally alive even when some choices have no defensible reason to be played. The analysis would be noisier and less disciplined before equilibrium reasoning even begins.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when a strategy is bad under one opponent action but useful under another. That is not dominance; that is a belief-dependent tradeoff that must be handled by best response or rationalizability.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because some choices can be rejected without forecasting the opponent. The dominance comparison formalizes “no matter what they do” as a set of inequalities across contingencies. That makes deletion a proof about payoffs rather than a guess about likely behavior.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is an inequality repeated across columns, states, or opponent actions. The concept says the comparison does not depend on a forecast. If the inequality holds everywhere, no belief about the opponent can rescue the dominated strategy.

Where it reappears: Dominance leads into iterated deletion and rationalizability, and it also prepares the ground for Nash because dominated strategies cannot be serious best responses. Its limit explains why beliefs later become necessary.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for arguments that rule out actions before equilibrium is computed because one action is never a best answer.

Concept limit

Iterated Deletion

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to ignore whether the deletion uses strict or weak dominance. Weak deletion can depend on order and needs more care.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by treating the order of deletion and the type of dominance as harmless details. With weak dominance especially, those details can change what remains, so the lecture distinction matters.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the deletion rule. Strict deletion has a different force than weak deletion, and common knowledge assumptions matter when interpreting many rounds of pruning. The boundary is why the lecture distinguishes the procedure from equilibrium selection. Surviving deletion means a strategy has not been ruled out by that rationality test; it does not mean the strategy will be played.

What breaks without it: Without iterated deletion, the course would miss how one round of reasoning changes the next. Some games reveal their strategic structure only after obviously dominated actions are removed and the remaining incentives are reconsidered.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when students treat deletion as bookkeeping rather than reasoning about shared rationality. Each round means players understand that earlier bad moves have been ruled out.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because rationality can be layered. Once one impossible action is removed, the remaining problem changes, and new impossibilities can appear. Iterated deletion is the formal procedure that keeps track of that changing action set instead of relying on informal rounds of intuition.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is repeated pruning over a shrinking action set. Each round applies the dominance comparison to the reduced game, so the domain of possible opponent actions changes as the procedure proceeds.

Where it reappears: Iterated deletion connects dominance to rationalizability and common knowledge. It also contrasts with Nash: deletion rules can narrow the game before equilibrium, but they do not always select a single stable prediction.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for step-by-step elimination arguments where rationality of others is used more than once.

Concept limit

Rationalizability

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to treat rationalizable as meaning correct or predicted. It means defensible under some belief, not uniquely expected.

Student trap: Students often get stuck because rationalizability feels permissive. It is permissive on purpose, but not empty: a strategy still has to be a best response to some belief, not just a move someone might randomly choose.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is mutual consistency. A rationalizable strategy may be defensible under some belief, but the beliefs across players may not fit together as an equilibrium.

What breaks without it: Without rationalizability, students may jump too quickly from dominance to Nash. They would miss the middle ground where a strategy survives because a player can hold a belief that makes it a best response.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when the modeler treats their own forecast as the only possible belief. Game theory often needs to distinguish unreasonable actions from reasonable actions under different expectations.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because plausible behavior depends on beliefs. A strategy can be reasonable even when it is not part of a Nash equilibrium, provided some coherent belief makes it optimal. Rationalizability formalizes that support relation between beliefs and best responses.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is support by belief. Instead of asking whether strategies match each other exactly, ask whether there exists a probability distribution over others’ actions that makes the strategy maximize expected payoff.

Where it reappears: Rationalizability shares the best-response primitive with Nash but relaxes mutual consistency. It also previews Bayesian thinking because beliefs become explicit inputs to choice rather than background intuition.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for models that want to separate impossible behavior from behavior that is merely one of several plausible responses.

Concept limit

Best Response

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to ask whether an action is rational without saying against what belief.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by saying an action is rational without saying relative to what. The missing belief or opponent action is usually where the real assumption is hiding.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the condition being held fixed. A best response tells the right action only after the opponent action, belief, type, or history has been named. This is why every later equilibrium check starts locally before making a global claim.

What breaks without it: Without best response, equilibrium would be a name without a test. The course could not check Nash profiles, bidding functions, mixed strategies, or type-contingent plans because each requires a player-level optimality calculation.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when the same action changes status across contingencies. Cutting price, bidding high, or sending a message can be best in one environment and poor in another.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because “good strategy” is incomplete without a condition. The best-response set stores the actions that maximize payoff given a named opponent action or belief. It is the reusable local object behind equilibrium, auctions, and dynamic deviation checks.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is argmax: search over the player’s available actions and keep the action or actions with the largest payoff under the specified condition. The condition is part of the object, not background prose.

Where it reappears: Best response connects to Nash, mixed strategies, minimax, Bayesian Nash, PBE, and mechanism design. Each later solution concept changes what is held fixed, but the conditional choice question remains.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for arrows, reaction functions, or optimization steps that start with given the other player does X.

Concept limit

Nash Equilibrium

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to hear equilibrium and think socially best. Nash can describe bad outcomes, including prisoner's dilemma, because it is about unilateral incentives, not collective welfare.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by importing the everyday meaning of equilibrium as balance or goodness. Nash equilibrium can be inefficient or ugly. Its claim is narrower: no single player wants to move alone.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is one-person deviation from a fixed profile. Nash does not handle noncredible threats, belief updating, coalitional deviations, or welfare without extra structure.

What breaks without it: Without Nash equilibrium, payoff tables would produce stories but not disciplined predictions. Any outcome could be proposed even if one participant has an obvious profitable deviation once everyone else behavior is named.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when the analyst checks only total payoff or social appeal. A collectively attractive outcome can still fail if one player can improve by deviating alone.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because strategic prediction is circular. Each player’s best action depends on the others’ actions, so the model needs a profile where all those conditional choices fit together. Nash equilibrium is the formal closure of the best-response loop.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is mutual best response. For every player i, the chosen action a_i must sit inside BR_i(a_-i). The notation says no one can improve by changing only their own component while the rest of the profile is held still.

Where it reappears: Nash connects backward to best response and forward to mixed strategies, subgame perfection, Bayesian Nash, and PBE. Each refinement keeps the same no-profitable-deviation core while repairing a missing feature such as time or private information.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for claims that a behavior pattern persists because no individual participant can gain by deviating alone.

Concept limit

Mixed Strategies

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to think mixed strategy means confusion. In the model, randomization can be deliberate and exactly calculated.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by asking what the player “really” chooses. The answer is that the random device is part of the strategy; after it realizes an action, the distribution was still the planned commitment.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is interpretation and commitment. A mixed strategy is useful when randomization is credible and payoff-relevant, but not every observed frequency is an equilibrium mixture. The boundary is that the model predicts a distribution, not necessarily a visible sequence that looks random in a small sample. Interpreting observed play requires care about commitment, learning, and repeated sampling.

What breaks without it: Without mixed strategies, the course would falsely conclude that some games have no stable prediction simply because no pure action profile works. It would also miss why randomness can be chosen as discipline rather than noise.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails in games like matching pennies, where any predictable pure move gives the opponent a profitable response. Stability requires making the opponent unable to improve by targeting a pattern.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because pure actions cannot always carry stable predictions. A probability distribution over actions lets the player control predictability itself. The distribution becomes the strategy because it determines opponents’ expected payoffs before the random action is realized.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is expected payoff under probabilities. If the opponent mixes, each of my pure actions has an expected payoff. In equilibrium, actions used with positive probability must usually earn the same expected payoff, otherwise I would shift probability toward the better one.

Where it reappears: Mixed strategies connect Nash to zero-sum games, minimax, and expected utility. The player is not choosing one payoff; they are choosing a distribution whose expected consequences shape the opponent response.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for indifference equations, randomized play, or models where predictable behavior would be punished.

Concept limit

Zero-Sum Games

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to apply zero-sum intuition to bargaining or market games where joint gains are possible.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by calling any rivalry zero-sum. Many competitive economic settings still have joint gains, inefficiencies, or possibilities for collusion. Zero-sum is a payoff structure, not a mood.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is exact payoff opposition. If players can both gain, both lose, bargain, collude, or create surplus, zero-sum tools can distort the strategic picture. The boundary helps students avoid overusing adversarial language. A market competitor can hurt you and still not be in a zero-sum game if pricing, entry, innovation, or collusion changes total surplus.

What breaks without it: Without the zero-sum distinction, students may apply adversarial security logic to games where cooperation or joint surplus exists. The course needs this special case precisely because its conclusions are sharper than in general games.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when rivalry coexists with mutual gains from agreement. Pricing, bargaining, and auctions can be competitive without being literally zero-sum.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because exact conflict has special structure. When one player’s payoff is the negative of the other’s, the model can collapse two objectives into one value seen from opposite sides. That structure justifies security and minimax tools that would be too strong in general games.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is payoff conservation. If player 1 gets u, player 2 gets -u. That structure means maximizing one side’s payoff is equivalent to minimizing the other side’s payoff, which is why max and min operations meet.

Where it reappears: Zero-sum games connect mixed strategies to minimax. They also mark a boundary with bargaining, repeated cooperation, and mechanism design, where the central issue is not only defeating the other side.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for adversarial models where maximizing my payoff is exactly minimizing yours.

Concept limit

Minimax

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to use minimax as a general decision rule in games that are not zero-sum or not adversarial in that strict way.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by confusing minimax with pessimism. It is not a personality trait; it is the rational response to a modeled opponent whose payoff is opposed to yours.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is adversarial structure. Minimax is appropriate when the opponent is modeled as choosing to hurt your payoff; it can be too conservative in cooperative or incomplete-information settings. The boundary also explains why minimax is attractive in some technical fields but not a universal behavioral theory. It gives a guarantee under hostile response; it does not describe trust, learning, bargaining, or mutually beneficial adjustment.

What breaks without it: Without minimax, the course could describe conflict but not the logic of protecting against exploitation. It would miss why randomization and worst-case evaluation can produce a stable value in zero-sum games.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when the high-upside action has a terrible weakness. In a hostile setting, the opponent is not sampling randomly from your hopes; they are choosing against you.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because adversarial choice requires a guarantee. The player needs to compare strategies by what the opponent can force against them. Minimax turns that defensive question into a nested optimization: protect the worst case, then choose the best protection.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is nested comparison. For each strategy, compute the worst payoff the opponent can force. Then choose the strategy whose worst case is largest. In zero-sum equilibrium, this security level lines up with the game’s value.

Where it reappears: Minimax connects to zero-sum value, mixed strategies, and best response. It also foreshadows robustness ideas outside game theory, but the course version is grounded in exact payoff opposition.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for worst-case guarantees, security levels, or saddle-point reasoning.

Concept limit

Backward Induction

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to reason from the start while treating later threats as fixed facts.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by reading the tree chronologically. Players live forward, but the analyst solves backward because later incentives determine which earlier expectations are credible.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the tree. Backward induction is only as good as the modeled histories, payoffs, and information. Hidden information or imperfect observation requires richer tools. The boundary becomes visible when real players have limited foresight or when the tree is not common knowledge. The course model assumes the relevant future choices are understood well enough to be folded backward.

What breaks without it: Without backward induction, a game tree would be a picture of possible histories but not a method for evaluating them. The model could accept plans that later players would abandon once their decision node is reached.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when a current action depends on a future threat. If the future threat is not optimal later, it should not influence the earlier player in the predicted way.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because sequential choice has nested future problems inside current decisions. A continuation value lets the solved future replace a whole subtree. Without that object, the analyst would have to carry every possible future story instead of using the later incentive calculation.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is recursion over continuation values. A node’s value is determined by the best value available at the next nodes for the player moving there. The game is solved by replacing future subtrees with their strategic value.

Where it reappears: Backward induction connects extensive form to subgame perfection, bargaining, finite repetition, and one-shot deviation. It is the recursion primitive behind the course treatment of time.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for finite sequential games where the last move can be solved and folded back into earlier choices.

Concept limit

Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to think any Nash equilibrium in a dynamic game is credible.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by ignoring histories that do not occur in equilibrium. Those histories are exactly where empty threats hide, so the course makes students inspect them.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is subgames. Some dynamic information problems do not have proper subgames at the histories where beliefs matter, which is why PBE enters later. This boundary is why the course later needs belief-based refinements. Some histories are inside clean subgames, but hidden-information histories can require the analyst to specify what players believe before optimal continuation play can even be evaluated. The refinement is small in wording but large in what behavior it rules out.

What breaks without it: Without subgame perfection, dynamic models can rest on noncredible threats. A strategy profile may pass the initial Nash test only because it prescribes off-path behavior that nobody would actually choose later.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails in entry-deterrence and bargaining-style examples where a player threatens costly punishment. If punishment is irrational after entry, the threat cannot discipline entry before it happens.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because a dynamic strategy includes plans for histories that may not occur. Those plans can support threats, so they must be checked. Subgame perfection formalizes the demand that continuation behavior is itself equilibrium behavior wherever a real subgame begins.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is equilibrium at every credible starting point. A subgame is a piece of the original tree that can be considered as a game on its own. The strategy profile must induce Nash behavior in each such piece.

Where it reappears: Subgame perfection connects Nash to backward induction and one-shot deviation. PBE later extends the same credibility demand when hidden information means beliefs must be specified too.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for dynamic games, off-path histories, or threats where the key question is whether the promised future action would really be optimal.

Concept limit

One-Shot Deviation Principle

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to use the shortcut without checking the assumptions that make local deviations enough.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by treating the principle as universally available. It depends on the structure of the dynamic game, so the shortcut must travel with its assumptions.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the theorem conditions. The shortcut cannot be used blindly in every dynamic game; the structure must justify local checks standing in for global deviations. The boundary is also pedagogical: the principle is a theorem that saves work, not a license to ignore dynamic incentives. Students still need to know what histories and one-step deviations must be checked.

What breaks without it: Without this principle, verifying repeated or dynamic equilibria can become intractable. Students may either skip deviation checks or drown in too many possible alternative histories.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when a long deviation looks mysterious but is really built from local profitable steps. The principle works only under the right dynamic conditions, so the shortcut itself has assumptions.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because dynamic strategies are too large to compare naively. A local deviation test gives the course a finite, teachable way to verify optimality under the right assumptions. It turns the huge space of possible strategy changes into a sequence of one-history checks.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is local optimality implying global optimality in the relevant dynamic environment. If no single stitch can be profitably pulled out, then a longer deviation cannot be assembled profitably from such local changes.

Where it reappears: One-shot deviation connects subgame perfection to repeated games and trigger strategies. It uses the same credibility primitive as backward induction but applies it to richer strategy plans.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for dynamic incentive proofs that replace many possible deviations with a one-period deviation inequality.

Concept limit

Bargaining

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to treat bargaining power as a vague personality trait when the model usually locates it in patience, outside options, timing, or information.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by assuming the fair split is the predicted split. Game theory asks what division survives incentives, not what division sounds fair from outside the model.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the source of bargaining power. If outside options, patience, information, or commitment are modeled wrongly, the predicted split can look precise while resting on fragile inputs. The boundary matters in applications because observed delay can come from strategic waiting, private information, transaction costs, or changing outside options. The bargaining model clarifies one mechanism, but the analyst still has to identify which pressure is active.

What breaks without it: Without bargaining theory, the course could say agreement is efficient but not explain who gets what or why delay occurs. It would miss how threat points and waiting costs shape the split.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when equal division ignores asymmetric patience or outside options. A player who can wait longer, or who has a better fallback, can change the strategic pressure of negotiation.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because surplus division depends on strategic alternatives, not only on the size of the surplus. Offers, rejection payoffs, delay costs, and outside options must be represented together. Bargaining models create that object so division can be explained by incentives rather than asserted fairness.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is payoff mapping plus continuation value. Each offer determines a division if accepted and a future subgame if rejected. The value of waiting or walking away sets the pressure on today’s demand.

Where it reappears: Bargaining connects payoff mapping to backward induction, discounting, and private information. It also links to mechanisms because rules and outside options shape what agreements are incentive-compatible.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for surplus division, alternating offers, take-it-or-leave-it offers, or incomplete information about values.

Concept limit

Finitely Repeated Games

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to assume repetition automatically creates cooperation. A known last round can destroy that logic.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by counting repetitions rather than asking whether the relationship has a strategic future at each date. Ten known rounds and an ongoing relationship are different objects.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the known endpoint. The finite repeated-game unraveling logic changes when the horizon is uncertain, reputational types exist, or players cannot observe deviations. The classroom value of the finite case is that it blocks a lazy intuition about repetition. More rounds do not automatically mean more cooperation; what matters is whether each round still has a future consequence that can discipline current behavior. That backward pressure is the lecture’s main warning about finite horizons.

What breaks without it: Without finite repetition, students might overgeneralize from ongoing relationships and assume history always disciplines behavior. The course needs the finite case to show why the endpoint is strategically powerful.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when the last round has the one-shot equilibrium, making the next-to-last round lose its punishment threat, and so on backward through the game.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because a known horizon changes every period’s incentives. The model needs to preserve the number of remaining rounds so last-period logic can be pulled backward. A finite repeated game is not just many one-shot games; it is a time-indexed strategic object.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is backward induction applied across periods. The terminal period anchors the continuation value, and earlier periods inherit that value. A fixed horizon can therefore turn future discipline into an empty promise near the end.

Where it reappears: Finite repetition connects backward induction to infinite repetition. It is the contrast case that makes discounting and no-known-final-round assumptions meaningful later.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for known horizons and unraveling arguments that start at the final period.

Concept limit

Infinitely Repeated Games

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to say infinite means literally forever. The model often means no known last round or a continuing relationship with future value.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by thinking infinite repetition guarantees cooperation. If players are impatient, cannot observe deviations, or cannot punish credibly, the cooperative path may fail.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is enforceability of the future. Infinite repetition does not help if punishment cannot be observed, is not credible, or is discounted too heavily. The lecture use is therefore conditional: first identify the continuation value, then ask whether the proposed punishment can actually be carried out. If either piece is missing, the phrase repeated relationship is not enough to support cooperation. The lecture also uses this topic to separate mathematical infinity from economic continuation. What matters for incentives is not a calendar that literally never ends, but the absence of a commonly known final moment at which punishment loses force.

What breaks without it: Without infinite repetition, the course would lack the clean model where ongoing relationships sustain behavior that fails in a one-shot interaction. It would also miss why patience is a strategic parameter.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when players are impatient or punishment is weak. Repetition helps only if the future is valuable enough and deviations can be answered credibly.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because ongoing relationships make future consequences part of current payoff. The discount factor and continuation payoff let the course compare today’s temptation with tomorrow’s punishment. Without that object, “the future matters” would remain a slogan rather than an inequality.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is a present value comparison. Future payoffs are weighted by powers of the discount factor. A trigger strategy works only when the discounted punishment loss is large enough to offset the immediate deviation gain.

Where it reappears: Infinite repetition connects finite horizons, folk theorem logic, implicit cartels, discounting, and subgame perfection. The same credibility question reappears, now stretched over an ongoing relationship.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for trigger strategies, continuation values, and inequalities comparing temptation today to punishment tomorrow.

Concept limit

Folk Theorem

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to read the folk theorem as predicting one outcome. It often says many outcomes are possible, which is powerful but also less predictive.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by reading the folk theorem as “cooperation happens.” The deeper message is multiplicity: many outcomes can happen, so institutions and history may be needed to select one.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is prediction. The folk theorem shows many payoffs can be sustained; it does not by itself select which one will occur in a specific institution. The boundary is selection. The theorem tells the course that many outcomes can be made stable, but a real application still needs institutions, norms, history, or communication to explain why one equilibrium rather than another is chosen.

What breaks without it: Without the folk theorem, students may think repeated games merely select one cooperative equilibrium. The theorem shows a much larger set of feasible, individually rational outcomes supported by punishment.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when it ignores individual rationality or feasible payoffs. Punishment can support only outcomes players prefer to their punishment value and that the stage game can actually generate.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because repeated games can support sets of payoffs rather than one obvious outcome. Feasibility, individual rationality, and patience define which payoff vectors can be sustained. The theorem needs that set-based object to explain both possibility and multiplicity.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is incentive compatibility over continuation payoffs. A proposed payoff vector must give each player enough value that deviation plus punishment is worse than staying on the path. The theorem describes a set, not a single prediction.

Where it reappears: The folk theorem connects infinite repetition, discounting, punishment, and cartel applications. It also motivates institutional detail because equilibrium multiplicity often needs context to choose among possible outcomes.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for repeated-game papers showing a large set of sustainable payoffs under patience and punishment.

Concept limit

Implicit Cartels

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to assume high prices prove explicit agreement. Repeated-game incentives can sometimes generate similar outcomes without a formal contract.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by equating collusion with communication. The model can produce tacit collusion if prices, market shares, or other signals let firms detect and punish deviations.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is monitoring and legality. Tacit collusion requires that firms can detect deviations and respond; market noise, entry, and antitrust constraints can break the mechanism. In lecture terms, the important boundary is that the model is explaining an incentive mechanism, not proving that firms actually coordinated in any given market. Evidence about prices, demand shocks, communication, and monitoring would be needed before applying the concept outside the classroom example. The concept also depends on market observability. If firms cannot tell whether a low price came from cheating, demand noise, cost shocks, or a new entrant, punishment may be too blunt or too costly to sustain the cartel path.

What breaks without it: Without the cartel application, repeated-game ideas could feel abstract. The course would miss how continuation values, monitoring, and punishment shape real pricing incentives and antitrust concerns.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when it looks only at the one-period gain from cutting price. The repeated-game calculation also includes the price war or punishment that may follow.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because tacit collusion depends on a comparison across time. The model must represent the current profit from cheating and the discounted loss from future punishment. That object turns cartel stability into an incentive constraint instead of a story about firms being friendly.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is the same deviation inequality from repeated games, translated into profit. The gain from undercutting today is compared with the discounted loss from future price competition.

Where it reappears: Implicit cartels connect infinite repetition, folk theorem logic, incentive compatibility, and market design. They also show where strategic cooperation can be socially harmful rather than beneficial.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for industries with repeated price interaction, observability of deviations, and credible punishment paths.

Concept limit

Imperfect Competition

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to treat imperfect competition as just monopoly with more firms. The whole point is that each firm has some power but not complete power. The strategic object is the interaction among firms, not only the demand curve facing one seller.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by mixing up the market story with the strategic object. The story may be coffee shops, patents, or internet providers, but the object is the payoff map from firm choices to profits.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is model specification. Cournot and Bertrand are not universal laws of competition; they are different ways to represent what firms choose. The lecture itself flags this by contrasting quantity choice and price choice. In real markets, capacity constraints, product differentiation, search frictions, contracts, and regulation can change which representation fits. The concept is strongest when the analyst can defend why price, quantity, entry, or another action is the strategic variable.

What breaks without it: Without this concept, the course could not explain why a firm may charge above cost, why price competition can be brutal, or why changing the modeling choice from quantity to price can change the prediction. Market behavior would be described as if firms either had no power or total power.

Failed shortcut: The simple monopoly approach fails when rivals can respond. A price that looks profitable in isolation may lose the market to an undercutting rival, while a quantity that looks safe may lower the price once another firm produces too.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because words like competition and market power are too loose. The model must say what firms choose, how choices determine price or demand, and how profit changes when rivals act. Best responses make the strategic dependence explicit.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is strategic profit maximization. Profit is not just revenue minus cost; in these models, revenue depends on the market price, and the market price depends on the total choices of firms. A best-response curve records the quantity or price that maximizes one firm's profit for each rival action.

Where it reappears: Imperfect competition connects strategic-form games, best response, Nash equilibrium, implicit cartels, and one-shot deviation. The Cournot and Bertrand contrast also previews why model representation matters: changing the action from quantity to price can change the strategic prediction.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for a model where firms choose prices, quantities, entry, capacity, or product features while knowing that rivals' actions change the payoff. If the paper asks for a best response by a firm rather than a single optimal monopoly price, this concept is active.

Concept limit

Bayesian Games

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to treat hidden information as random noise outside the game. In Bayesian games, the uncertainty is part of the strategic structure.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by treating uncertainty as noise outside the model. In a Bayesian game, uncertainty is inside the model and has named objects: types, beliefs, strategies, and payoffs.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the type model. If the type space, priors, or payoff dependence are wrong, Bayesian equilibrium can be internally consistent but externally misleading. The lecture boundary also explains why the type space is a modeling choice. Too few types can erase important private information; too many can make the model flexible without adding insight. The useful type structure is the one that carries the strategic uncertainty actually driving behavior. The model’s usefulness depends on naming that hidden structure carefully.

What breaks without it: Without Bayesian games, the course would treat hidden values, costs, and motives as if everyone knew them. Auctions, signaling, and incomplete-information bargaining would collapse into misleading complete-information models.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when one action is optimal against one type but not another. The player is choosing against a probability distribution, not a single known opponent.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because hidden information changes the identity of the decision problem. Types and priors let the model represent what each player knows privately and what others believe. Without them, private values and costs would be invisible to the equilibrium calculation.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is expected utility over types. A player chooses an action or plan while averaging over the possible hidden states they do not observe. Priors define the probability weights before actions reveal anything.

Where it reappears: Bayesian games connect expected utility to Bayesian Nash, auctions, signaling, and PBE. They are the private-information foundation for the final third of the course.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for models with types, priors, private values, costs, signals, or asymmetric information.

Concept limit

Types And Beliefs

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to blur type and action. A type is hidden information; an action is a choice that may reveal or conceal it.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by saying “the player believes the type” as if belief and type were the same thing. One is the hidden state; the other is someone’s uncertain view of it.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is discipline on beliefs. Beliefs are necessary, but they should come from priors, signals, observations, or equilibrium restrictions rather than being chosen to rescue any story. The lecture boundary is that beliefs should be inspectable. A model that can support any action by inventing a convenient belief has stopped explaining. The belief structure must connect to priors, information, or equilibrium restrictions.

What breaks without it: Without types and beliefs, private-information models would not say who knows what or how uncertainty enters payoffs. The analyst could not distinguish a high-value bidder from a low-value bidder or a receiver belief from sender truth.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when beliefs are treated as facts. A receiver may believe a sender is high type, but that belief must be updated and tested against incentives.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because uncertainty has two sides: the hidden state and someone’s probability over it. Types represent the state; beliefs represent the uncertainty. Keeping them separate lets the course analyze signaling, updating, and type-contingent strategy without confusing truth with perception.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is a state space with probability weights. The type indexes which payoff function or information situation is true; the belief says how likely each index seems to another player. Updating changes the weights after observations.

Where it reappears: Types and beliefs connect Bayesian games, Bayesian Nash, auctions, signaling, cheap talk, and PBE. They are also the reason expected utility keeps reappearing in private-information settings.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for any model where behavior depends on values, costs, quality, or information that only one side observes.

Concept limit

Bayesian Nash Equilibrium

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to look for one action per player. In Bayesian Nash, a strategy is a rule mapping types to actions.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by checking only the average player. Bayesian Nash is stricter: a proposed strategy can fail because one particular type wants to deviate, even if the pooled behavior looks reasonable.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is timing and updating. Bayesian Nash handles static type uncertainty, but dynamic signals and histories require belief systems such as PBE. The boundary is why Bayesian Nash is not the final word for signaling games. It handles plans by type before dynamic observations, but once actions reveal information over time, equilibrium must also discipline posterior beliefs. Each type receives its own incentive audit, not just the player as a whole.

What breaks without it: Without Bayesian Nash, the course could define types and beliefs but not check whether type-contingent strategies hold together. A proposed bidding or action rule might fail for one hidden type even if it sounds plausible overall.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when pooling all types hides profitable deviations. A high-value bidder and a low-value bidder may face different incentives even under the same auction rule.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because hidden types need their own stability checks. A single action per player is not enough; each possible type needs a plan, and each plan must be optimal in expectation. Bayesian Nash equilibrium formalizes mutual consistency across those type-indexed plans.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is mutual consistency across type-contingent plans. For each type, hold fixed the strategy rules of others and the belief distribution over their types, then maximize expected payoff. Every type must be content with its assigned action.

Where it reappears: Bayesian Nash connects Nash equilibrium to expected utility, types, beliefs, and auctions. PBE later adds timing and belief updating after observed histories.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for type-contingent strategies and expected-payoff best response conditions.

Concept limit

Screening And Adverse Selection

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to treat demand as only fewer people buying when price rises. In adverse selection, the identity of the buyers changes too, and that composition change can matter more than the number of buyers.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by treating hidden information as just another probability. The lecture point is sharper: the action or price changes which hidden types remain in the game, so the probability distribution after participation is not the original distribution.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the source of selection. Not every failed trade is adverse selection; the mechanism requires hidden type information and terms that select different types differently. The evidence supports the screening/adverse-selection logic in the lecture's buyer-seller and insurance examples. Broader claims about real insurance or legal bargaining require evidence about actual information, costs, offers, and participation.

What breaks without it: Without this concept, the course would predict trade whenever buyer value exceeds seller cost. It would miss the reason some trades fail: the terms that attract one type may drive away another or reveal information that changes future bargaining.

Failed shortcut: The complete-information bargaining model fails because offers can carry information and because acceptance is selective. Once an offer filters types, the uninformed side is no longer facing the original population.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary average reasoning lies in selected populations. A cutoff, participation condition, or posterior distribution is needed to say who accepts, who rejects, and what the uninformed side should believe after observing that choice.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is conditional composition. Start with a population of possible types. A rule selects a subset of those types. The payoff calculation must use the selected subset, because the rule itself changed who participates.

Where it reappears: Screening and adverse selection connect Bayesian games, types and beliefs, bargaining, signaling, auctions, and PBE. Signaling asks what a privately informed first mover reveals through action; screening asks how an uninformed first mover designs a rule that makes hidden types reveal themselves by accepting or rejecting.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for models where an offer, price, contract, or test makes different hidden types sort themselves. If changing the rule changes the pool of participants, the screening/adverse-selection primitive is active.

Concept limit

Auctions

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to think the highest-value bidder automatically bids their value in every auction format.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by treating bids as direct value reports. In many auctions, bids are strategic actions distorted by payment rules, risk, competition, and beliefs about rivals.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is assumptions about values and bidders. Risk attitudes, budgets, common values, entry, collusion, and correlated information can all change bidding logic. The lecture boundary is especially important because auction predictions are assumption-sensitive. Changing the value environment, bidder risk, reserve price, or information structure can change whether truthful bidding, bid shading, or revenue comparisons survive. The auction rule and the bidder’s information must therefore be read together.

What breaks without it: Without auction theory, the course would miss one of the clearest economic applications of incomplete-information games. It could not explain bid shading, truthful bidding in special formats, or why payment rules change behavior.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails in first-price auctions, where bidding full value leaves no surplus if the bidder wins. Strategic bidding balances winning probability against the price paid.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because auction outcomes are created by rules. Values, bids, allocation, and payments have to be represented together so the course can ask how a bidder trades off winning more often against paying more when they win.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is expected payoff from bidding. A bid affects the probability of winning and the payment if winning occurs. The bidder chooses the bid that balances those two forces against beliefs about rival bids or values.

Where it reappears: Auctions connect Bayesian games, expected utility, incentive compatibility, and revenue equivalence. Ad auctions extend the same logic when platforms allocate ranked positions rather than a single object.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for allocation and payment rules that transform private values into strategic bids.

Concept limit

Revenue Equivalence

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to quote revenue equivalence without the assumptions. Change risk, affiliation, reserve prices, or asymmetry and the result can fail.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by comparing the rules while holding bids fixed. Equilibrium bids change with the auction format, and those strategic adjustments are exactly what the theorem accounts for.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is the theorem assumptions. Once values are affiliated, bidders are risk averse, reserve prices change, or allocation rules differ, equivalence may fail. The teaching payoff is that failure is informative. When revenue equivalence breaks, the reason usually points to a real economic force such as risk aversion, common values, entry, asymmetry, or a changed allocation rule. The theorem is a map of assumptions as much as a revenue result.

What breaks without it: Without revenue equivalence, every auction comparison would be treated as an isolated case. The course would miss the deeper structure that makes some formats equivalent and the assumptions that make equivalence fragile.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when it compares rules while holding bids fixed. Bidders change their bids when the rule changes, so revenue comparison must include the equilibrium response.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because comparing auction formats requires more than comparing labels. Expected revenue depends on allocation probabilities, payment rules, and equilibrium bidding. Revenue equivalence formalizes the conditions under which those pieces line up across mechanisms.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is that expected payment can be pinned down by allocation rules plus boundary conditions under the standard assumptions. If the same type has the same chance of winning across mechanisms, expected revenue can match even when payment timing differs.

Where it reappears: Revenue equivalence connects auctions, Bayesian Nash, expected payments, and incentive compatibility. It also prepares students to read mechanism papers by checking distributional and valuation assumptions before trusting a revenue claim.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for auction comparisons where allocation sameness and bidder payoff normalization drive payment conclusions.

Concept limit

Ad Auctions

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to treat ad auctions as ordinary one-object auctions. Position, click probability, and platform quality adjustments change the strategic object.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by seeing only bids. In sponsored search, the highest bid may not create the highest platform value if relevance and click probability differ.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is platform feedback. Click predictions, quality scores, user behavior, and repeated advertiser learning can make the simple auction model only a first approximation. The lecture boundary is that real platforms update predictions and rules over time. A static ad-auction model can explain the core incentive tradeoff, but platform learning and advertiser experimentation can add dynamics that the basic mechanism does not fully capture. The platform’s ranking formula becomes part of the strategic environment.

What breaks without it: Without ad auctions, the course would leave mechanism design at textbook allocation. It would miss how online markets mix bidding strategy with measurement, ranking rules, and platform incentives.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when a high bid on a low-quality ad produces fewer useful clicks than a lower bid on a better match. The platform is pricing attention, not only selling rank.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because platform auctions allocate attention through rankings, clicks, and quality adjustments. A simple highest-bid object misses the product being sold. The ad-auction model keeps bid, position, click probability, and value in the same incentive calculation.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is an allocation rule over ranked slots plus a payment rule per click or impression. A bidder’s payoff depends on position, click probability, value per click, price, and sometimes quality adjustments.

Where it reappears: Ad auctions connect standard auctions to mechanism design, payoff mapping, Bayesian reasoning, and repeated platform participation. They also show why real institutions often add engineering details to clean theory.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for markets with ranked slots, click probabilities, quality scores, and generalized second-price style rules.

Concept limit

Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to focus only on strategies and forget beliefs. In hidden-information dynamic games, beliefs are part of the equilibrium object.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by solving the strategy profile and treating beliefs as commentary. In PBE, beliefs are part of the equilibrium data, especially because off-path beliefs can affect credibility.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is off-path freedom. PBE disciplines beliefs where Bayes rule applies, but unexpected histories can still leave multiple belief specifications and multiple equilibria. The practical reading rule is to inspect both halves of the candidate equilibrium. If the strategies look sensible but the beliefs are arbitrary, or the beliefs look plausible but the continuation actions are not optimal, the PBE claim has not been earned. Belief discipline is therefore not optional decoration. The lecture uses that discipline to keep inference and sequential choice tied together.

What breaks without it: Without PBE, signaling and other dynamic asymmetric-information models could prescribe actions without explaining the beliefs that make those actions rational. Credibility would be separated from learning.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when an off-path event occurs. Bayes rule may not determine beliefs there, but the model still has to specify beliefs carefully because they affect continuation behavior.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because dynamic private-information games require beliefs and actions to discipline each other. Strategies say what players do; beliefs say what they infer after histories. PBE binds the two so credibility and updating are checked together.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is a coupled object. Beliefs determine optimal continuation actions, while strategies determine which observations are likely and therefore how beliefs should update. Neither side can be checked in isolation.

Where it reappears: PBE connects extensive form, Bayesian games, types and beliefs, signaling, and subgame perfection. It is where the course asks credibility and inference to pass the same test.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for dynamic asymmetric-information models with off-path beliefs, updating, and sequential rationality.

Concept limit

Signaling

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to assume any costly action is a signal. It signals only if costs and incentives differ across types in a way receivers can reason from.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by assuming costly means informative. A costly signal is informative only if the cost or benefit differs across types in the right direction.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is mimicry. A signal separates types only when the wrong type does not want to imitate; if imitation is cheap enough, pooling can destroy information. The boundary also forces an empirical question. If the real-world signal is education, certification, price, or warranty, the analyst still has to ask whether costs really differ by type in the direction the model requires. The signal must survive the imitation test. Receiver beliefs and sender incentives must therefore be solved as one system, not separately.

What breaks without it: Without signaling, the course could not explain education, warranties, investment, or other actions whose value partly comes from what observers infer. It would treat all messages as direct information rather than incentive-shaped evidence.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when the signal is equally cheap for all types. If every type would send it, the receiver should not infer separation from observing it.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because observers infer hidden type from costly or informative action. A signaling model must represent sender types, signal costs, receiver beliefs, and receiver responses together. Otherwise the analyst cannot check whether imitation destroys the message.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is incentive compatibility across types plus belief updating. For separation, each type must prefer its assigned signal over mimicking another type. The receiver’s posterior belief then justifies the response to each signal.

Where it reappears: Signaling connects Bayesian games, PBE, beliefs, incentive compatibility, and cheap talk. It is the costly-action counterpart to costless messages and the dynamic counterpart to static hidden types.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for actions that are valuable mainly because observers infer hidden quality, commitment, or type from them.

Concept limit

Cheap Talk

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to say cheap means irrelevant. Costless speech can matter when incentives make truthful communication self-enforcing.

Student trap: Students often get stuck by saying cheap talk is either always useless or always informative. The lecture point is conditional: credibility depends on payoff alignment and receiver response.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is incentive alignment. Costless communication can transmit information only when the sender prefers a receiver response close enough to the truth. This is why cheap talk is a useful boundary case after signaling. It asks how much information can be transmitted when the usual separating device, costly action, has been removed. The answer depends on preferences, not on the beauty of the message. Payoff alignment is the hidden engine. The receiver listens only when the sender’s strategic interest makes listening defensible.

What breaks without it: Without cheap talk, the course would treat all communication as either irrelevant noise or costly signaling. It would miss the middle case where aligned interests make some costless messages credible enough to affect actions.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when preferences are partially aligned. In those cases, a message can partition information usefully even though it carries no direct cost.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because costless words have no automatic credibility. The model needs sender preferences, receiver actions, states, and message rules so it can ask whether truthful or partially truthful communication is self-enforcing.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is partitioning information through equilibrium messages. A message can group states into regions where truthful communication is self-enforcing because the sender prefers the receiver response induced by that message.

Where it reappears: Cheap talk connects signaling, Bayesian games, beliefs, and common knowledge. It also draws a boundary: if incentives are opposed, speech may pool; if sufficiently aligned, speech can guide receiver action.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for models where messages are nonbinding and the key question is whether incentives allow information transmission.

Concept limit

Common Knowledge

Common misunderstanding: The mistake is to treat true, known, and common knowledge as the same. They are different levels of shared awareness.

Student trap: Students often get stuck because the infinite chain sounds philosophical. The course uses it for concrete coordination: people act differently when a fact is publicly shared than when each person only privately receives it.

Where the idea stops working: The boundary is public assurance. A fact can be true and widely known without becoming common knowledge if players doubt whether others received or understood it. The boundary is that common knowledge is stronger than public posting in the ordinary sense. If people miss the announcement, doubt its authenticity, or doubt whether others saw it, the recursive chain can fail.

What breaks without it: Without common knowledge, the course would miss higher-order uncertainty. Players might all know the same event, yet hesitate because they do not know whether others know, or whether others know that they know.

Failed shortcut: The simple approach fails when private notifications replace public announcements. Each person may have the fact, but not the assurance that others have the same fact and understand that it is shared.

Why the formal idea has to exist: The mathematical object has to exist because coordination can depend on levels of knowledge, not only on the fact itself. Recursive knowledge operators let the course distinguish private knowledge from public assurance. That distinction explains why announcements can move behavior when private messages cannot.

Mathematical intuition: The mathematical intuition is recursive knowledge. Start with an event E. Apply the “everyone knows” operator repeatedly: everyone knows E, everyone knows that everyone knows E, and so on without a finite stopping point.

Where it reappears: Common knowledge connects iterated deletion, rationalizability, equilibrium selection, cheap talk, and public communication. It is the knowledge-side analogue of mutual consistency: everyone reasoning must line up at many levels.

How to recognize it elsewhere: Look for coordination failures, public announcements, or arguments that depend on what everyone knows about everyone else's knowledge.

Theme-Level Boundaries

Theme limit

Choice Under Tradeoffs

Where the analogy breaks: The analogy breaks if the utility number is read as a psychological meter. In these lectures, utility is a representation of choice behavior. Changing the units can leave ordinal choice unchanged, while expected-utility comparisons need stronger structure because lotteries require meaningful tradeoffs across uncertain outcomes.

Theme limit

Strategic Dependence

Where the analogy breaks: A table can hide timing and information, while a tree can become misleading if the information sets or payoffs are wrong. Dominance is also fragile: strict dominance is a strong deletion rule, but weak dominance and belief-based arguments require more care.

Theme limit

Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency

Where the analogy breaks: Equilibrium does not mean efficient, ethical, or likely in a behavioral sense. It also changes shape when time, private information, or beliefs enter; that is why later lectures need subgame-perfect, Bayesian Nash, and perfect Bayesian equilibrium rather than one universal test.

Theme limit

Time, Threats, And Credibility

Where the analogy breaks: Backward logic can overstate precision if the tree omits uncertainty, private information, renegotiation, or limited foresight. A threat that is credible in one subgame may fail once beliefs or outside options change.

Theme limit

Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Where the analogy breaks: The repeated-game analogy breaks when monitoring is poor, players are too impatient, punishment is not credible, entry changes the player set, or the horizon is known to end. Future discipline is a mechanism with conditions, not a guarantee of cooperation.

Theme limit

Private Information And Beliefs

Where the analogy breaks: Beliefs are not free excuses. Off-path beliefs, impossible observations, and strategic signaling can make the model delicate. Treating any arbitrary belief as equally disciplined can make a private-information model explain too much.

Theme limit

Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Where the analogy breaks: Auction results can collapse when assumptions change: correlated values, risk aversion, budgets, entry costs, reserve prices, quality scores, or repeated interaction can all move the answer. Design claims have to travel with their assumptions. A familiar format name is weak evidence unless the allocation and payment rules match the theorem.

Theme limit

Communication And Knowledge

Where the analogy breaks: A signal is not the same as cheap talk, and mutual knowledge is not automatically common knowledge. Costs, preferences, observability, and higher-order beliefs determine which communication model applies. The same sentence can be a credible signal in one setting, empty persuasion in another, and a coordination device in a third.

Equation Misreads

Equation misread

Dominance Inequality

Common misread: The common mistake is deleting a strategy because it performs badly in the most likely case. That is not dominance. Dominance requires the inequality to hold across every case named by the model.

Equation misread

Expected Utility

Common misread: The formula is not saying the average state actually happens. It is a decision rule for choosing under uncertainty.

Equation misread

Best Response

Common misread: A best response is always conditional. If the opponent action or belief changes, the best response may change too.

Equation misread

Nash Equilibrium

Common misread: Equilibrium does not mean the best overall outcome. It means no single player wants to move alone under the modeled payoffs.

Equation misread

Backward Induction

Common misread: Solving backward is only as good as the tree. If the tree leaves out information, renegotiation, or hidden types, the backward calculation can be precise but misleading.

Equation misread

Discounted Payoff Sum

Common misread: The formula does not make future punishment automatically powerful. If delta is small, future consequences may barely matter.

Equation misread

Bayes Rule

Common misread: Bayes rule does not tell you off-path beliefs when the observed signal has zero probability under the proposed strategy. That is why dynamic information games need careful belief discipline.

Equation misread

Auction Expected Payment

Common misread: A higher bid can raise both the chance of winning and the payment made when winning. The bidder's incentive depends on both effects, not one alone.

Equation misread

Common Knowledge Recursion

Common misread: A fact being true or widely known is not enough. Common knowledge requires the recursive publicness that lets players reason about other players' reasoning.
