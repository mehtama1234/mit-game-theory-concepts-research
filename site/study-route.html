Study Route

A compact path through the course, from first choice primitives to evidence-backed strategic reasoning.

Study route

Start With Choice Before Strategy

Reader question: What does a player want before anyone else enters the story?

Plain-language goal: Learn why preferences, utility, and expected utility are the measuring tools underneath every later payoff table.

Problem pressure: The course starts with one chooser because strategic math has nothing to stand on until the model knows what outcomes mean to that chooser. A payoff number is not raw happiness, money, or moral value by itself. It is a compact way to preserve a ranking of outcomes, and uncertainty forces the ranking to include possible futures that have not happened yet.

Why this stage comes now: If a reader skips this stage, every later game table looks more objective than it is. The later analysis of firms, bidders, senders, receivers, and cartels all depends on the same hidden step: someone decided what each outcome is worth to each player. This stage teaches that payoffs are constructed objects, not facts handed down by the universe.

Mathematical lever: The lever is mapping outcomes to ordered numbers, then weighting uncertain outcomes by their chances. In plain words, the math lets a choice made before uncertainty resolves be compared as one expected value rather than as a pile of disconnected possible endings.

Misuse warning: The common misuse is treating utility as a psychological meter or treating expected utility as a promise about what will happen. It is neither. It is a disciplined way to compare actions before the uncertain state is known.

Bridge to next stage: Once each player's payoff meaning is explicit, the next question is how to represent a situation where several choosers affect one another. That is why the route moves from individual choice to tables, trees, actions, and deletion rules.

Active study move: For each early example, rewrite the outcome in plain language before looking at the number. Say who is choosing, what alternatives are being compared, and what uncertainty remains. Then explain what information the utility number keeps and what information it throws away.

Proof of understanding: You understand this stage when you can take a new payoff table and reverse-engineer the preference claim behind each entry without treating the entries as interpersonal happiness units. You should also be able to say when changing the scale preserves the model and when risk makes the scale matter.

Transfer warning: When moving to papers or systems, do not trust objectives until you know what preference or welfare claim they encode. A clean-looking reward, payoff, or loss can hide the most important modeling assumption.

Study route

Represent The Strategic Situation

Reader question: What object keeps everyone's choices, timing, information, and payoffs visible?

Plain-language goal: Move from one-person choice to game representation: tables for simultaneous dependence, trees for time and information.

Problem pressure: Strategic confusion usually begins before any equilibrium calculation. The analyst has to decide who chooses, what each person can choose, whether choices happen at the same time, what each player observes, and what each outcome pays. A flat story hides these pieces; a game representation makes them inspectable.

Why this stage comes now: Representation comes before solution because the wrong representation answers the wrong question cleanly. A payoff table is useful when the issue is simultaneous dependence. A tree is needed when timing, later choices, and observed history matter. Dominance and rationalizability then show that some actions can be ruled out before full mutual consistency is demanded.

Mathematical lever: The lever is structured bookkeeping: actions become rows, columns, branches, or information sets; payoffs become entries at outcomes; pruning uses inequalities to remove actions that cannot be defended. The math matters because it prevents the reader from smuggling timing or beliefs into an informal story.

Misuse warning: The common misuse is solving the model that is easiest to draw. A table can make a threat look fixed even when it should be tested later in a tree. A deletion step can also be too strong if it quietly assumes beliefs or ignores a rival action.

Bridge to next stage: After the game object is visible and obviously indefensible actions are removed, the next problem is prediction: can the remaining proposed behavior survive every player's incentive to change alone in the represented situation?

Active study move: Take each example and draw both the compact table and the time-aware tree when possible. Mark which facts disappear in the table and which facts become visible in the tree. Then run one dominance comparison by holding each opponent action fixed.

Proof of understanding: You understand this stage when you can explain why the same story can produce different analysis depending on whether timing, observation, and histories are represented explicitly. You should be able to point to the exact row, branch, or information set where the modeling choice changes incentives.

Transfer warning: In a new paper, inspect the representation before the theorem. If the form hides timing or information, later algebra may be precise while answering the wrong strategic question. A compact normal form is useful only after the lost timing and observation facts have been judged irrelevant or encoded in strategies.

Study route

Make Predictions Survive Deviations

Reader question: Can each player look at the proposed outcome and still want to stay put?

Plain-language goal: Understand equilibrium as a mutual consistency test, then see why conflict and randomization require sharper versions of the same idea.

Problem pressure: A strategic prediction is fragile because every player may want to change once they see what the others are doing. The course therefore needs a stability test. The point is not to find the nicest outcome, the fairest outcome, or the outcome with the largest total payoff. The point is to ask whether anyone has a unilateral reason to move away.

Why this stage comes now: Equilibrium comes after representation because best responses need a defined action set and payoff table. It also comes before dynamic and information-rich models because Nash gives the base deviation idea that later concepts repair: subgame perfection adds later-history checks, Bayesian equilibrium adds type-contingent beliefs, and mixed strategies handle situations where predictable pure actions are exploitable.

Mathematical lever: The lever is the best-response comparison. Hold everyone else fixed, ask what one player would choose, and require the proposed outcome to pass that check for every player. In zero-sum or adversarial settings, randomization can be the stable plan because it removes the opponent's obvious target.

Misuse warning: The common misuse is reading equilibrium as endorsement. A Nash equilibrium can be inefficient, unfair, or unpleasant. It can also be one of several equilibria. Stability under unilateral deviation is a narrower claim than social goodness or uniqueness.

Bridge to next stage: Once the reader understands one-shot stability, the next question is whether a plan remains stable at future decision points. That is where threats, promises, bargaining, finite endings, and repeated punishment enter.

Active study move: For every claimed prediction, freeze all but one player and list that player's deviations. Repeat until each player has been checked. In mixed or zero-sum examples, ask what pure action would be exploited if the player became predictable.

Proof of understanding: You understand this stage when you can reject a plausible-sounding outcome because one player has a profitable unilateral deviation, while also refusing to call every equilibrium good. You should be able to write the deviation that fails and the payoff comparison that makes it fail.

Transfer warning: When reading applied work, separate stability, welfare, uniqueness, and robustness. Equilibrium language proves much less than readers often import into it. If a paper needs welfare or policy force, look for an added argument beyond the no-deviation check.

Study route

Test Time, Threats, And Patience

Reader question: Will a promised future action still make sense when the future arrives?

Plain-language goal: Use backward reasoning, subgame checks, bargaining logic, and repeated-game discipline to separate credible plans from empty announcements.

Problem pressure: Time creates a special kind of strategic trap: a person can announce a future action today that will no longer be attractive when the future arrives. The course uses dynamic games to make that future moment visible. It then uses repeated games to show when the future can discipline the present instead of merely undermining promises.

Why this stage comes now: This stage follows equilibrium because dynamic games need equilibrium logic at more than one point in the tree. A threat must pass the later best-response test, not just the initial announcement test. Repetition then changes the calculation by adding future rewards and punishments, but only when monitoring, patience, and credible punishment are strong enough.

Mathematical lever: The levers are backward recursion and discounting. Backward recursion solves later decisions before earlier ones. Discounting compares today's temptation with tomorrow's punishment by shrinking future payoffs according to how much players care about later periods.

Misuse warning: The common misuse is saying either all threats work because they were announced or all cooperation works because the game repeats. The course is stricter: threats require future credibility, and repeated cooperation requires an incentive inequality that actually beats deviation.

Bridge to next stage: After time is handled, the route turns to hidden information. Future incentives are not enough when players do not know types, costs, values, histories, signals, or what others have heard.

Active study move: When a future action matters, jump to the future node first and ask what the player wants there. For repeated games, write the current deviation gain and the discounted punishment loss in words before looking at formulas.

Proof of understanding: You understand this stage when you can tell the difference between a threat that is merely announced, a threat made credible by future payoffs, and a punishment path that is itself worth carrying out after the relevant history occurs.

Transfer warning: Do not let dynamic language substitute for dynamic incentives. Contracts, reputations, deadlines, monitoring, and outside options must be inside the payoff logic before time-based claims should be trusted. If a model says the future disciplines behavior, ask who observes deviation and why punishment remains optimal later.

Study route

Reason With Hidden Information

Reader question: What changes when players know different values, costs, types, histories, or facts that other players cannot directly see?

Plain-language goal: Track types, beliefs, updating, signaling, cheap talk, and common knowledge without treating beliefs as arbitrary story glue.

Problem pressure: Many strategic situations are hard because behavior is also evidence. A warranty may be both a promise and a clue about quality. A message may be useful or empty depending on the speaker's incentives. A public announcement may matter even when the underlying fact was already privately known.

Why this stage comes now: Information comes after the basic timing and equilibrium ideas because hidden information makes every earlier object conditional. Actions can depend on type, beliefs must be updated from observed behavior, and equilibrium must say what players believe at information sets as well as what they choose.

Mathematical lever: The levers are beliefs, Bayes-style updating, incentive compatibility, and knowledge recursion. Beliefs represent uncertainty over hidden types or states. Incentive compatibility asks whether the predicted type really wants to take the predicted action. Knowledge recursion tracks the layers of what everyone knows about what everyone knows.

Misuse warning: The common misuse is treating beliefs as free story glue. In a disciplined model, beliefs need a source: priors, signals, observed actions, public announcements, or equilibrium restrictions. If any type can cheaply imitate the same signal, the signal does not reveal type by magic.

Bridge to next stage: Once hidden information is visible, rule design becomes the natural next step. Auctions, screening, and market mechanisms are attempts to build rules that make privately informed people reveal or act on information in useful ways.

Active study move: For each information example, name the hidden fact, list possible types, and say what each type wants the receiver to believe. Then ask whether the observed action actually separates types or whether all types would imitate it.

Proof of understanding: You understand this stage when you can trace a belief from prior to signal to posterior, while noticing when off-path beliefs or cheap messages need extra discipline. You should be able to say which type wants to imitate and what prevents or permits that imitation.

Transfer warning: In new papers, beliefs are the danger zone. If a result depends on priors, likelihoods, off-path beliefs, or unverified publicness, mark the conclusion as assumption-sensitive. Strong conclusions need either evidence for those beliefs or a robustness check showing they are not doing all the work.

Study route

Read Rules As Incentive Machines

Reader question: How do auction and market rules change what strategic people choose?

Plain-language goal: See mechanism and auction topics as design problems: the rule must make the desired behavior privately sensible.

Problem pressure: Rules do not merely describe a market; they shape what selfish participants find worth doing. A payment rule changes bids. A ranking rule changes ad design. A screening rule changes who participates. The design problem is to make the private best response line up with the public or platform goal.

Why this stage comes now: Design belongs near the end because it uses almost every previous primitive. The designer needs payoffs, beliefs, incentives, information, and equilibrium responses. Auctions and screening are not isolated topics; they are places where the course asks how rules convert hidden values and strategic actions into allocation and payment outcomes.

Mathematical lever: The levers are allocation rules, payment rules, expected utility, and incentive compatibility. In plain language, the math separates who gets the object, what they pay, what they privately know, and whether telling the truth or bidding in a certain way is actually in their interest.

Misuse warning: The common misuse is trusting the label of a mechanism. Calling something a second-price auction, ad auction, or screening device is not enough. The reader must inspect the actual allocation rule, payment rule, information environment, and deviation opportunities.

Bridge to next stage: This stage closes the route by turning game theory into model-reading discipline. A reader should now be able to open a new paper or system design and ask what choices, incentives, beliefs, timing, and rules are doing the real work.

Active study move: For each mechanism, split the rule into allocation, payment, information, and participation. Then stand inside one participant's incentives and compare truthful or intended behavior with the best manipulation the rule permits under the stated assumptions.

Proof of understanding: You understand this stage when you can explain why a rule's label is less important than the deviation incentives it creates for privately informed participants. You should be able to identify the allocation rule, payment rule, information assumption, and best manipulation attempt.

Transfer warning: When moving to real platforms or markets, check budgets, measurement error, repeated play, quality scores, entry, and collusion before importing a clean auction or mechanism theorem. The theorem transfers only if the real institution preserves the incentive constraints that made the clean rule work.
