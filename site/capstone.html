Capstone Self-Test

Transfer checks for whether the reader can use the lab, not just recognize labels. Each card asks for a model choice, a mathematical check, a mistake diagnosis, and transcript-backed evidence.

Capstone self-test

Diagnose whether a threat changes entry behavior

Scenario: A dominant firm announces that if a small competitor enters, it will start a costly price war. The entrant must decide whether the announcement should keep it out.

Reader task: Decide whether the announcement is a credible strategic constraint or just cheap language. Name the representation, the future decision point, and the mathematical check that decides the case.

Expected reasoning: Represent the situation as a sequence, not only as a payoff table. First locate the later moment when the incumbent would actually choose fight or accommodate. Then compare the incumbent's payoffs at that later node. If fighting is worse once entry has happened, the earlier threat should not be believed unless some commitment device changes the later payoffs.

Math check: Use backward induction: solve the final choice first, assign that continuation value to the earlier branch, then let the entrant compare entering against staying out. The math matters because the current announcement is tested by future incentives, not by how forceful it sounds.

Evidence check: Use the extensive-form and backward-induction evidence records to confirm that the lecture treats trees as devices for exposing future choice points. Use the subgame-perfect evidence to check that credibility is evaluated inside later subgames.

What a wrong answer reveals: If the reader says the entrant should stay out because the incumbent threatened to fight, they are confusing speech with commitment. If they solve only the starting node, they are missing the reason dynamic games need backward reasoning.

Transfer prompt: Apply the same test to a platform that threatens to ban sellers after a rule violation. What future payoff would make the ban credible, and what future payoff would make it empty?

Minimum passing answer: A passing answer must say this is a sequential game, identify the incumbent's later choice, and test the threat at that later node. It should say that a threat changes entry only if fighting is optimal later or if some commitment device changes the later payoff comparison. The answer should not treat the announcement itself as proof.

Self-audit checklist: Check three things before accepting the answer. First, did it draw or describe the timing? Second, did it solve the future response before the entrant's first move? Third, did it name the assumption that could make the threat credible, such as reputation, contractual commitment, automated enforcement, or future deterrence value?

Transfer failure mode: The transfer fails when the new setting has a real commitment technology or a repeated-reputation motive that the simple entry story left out. A platform ban may be credible if failing to ban today encourages many future violations. In that case the future payoff includes deterrence, and the old one-shot accommodation logic is no longer enough.

Grading rubric: A strong answer earns credit by drawing the timeline before evaluating the threat, naming the later incumbent decision, and comparing fight versus accommodate at that later node. Extra credit comes from naming a commitment technology that would change the result. The answer fails if it treats the announcement as commitment, if it never checks the incumbent's later payoff, or if it calls the outcome Nash without asking whether the continuation behavior is credible.

Capstone self-test

Read an auction rule as an incentive machine

Scenario: A marketplace changes its ad auction so bids, quality scores, and payment rules jointly determine which ad appears and what the winner pays.

Reader task: Separate the designer's goal from each bidder's goal, then explain whether truthful or shaded bidding is encouraged by the rule.

Expected reasoning: Start by identifying what the platform controls: allocation and payment rules. Then read from the bidder's point of view. A bidder compares the payoff from one report with the payoff from another report, taking into account both chance of winning and price paid. The rule matters only through the incentives it creates for participants who care about their own values and costs.

Math check: The core check is an incentive constraint: payoff from intended behavior must be at least as high as payoff from a misreport. In auction examples, expected payment combines the probability of winning with the price conditional on winning, so bid choice cannot be judged by price alone.

Evidence check: Use the auction, revenue-equivalence, and ad-auction evidence records to confirm that the lectures connect allocation, payment, and incentives rather than treating auction formats as labels.

What a wrong answer reveals: If the reader says the platform can simply ask bidders to be truthful, they are missing the private-information problem. If they look only at the winning price, they are ignoring how the rule changes the probability of winning.

Transfer prompt: Apply the same reasoning to a hiring platform that asks applicants to report skills. What rule would make truthful reporting attractive, and what hidden information might still break the design?

Minimum passing answer: A passing answer must separate the platform's objective from the bidder's payoff, then split the rule into allocation and payment. It should explain that a bidder compares reports by expected utility: chance of winning, value if shown, and payment if charged. If the answer says truthful bidding happens because the platform wants truth, it fails the incentive test.

Self-audit checklist: Check whether the answer names the private information, the report or bid, the allocation rule, the payment rule, and the tempting misreport. Then check whether it compares the intended report against at least one deviation from the bidder's point of view. Finally, ask whether quality scores, budgets, or repeated participation changed the textbook auction assumptions.

Transfer failure mode: The transfer fails when the hiring platform cannot verify skills, when applicants face different costs of acquiring credentials, or when the platform's ranking rule rewards exaggeration. In that setting the auction analogy is useful only after rebuilding the allocation and payment objects as interview slots, wages, reputational costs, or screening burdens.

Grading rubric: A strong answer earns credit by separating the platform's design problem from each bidder's private payoff problem, then writing the intended report and the tempting misreport as alternatives. Extra credit comes from explaining how quality scores change allocation rather than simply raising or lowering price. The answer fails if it says the rule works because the platform wants truth, if it ignores payment conditional on winning, or if it never states the bidder's private information.

Capstone self-test

Tell whether an action really reveals hidden information

Scenario: A seller offers a warranty and claims that only high-quality sellers would be willing to do so. Buyers update their beliefs after observing the warranty.

Reader task: Decide whether the warranty separates high-quality sellers from low-quality sellers or whether both types would imitate it.

Expected reasoning: Identify the hidden types, the action buyers observe, and the payoff from imitating. A signal works only if the cost or benefit of the action differs across types enough that one type wants the action and the other does not. If both types want the same visible action, observing it cannot teach buyers which type they face.

Math check: Use Bayes rule for beliefs on observed paths and incentive constraints for type behavior. The separating story requires two comparisons: the high type prefers the signal to hiding, and the low type prefers not to imitate the signal. Beliefs and actions must fit together.

Evidence check: Use the types-and-beliefs, perfect-Bayesian-equilibrium, signaling, and screening evidence records. They show that hidden information changes both what people do and what others infer from what they see.

What a wrong answer reveals: If the reader says the warranty proves quality by itself, they are skipping the imitation check. If they update beliefs without asking how likely each type was to choose the warranty, they are using belief language without Bayesian discipline.

Transfer prompt: Apply the same test to a startup founder buying an expensive certification. When would the certification reveal ability, and when would it become a badge that every type buys?

Minimum passing answer: A passing answer must name at least two hidden types, state what action is observed, and compare the payoff from sending the signal for each type. It must also say how buyers update after seeing the warranty. The signal separates only if the intended type wants to send it and the imitation type does not.

Self-audit checklist: Check whether the answer has both halves of the model: the receiver's belief update and the sender's incentive check. Then ask whether the signal is cheaper, more valuable, or more credible for one type than another. Finally, verify that the proposed belief follows from behavior on the observed path rather than being invented after the equilibrium is declared.

Transfer failure mode: The transfer fails when the certification is easy for all founders to buy, when investors cannot tell whether it was earned honestly, or when low-ability founders benefit enough from imitation to absorb the cost. Then the badge may advertise ambition, but it does not separate ability without another cost, verification mechanism, or screening rule.

Grading rubric: A strong answer earns credit by naming the hidden types, the observed action, the receiver's belief after observing it, and both sender incentive constraints. Extra credit comes from explaining pooling as a real possibility rather than treating it as failure to understand the signal. The answer fails if it updates the buyer's belief without asking which type would choose the warranty, or if it assumes cost alone makes an action informative.

Capstone self-test

Check whether future punishment can support cooperation

Scenario: Two firms can keep prices high each month, but either firm can secretly undercut this month and earn a short-term gain before the other responds.

Reader task: Decide whether cooperation can be stable, name the immediate temptation, name the future punishment, and state exactly what comparison the model must pass.

Expected reasoning: Separate the immediate temptation from the future discipline. Cooperation is possible only if the value lost from future punishment is large enough to outweigh the one-time gain from cheating. Then check whether the punishment is itself credible when the future arrives.

Math check: Use the discounted-value inequality. Today's cheating gain is compared with a stream of future losses weighted by delta, delta squared, and later powers. A higher delta means future consequences matter more today; a lower delta makes the temptation harder to discipline.

Evidence check: Use the repeated-game, folk-theorem, implicit-cartel, and one-shot-deviation evidence records. They show that repetition changes incentives by changing continuation values, not by making players nicer.

What a wrong answer reveals: If the reader says repetition automatically creates cooperation, they are missing the discount-factor comparison. If they specify a punishment but never check that the punishment is credible, they are importing the dynamic-credibility error into repeated games.

Transfer prompt: Apply the same reasoning to creators on a shared platform who could copy each other's work once. What makes future exclusion strong enough to discourage copying?

Minimum passing answer: A passing answer must write the two continuation stories in words: cooperate and keep the future, or cheat now and face punishment later. It should say that cooperation is stable only if the discounted future loss is at least as large as the immediate cheating gain. It must also check that punishment is observable and credible. The answer should explicitly reject the shortcut that repeated contact by itself creates trust.

Self-audit checklist: Check whether the answer identifies the short-run gain, the punishment path, the discount factor, and the monitoring assumption. Then ask whether punishers actually want to punish when the future arrives. Finally, check whether the same players expect enough future interaction for continuation value to matter today.

Transfer failure mode: The transfer fails when copying is hard to detect, when platform exclusion is too costly to enforce, when creators do not expect future interaction, or when punishment violates the platform's own incentives. In those cases the future cannot discipline the present unless the model adds monitoring, enforceable sanctions, or a reputation mechanism. It also fails when mistaken accusations are common, because punishment then weakens cooperation instead of protecting it.

Grading rubric: A strong answer earns credit by writing the comparison between one-time cheating gain and discounted future loss in words before using symbols. Extra credit comes from checking that punishment is observable and credible for the punishers, not only painful for the cheater. The answer fails if it says repetition creates trust by itself, if it forgets the discount factor, or if it specifies punishment without checking whether anyone will actually carry it out.

Capstone self-test

Distinguish private information from shared awareness

Scenario: Several people privately receive the same warning about a coordination problem, but only later does a public announcement reveal that everyone received it.

Reader task: Explain why the public announcement can change behavior even if it contains no new physical fact.

Expected reasoning: The key is not only who knows the fact. The key is who knows that others know, and who knows that others know that they know. Coordination often depends on shared confidence about other people's awareness. A public announcement can create that shared ladder of belief.

Math check: Use knowledge recursion. Common knowledge is not a larger pile of private facts; it is a recursive condition about mutual awareness. The mathematical idea exists because coordinated action can require beliefs about beliefs, not only beliefs about the world.

Evidence check: Use common-knowledge, cheap-talk, rationalizability, and Nash evidence. These records connect public information, strategic communication, and the layers of reasoning needed when choices depend on what others know.

What a wrong answer reveals: If the reader says the announcement should not matter because everyone already knew the fact, they are treating information as individual storage rather than shared strategic structure. If they assume words always persuade, they are missing cheap-talk incentives.

Transfer prompt: Apply the same test to a bank run warning or a platform outage notice. When does publicness change behavior even before anyone's private fact changes?

Minimum passing answer: A passing answer must distinguish the fact from the public event that makes the fact shared. It should say that coordination can require beliefs about other people's beliefs, so a public announcement can change action even for people who already privately knew the content. It should also separate publicness from truthfulness. The answer should name what action becomes safer once people know that others received the same message.

Self-audit checklist: Check whether the answer names the action that requires coordination and the level of shared awareness needed for that action. Then ask whether the message was actually public, whether everyone knew it was public, and whether sender incentives made the announcement credible. If cheap talk is involved, the answer needs a separate incentive check. Also check whether the model truly needs common knowledge or only one or two layers of belief.

Transfer failure mode: The transfer fails when the announcement reaches groups unevenly, when people interpret it differently, when receivers doubt who else heard it, or when the sender benefits from exaggeration. A platform outage notice can be public but not credible, or credible but not common knowledge, and those are different failures. It also fails when the coordinated action depends on private costs that the announcement does not reveal.

Grading rubric: A strong answer earns credit by separating the content of the warning from the public event that makes the warning shared. Extra credit comes from naming the coordinated action that becomes safer only after higher-order beliefs align. The answer fails if it says the announcement cannot matter because everyone privately knew the fact, if it treats publicness as the same thing as truth, or if it ignores sender incentives in a cheap-talk setting.

Capstone self-test

Recognize when randomness is strategic rather than indecision

Scenario: Two competitors each have a move that works well only when the other side fails to predict it. No single pure move remains stable.

Reader task: Explain why the model may ask players to randomize, and identify whose payoffs are equalized when the mixing probability is chosen.

Expected reasoning: Predictability creates exploitation. If one pure action can be countered, a stable pattern may require a lottery over actions. The randomization is chosen so the other side cannot improve by switching to a different response. This is a discipline on incentives, not a claim that the player is confused.

Math check: Set the opponent's expected payoffs from their relevant actions equal, then solve for the mixing probability that makes the opponent indifferent. The reason this feels backwards is that one player's probability controls the other player's comparison.

Evidence check: Use the mixed-strategy, minimax, expected-utility, and Nash evidence records to check the distinction between uncertainty, randomization, and mutual best response.

What a wrong answer reveals: If the reader says mixed strategies mean the player does not care, they are missing the strategic role of probability. If they equalize the wrong player's payoffs, they will solve the wrong incentive problem.

Transfer prompt: Apply the same reasoning to cybersecurity defenses that randomize inspection times. Whose behavior is the randomization meant to discipline?

Minimum passing answer: A passing answer must say that randomization prevents the opponent from exploiting a predictable pure move. It should identify which player's mixing probability is being chosen and whose payoff comparison is being equalized. The answer should make clear that indifference is produced by the strategic environment, not by personal apathy. It should also say why no pure action survives the no-deviation check.

Self-audit checklist: Check whether the answer names the pure actions, the opponent's possible responses, and the expected payoff comparison. Then verify that it equalizes the opponent's relevant actions when solving for this player's mixing probability. Finally, ask whether the resulting probabilities are feasible and whether both sides are best responding to the mixed profile. If the calculation equalizes the mixer's own payoffs first, the answer may have solved the wrong side of the incentive problem.

Transfer failure mode: The transfer fails when randomness cannot be committed to, when the opponent observes the random seed, when inspection costs vary over time, or when the defender's objective includes constraints outside the payoff table. Cybersecurity randomization only disciplines attackers if attackers cannot predict or cheaply observe the schedule before choosing where to attack. It also fails if the defender cannot tolerate the risk created by low-inspection periods.

Grading rubric: A strong answer earns credit by identifying the pure actions, explaining why each pure action can be exploited, and stating whose payoff comparison is equalized by the mixing probability. Extra credit comes from checking that the resulting probabilities are feasible and that both sides are best responding. The answer fails if it treats mixing as indecision, if it equalizes the wrong player's payoffs, or if it forgets that the randomization must be hidden or committed enough to affect the opponent.
