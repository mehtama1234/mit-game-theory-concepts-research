Lecture 7: Zero-Sum Games

Studies exact conflict, where one player's gain is the other's loss and minimax/security reasoning becomes natural.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture studies exact conflict, where one player's gain is the other's loss. This special structure makes security reasoning natural: each player asks how to protect against the opponent's best attack.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through maxmin, minimax, and mixed strategies. The player compares worst-case payoffs and may randomize to avoid being exploited by a predictable action.

The mathematical object has to exist because pure actions cannot always carry stable predictions. A probability distribution over actions lets the player control predictability itself. The distribution becomes the strategy because it determines opponents’ expected payoffs before the random action is realized. The mathematical object has to exist because exact conflict has special structure. When one player’s payoff is the negative of the other’s, the model can collapse two objectives into one value seen from opposite sides. That structure justifies security and minimax tools that would be too strong in general games. The mathematical object has to exist because adversarial choice requires a guarantee. The player needs to compare strategies by what the opponent can force against them. Minimax turns that defensive question into a nested optimization: protect the worst case, then choose the best protection.

Worked Mini-Example

In matching pennies, choosing heads every time can be exploited. Randomizing evenly makes the opponent unable to target a fixed pattern, which gives the player a security value.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to apply zero-sum intuition everywhere. Many economic games mix conflict with shared surplus, future cooperation, or side effects, so exact-opposition logic may overstate rivalry.

Students often get stuck by asking what the player “really” chooses. The answer is that the random device is part of the strategy; after it realizes an action, the distribution was still the planned commitment. Students often get stuck by calling any rivalry zero-sum. Many competitive economic settings still have joint gains, inefficiencies, or possibilities for collusion. Zero-sum is a payoff structure, not a mood. Students often get stuck by confusing minimax with pessimism. It is not a personality trait; it is the rational response to a modeled opponent whose payoff is opposed to yours.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for indifference equations, randomized play, or models where predictable behavior would be punished. Look for adversarial models where maximizing my payoff is exactly minimizing yours. Look for worst-case guarantees, security levels, or saddle-point reasoning.

Concepts In This Lecture

Mixed Strategies

Problem: The problem is what to predict when choosing one pure action would make a player exploitable or when no pure-action equilibrium exists.

Mathematical handle: The object is a probability distribution over pure strategies, often chosen so the other player has no strict favorite response.

What breaks without it: Without mixed strategies, the course would falsely conclude that some games have no stable prediction simply because no pure action profile works. It would also miss why randomness can be chosen as discipline rather than noise.

Open concept page

Zero-Sum Games

Problem: The problem is pure conflict: one side's gain is the other side's loss, so cooperation or joint surplus is not the issue.

Mathematical handle: The object is a payoff function where player two's payoff is the negative of player one's payoff.

What breaks without it: Without the zero-sum distinction, students may apply adversarial security logic to games where cooperation or joint surplus exists. The course needs this special case precisely because its conclusions are sharper than in general games.

Open concept page

Minimax

Problem: The problem is choosing under hostile response: what can I guarantee if the other side tries to hurt me as much as possible?

Mathematical handle: The object is a security calculation: maximize the minimum payoff you can be forced to accept, or minimize the maximum damage the opponent can impose.

What breaks without it: Without minimax, the course could describe conflict but not the logic of protecting against exploitation. It would miss why randomization and worst-case evaluation can produce a stable value in zero-sum games.
