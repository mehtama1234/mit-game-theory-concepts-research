Lecture 15: Implicit Cartels

Applies repeated-game enforcement to firms that may sustain collusion without an explicit enforceable contract.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture applies repeated-game logic to firms that may keep prices high without an explicit enforceable contract. The strategic question is whether future punishment can discipline today's undercutting.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as a cartel incentive constraint. Collusive profit today plus future cooperation is compared with the one-time cheating gain plus future punishment profit.

The mathematical object has to exist because tacit collusion depends on a comparison across time. The model must represent the current profit from cheating and the discounted loss from future punishment. That object turns cartel stability into an incentive constraint instead of a story about firms being friendly.

Worked Mini-Example

A gas station could cut price and win customers today. If nearby stations respond with a price war, the discounted future loss may make quiet high pricing self-enforcing.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to infer explicit agreement from high prices alone. The model explains an incentive mechanism; real antitrust claims require evidence about communication, monitoring, and market conditions.

Students often get stuck by equating collusion with communication. The model can produce tacit collusion if prices, market shares, or other signals let firms detect and punish deviations.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for industries with repeated price interaction, observability of deviations, and credible punishment paths.

Concepts In This Lecture

Implicit Cartels

Problem: The problem is how firms might keep prices high without signing an explicit cartel agreement.

Mathematical handle: The object is a repeated oligopoly game comparing collusive profits, deviation profits, and future punishment profits.

What breaks without it: Without the cartel application, repeated-game ideas could feel abstract. The course would miss how continuation values, monitoring, and punishment shape real pricing incentives and antitrust concerns.
