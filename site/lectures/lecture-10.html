Lecture 10: Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

Repairs Nash for dynamic games by requiring continuation behavior to remain rational inside every subgame.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture repairs Nash for dynamic settings. It requires strategies to remain equilibrium behavior after every proper subgame, so threats and promises must be credible at the histories where they matter.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters by applying Nash equilibrium inside each subgame. A full strategy profile must pass the no-deviation test not only at the start but after relevant histories.

The mathematical object has to exist because strategic prediction is circular. Each player’s best action depends on the others’ actions, so the model needs a profile where all those conditional choices fit together. Nash equilibrium is the formal closure of the best-response loop. The mathematical object has to exist because a dynamic strategy includes plans for histories that may not occur. Those plans can support threats, so they must be checked. Subgame perfection formalizes the demand that continuation behavior is itself equilibrium behavior wherever a real subgame begins.

Worked Mini-Example

A firm may threaten a price war after entry. Subgame perfection asks whether the price war would actually be profitable once entry has happened, not whether it scares entrants beforehand.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to check only the opening node. A dynamic prediction can look stable initially while relying on later behavior no player would want to carry out.

Students often get stuck by importing the everyday meaning of equilibrium as balance or goodness. Nash equilibrium can be inefficient or ugly. Its claim is narrower: no single player wants to move alone. Students often get stuck by ignoring histories that do not occur in equilibrium. Those histories are exactly where empty threats hide, so the course makes students inspect them.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for claims that a behavior pattern persists because no individual participant can gain by deviating alone. Look for dynamic games, off-path histories, or threats where the key question is whether the promised future action would really be optimal.

Concepts In This Lecture

Nash Equilibrium

Problem: The problem is making a prediction that does not immediately fall apart when each participant asks, given what the others are doing, should I switch?

Mathematical handle: The object is an action or strategy profile where every player's choice is a best response to the others' choices.

What breaks without it: Without Nash equilibrium, payoff tables would produce stories but not disciplined predictions. Any outcome could be proposed even if one participant has an obvious profitable deviation once everyone else behavior is named.

Open concept page

Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

Problem: The problem is that Nash equilibrium can allow plans based on threats that would not be carried out if the threatened moment arrived.

Mathematical handle: The object is a Nash equilibrium that also induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame.

What breaks without it: Without subgame perfection, dynamic models can rest on noncredible threats. A strategy profile may pass the initial Nash test only because it prescribes off-path behavior that nobody would actually choose later.
