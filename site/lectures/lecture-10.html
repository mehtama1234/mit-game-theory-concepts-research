Lecture 10 · Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency, Time, Threats, And Credibility

Lecture 10: Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

Repairs Nash for dynamic games by requiring continuation behavior to remain rational inside every subgame.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: if a strategy profile is Nash at the start, why ask more? The lecture shows that a profile can be stable initially while relying on later behavior nobody would actually choose.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture repairs Nash for dynamic settings. It requires strategies to remain equilibrium behavior after every proper subgame, so threats and promises must be credible at the histories where they matter.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters by applying Nash equilibrium inside each subgame. A full strategy profile must pass the no-deviation test not only at the start but after relevant histories.

Strategic prediction is circular. Each player's best move depends on what others do, which depends on what each of them expects, which depends on what everyone expects the first player to do. The model needs a fixed point where all these expectations and moves fit together. Nash equilibrium is that fixed point—it is the formal answer to the question, "What profile survives the logical loop of mutual best response?" A strategy written down includes plans for futures that may never occur — branches of the tree the players hope to avoid. Those plans can look great on paper but be impossible to execute. Mathematics formalizes the requirement that every plan, even the ones hidden in branches that never happen, must still be a plan the player would choose if that branch actually arrived. Subgame perfection is the formal statement of this requirement.

Worked Mini-Example

A firm may threaten a price war after entry. Subgame perfection asks whether the price war would actually be profitable once entry has happened, not whether it scares entrants beforehand.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to check only the opening node. A dynamic prediction can look stable initially while relying on later behavior no player would want to carry out.

The tempting shortcut is to check incentives only before the game begins. That fails in dynamic games because threats and promises live at later histories, and those histories need their own rationality tests.

A common trap is to confuse Nash equilibrium with a good outcome or a balanced state. Students hear equilibrium and imagine something stable and fair. But a Nash equilibrium can be the worst outcome available, unfair, or wasteful. It only guarantees one narrow thing: no single player can unilaterally improve. Do not expect it to be nice. Students get tripped up because they only check whether people want to deviate at the start. They ignore paths that 'should not happen' in equilibrium. But threats only work by affecting what players expect to happen if they deviate. Those off-path scenarios are where hollow threats hide, so the course forces you to look.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that credibility is local. A strategy must remain a Nash equilibrium inside every relevant subgame, so the solution concept rejects plans that survive only because impossible-looking histories were ignored.

Reader Work

For every threat or promise, jump to the history where it would be carried out and solve from there. The work is to test credibility locally, not just at the initial node where the threat changes behavior.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: name a Nash-looking strategy profile that relies on a later threat. Then test the threat inside the subgame after the relevant history. If it fails there, explain why Nash at the start was too weak.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to the one-shot deviation principle: once dynamic credibility is the standard, the next problem is finding a practical way to check many possible deviations.

How To Recognize This Later

In new situations, look for a pattern of behavior where no single participant can improve their own outcome by changing their own move alone, given that everyone else keeps doing what they are doing. When you spot that pattern, you have identified a candidate Nash equilibrium. Watch for any strategic situation where someone makes a threat or promise, and ask: would they actually follow through? Watch for games that play out in stages. Watch for plans that include moves 'just in case' on branches that should not happen. In all these cases, subgame perfection is the question: would the plan hold up even if that supposedly-impossible branch showed up?

Concepts In This Lecture

Nash Equilibrium

Problem: You are trying to predict what a group of people will do when they are all trying to outthink each other. The problem is: your prediction only works if, after each person sees what you said the others would do, that person does not regret their own choice and decide to change.

Mathematical handle: The object is a profile—a list of each player's move—where every player's move is a best response to the others' moves. You can write it as a tuple or a list, but the key is that each component meets the best-response test.

What breaks without it: Without the Nash test, any outcome could be defended just by naming it. Someone could propose a pattern and offer no reason why each person would stick to it. Nash forces the question: if everyone else follows this pattern, does each individual want to follow it too?

Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

Problem: Sometimes a player says 'I will do something painful to punish you.' That threat only works if the player would actually carry it out if the moment came. But if the painful thing hurts the player too, they might not do it. A plan built on such a hollow threat falls apart once you realize the threat is fake.

Mathematical handle: Start with a Nash equilibrium — a plan where nobody wants to switch at the beginning. Now make a stronger requirement: that same plan must also be a Nash equilibrium inside every 'subgame' — every piece of the game tree that can stand on its own as a complete game at some later point. If a plan fails this test anywhere, reject it.

What breaks without it: Without it, you can build strategies on promises you would never keep. A player might say 'if you enter, I will fight back hard' — but if fighting back after entry would lose them money, they would never actually do it. The equilibrium survives only because it hides this broken promise.
