Lecture 23: Bargaining with Incomplete Information

Adds hidden values or costs to negotiation, making delay and offers partly about learning what the other side knows.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture revisits bargaining after adding hidden values, costs, or quality. Complete information predicted immediate agreement when gains from trade exist; hidden information can create screening, delay, disagreement, or adverse selection.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through types, participation constraints, posted prices, posterior beliefs, and selected pools of participants. The payoff calculation must account for who accepts, not only what price is posted.

The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary average reasoning lies in selected populations. A cutoff, participation condition, or posterior distribution is needed to say who accepts, who rejects, and what the uninformed side should believe after observing that choice.

Worked Mini-Example

An insurer posts a price. The buyers who accept may be the people with the highest expected costs, so raising the price can worsen the pool rather than simply increase revenue per buyer.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to reason from the original average population. Screening changes the composition of participants, so the relevant average after the offer may be very different.

Students often get stuck by treating hidden information as just another probability. The lecture point is sharper: the action or price changes which hidden types remain in the game, so the probability distribution after participation is not the original distribution.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for models where an offer, price, contract, or test makes different hidden types sort themselves. If changing the rule changes the pool of participants, the screening/adverse-selection primitive is active.

Screening And Adverse Selection

Concepts In This Lecture

Screening And Adverse Selection

Problem: The problem is that a price or offer can change who shows up. A seller, insurer, or bargainer may set a rule thinking about an average customer, but the people who accept are not average; they are selected by the rule itself.

Mathematical handle: The object is an expected payoff under a cutoff rule: a price or offer is accepted only by types for whom accepting is better than rejecting, so the distribution of accepted types changes with the price.

What breaks without it: Without this concept, the course would predict trade whenever buyer value exceeds seller cost. It would miss the reason some trades fail: the terms that attract one type may drive away another or reveal information that changes future bargaining.
