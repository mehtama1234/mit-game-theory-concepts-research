Lecture 1 · Choice Under Tradeoffs, Strategic Dependence, Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Lecture 1: Introduction to Individual Decision-Making

Builds the individual-choice foundation: preferences, utility, beliefs, lotteries, and the idea that choices need a payoff language before strategy can begin.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: can we just say what people choose and move on? The lecture slows that down because choice is not usable in a model until the alternatives can be compared in a stable way.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture starts before game theory: a player cannot reason strategically until the model says what that player prefers. It builds from ranked outcomes to utility numbers and then to risky choices, so later payoff tables are not arbitrary decoration.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as representation. A preference ordering becomes a utility function, and uncertain outcomes become expected utility. The point is not to measure happiness; it is to make comparisons precise enough for later strategic checks.

The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary language can hide contradictions. People can say they like many things, but a model needs to know whether those comparisons can be used together. A preference relation is the smallest formal object that lets the course ask whether the choices are complete enough and consistent enough to support later payoff reasoning. The mathematical object has to exist because tables and equations cannot operate directly on vague rankings. Utility turns the ranking into a number while preserving the choice information the model needs. It is the handle that lets the course move from “preferred” to “has the larger payoff in this calculation.” The mathematical object has to exist because uncertainty creates bundles of possible outcomes. A player choosing now cannot optimize separately in each future state; they need one comparison for the whole risky action. Expected utility is that comparison, built by weighting each consequence by the probability assigned before the outcome is known. The mathematical object has to exist because strategic dependence needs a container. If each payoff depends on everyone’s action, the model needs a way to list the whole action profile and the payoff consequences together. Strategic form is the simplest object that keeps those dependencies visible and checkable. The mathematical object has to exist because plausible behavior depends on beliefs. A strategy can be reasonable even when it is not part of a Nash equilibrium, provided some coherent belief makes it optimal. Rationalizability formalizes that support relation between beliefs and best responses. The mathematical object has to exist because auction outcomes are created by rules. Values, bids, allocation, and payments have to be represented together so the course can ask how a bidder trades off winning more often against paying more when they win.

Worked Mini-Example

If someone chooses between a sure payment and a lottery, the model needs more than a list of outcomes. It needs a rule for comparing risk, so the lecture turns probability-weighted consequences into a choice object.

Mistakes To Avoid

The easy mistake is to treat utility as a psychological substance. In this course, utility is a compact ranking device that lets later game models compute what a player would rather do.

The tempting shortcut is to treat payoff numbers as direct measurements of pleasure, money, or moral value. That shortcut fails because the later strategic model only needs a disciplined ranking that survives comparisons, lotteries, and counterfactual choices.

Students often get stuck by asking whether the preference is realistic for every human mood. The course is doing a modeling move: it asks whether the relevant choices in the problem can be treated as stable enough for strategic analysis. Students often get stuck by treating utility points like dollars or happiness units. Sometimes dollars enter utility, but utility itself is the model’s choice index, not necessarily a literal commodity. Students often get stuck by averaging dollar outcomes when the model needs utility outcomes, or by forgetting that the probabilities are beliefs from the player’s point of view rather than facts known after the uncertainty resolves. Students often get stuck by reading a row as if it has a single value. A row has several consequences, one for each possible opponent action, so a strategy cannot be judged until the contingency is named. Students often get stuck because rationalizability feels permissive. It is permissive on purpose, but not empty: a strategy still has to be a best response to some belief, not just a move someone might randomly choose. Students often get stuck by treating bids as direct value reports. In many auctions, bids are strategic actions distorted by payment rules, risk, competition, and beliefs about rivals.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that game theory starts with a language for individual tradeoffs before it talks about strategic conflict. Preferences say what is better than what; utility compresses those rankings into numbers; expected utility adds uncertainty without pretending the numbers are a happiness meter.

Reader Work

While reading this lecture, build a small preference table before writing any utility numbers. Mark which comparisons are rankings, which comparisons involve uncertainty, and which claims would change if the utility scale were stretched. The work is to separate choice order from psychological interpretation.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: explain why two different utility scales can represent the same preference ordering, then give one situation where the spacing between utility numbers would start to matter. If you cannot separate ranking from scale, the later payoff tables will feel more objective than they are.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to representation of games: once each player has a payoff language, the next problem is to describe who moves, what they can do, what they know, and how everyone's choices jointly create outcomes.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for the place where an economic model has to say what an actor counts as better before any strategic reasoning begins. Look for a paper turning goals, payoffs, welfare, or preferences into a number that can be compared or optimized. Look for private information, lotteries, risk, or unknown types where the model has to average payoffs across possible worlds. Look for simultaneous moves, payoff matrices, or models where the exact timing is suppressed so strategic dependence can be seen cleanly. Look for models that want to separate impossible behavior from behavior that is merely one of several plausible responses. Look for allocation and payment rules that transform private values into strategic bids.

Concepts In This Lecture

Preferences

Problem: The problem is that before we talk about strategy, we need a way to say what one person is trying to get. A person may like one outcome more than another even when dollars, risk, effort, and timing are mixed together.

Mathematical handle: The object is a preference relation: a comparison rule over outcomes. When it is complete the person can compare any two outcomes; when it is transitive the comparisons do not cycle back on themselves.

What breaks without it: Without preferences, a payoff table has no disciplined source. The analyst could assign numbers, but there would be no explanation of why a player ranks one result over another or why a later best-response calculation should be trusted.

Open concept page

Utility

Problem: The problem is that rankings are awkward to compute with. A payoff table, expected value, or equilibrium check needs numbers, not only verbal statements like this outcome is preferred.

Mathematical handle: The object is a utility function u that assigns a number to each outcome while preserving the preference ordering. Higher utility means higher in the ranking, not necessarily more money or happiness.

What breaks without it: Without utility, later algebra would float free from choice behavior. The course could say one outcome is preferred, but it could not easily compute expected payoffs, best responses, or surplus divisions.

Open concept page

Expected Utility

Problem: The problem is choosing when the outcome is uncertain: a bid might win or lose, a risky action might pay off or fail, and a type may be one of several possibilities.

Mathematical handle: The object is expected utility: utility in each possible state weighted by that state's probability. It compresses a risky situation into one comparison number.

What breaks without it: Without expected utility, Bayesian games and auctions would have no disciplined way to evaluate actions under unknown types or rival values. The model would either ignore risk or smuggle in an unspecified attitude toward it.

Open concept page

Strategic Form Games

Problem: The problem is representing a situation where everyone chooses without first seeing the others' choices, yet everyone's payoff depends on the whole combination.

Mathematical handle: The object is a normal-form game: players, action sets, and payoff functions collected into a table or payoff map.

What breaks without it: Without strategic form, simultaneous games would remain verbal scenarios. Students could argue about incentives, but they would lack the structured object needed to compare rows, columns, and deviations cleanly.

Open concept page

Rationalizability

Problem: The problem is deciding which strategies can be defended as sensible without requiring everyone to land on one exact equilibrium.

Mathematical handle: The object is a set of strategies that survive because each can be a best response to some belief about the other players' surviving strategies.

What breaks without it: Without rationalizability, students may jump too quickly from dominance to Nash. They would miss the middle ground where a strategy survives because a player can hold a belief that makes it a best response.

Open concept page

Auctions

Problem: The problem is allocating an object when people know their own values but the seller and rival bidders may not.

Mathematical handle: The object is an auction mechanism: bidder values, bid spaces, allocation rules, and payment rules.

What breaks without it: Without auction theory, the course would miss one of the clearest economic applications of incomplete-information games. It could not explain bid shading, truthful bidding in special formats, or why payment rules change behavior.
