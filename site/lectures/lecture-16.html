Lecture 16 · Choice Under Tradeoffs, Private Information And Beliefs

Lecture 16: Bayesian Games

Introduces hidden types and priors so strategy can be modeled when players do not share the same information.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: can we just say players are uncertain and keep using the same game? The lecture says uncertainty must be modeled: who knows what, what is hidden, and what probabilities are assigned.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture begins the incomplete-information part of the course. It introduces types and priors so hidden values, costs, beliefs, or payoff states become part of the game rather than vague uncertainty.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through type spaces and probability distributions. A strategy can become a rule from types to actions, and payoffs are evaluated in expectation over unknown types.

Uncertainty creates a bundle of possible outcomes. You choose now and find out later which one happens. You cannot optimize separately for each future state—you need one score for the whole risky action. Expected utility is that one score, built by summing (payoff × probability) over all possibilities you assign a belief to. The mathematical machinery exists because private information changes what the decision problem is. If payoffs depend on a hidden type, you cannot solve the game by looking at one payoff table. You need types to name what is hidden, priors to say how likely each type is, and type-contingent strategies to specify what each type does. Without these objects, private values and costs would be invisible. The equilibrium calculation would be meaningless. Uncertainty has two faces: what the world actually is, and what someone thinks the world is. Types capture the first; beliefs capture the second. To analyze strategic moves—like lying, signaling, or updating—you must keep them separate. If you blur them, you cannot ask whether a move reveals truth or hides it. Ordinary reasoning with an average lie in filtered populations. If you set a price and only certain types accept, the people who say yes are not representative of everyone you offered to. You need a math object—a cutoff rule, a participation condition, or a posterior belief—to track who joins after the rule is made known. That object has to capture the idea that some types prefer in and some prefer out, and the payoff depends only on the ones who accept.

Worked Mini-Example

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to say players are uncertain and leave it there. Bayesian games force the model to name what is hidden, who knows it, and what probabilities others assign.

The tempting shortcut is to use uncertainty as a verbal excuse for anything. That fails because incomplete-information analysis needs a type space, priors, and strategies that specify what each possible type would do.

Two common traps: students sometimes average dollar amounts when they should average payoff (utility) amounts, because payoff measures what you care about, not the raw dollars. Also, students forget that probabilities come from your belief before you know the answer—they are not frequencies from looking back at what already happened. Students get stuck by thinking uncertainty is external—something that happens to the model. In a Bayesian game, uncertainty is internal and has structure. The opponent has a type. That type affects their payoff. You hold a prior—a probability over that type. Your strategy responds to your own type. Everyone is doing this at the same time. The model is not incomplete because of uncertainty; the uncertainty itself is part of what defines the model. Students mix up the statement: the player believes the type. But type is the reality; belief is the guess. The player knows their own type. The other player has a belief about what type the first player has. Conflating them breaks the model because you lose the distinction between what one person knows and what another person guesses. Students often slip into thinking of hidden information as just another source of randomness or uncertainty. But the tricky part is that the action—the price or offer—changes which types remain. The probability distribution after the action is not the same as before. Hidden information by itself is just hard to predict. But screening and adverse selection say: the prediction problem gets worse because your action filters out some types and leaves others.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that private information changes the object of strategy. A player no longer chooses just an action; the model often needs a rule mapping each type to an action.

Reader Work

Before solving, list the hidden types, who observes each type, and the prior probabilities. Then write a strategy as a rule for every type. The work is to stop using uncertainty as a vague excuse and put it into the model.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: rewrite a complete-information strategy as a type-contingent rule. Then identify the prior belief that lets other players reason about hidden types. If there is no type rule, the private-information structure is not yet modeled.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to Bayesian Nash applications: once types and priors are in the model, the next step is checking expected best responses for each type in concrete settings.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for private information, uncertain types, lotteries, or risk where the model must add up payoffs across multiple possible worlds weighted by beliefs. When you see a model or situation with hidden types, secret values, private costs, private information, asymmetric information, signals, or one player knowing something the others must guess at—that is Bayesian-game territory. Look for any situation where behavior depends on something one side knows but the other does not: a company's profit or strength, a worker's ability, a customer's willingness to pay, the condition of an asset, or a person's type or preference. That is where types and beliefs live. Look for situations where an offer, price, contract term, or test makes people sort themselves based on what they know about themselves. If changing the offer changes who accepts it, and if the people who accept have different hidden types from the people who reject, then screening and adverse selection are operating. Watch for cases where a rule meant to apply to an average instead pulls in a selected population.

Concepts In This Lecture

Expected Utility

Problem: You often must decide before you know what will happen: will a bid win or lose? Will a risky investment pay off or lose money? Will someone turn out to be honest or dishonest? You need a way to compare choices when the future is uncertain.

Mathematical handle: Expected utility is the number you get when you weight each possible payoff by its probability and sum them. It squeezes a risky situation down to one score so you can compare choices.

What breaks without it: Without expected utility, games where players have hidden information—like auctions or contract negotiations—would have no clear way to score an action. You could not reason about what a rational player would do when they do not know the full picture.

Bayesian Games

Problem: When you bid in an auction, you might know what an item is worth to you, but you do not know how much it is worth to your rivals. You must decide how much to bid, facing uncertainty about what they value. This is the core: strategic choice when you lack full information about who you are playing and what the other side cares about.

Mathematical handle: The object has four pieces: a set of possible hidden types (for each player), a prior—a probability for each type, a set of actions each type can take, and payoff functions that depend on actions and types. All four must work together to define the game.

What breaks without it: If you model every game as if everyone knew everything, auctions would collapse. You would treat a bidder who knows the item is worth 50 to them the same as a bidder unsure whether it is worth 20 or 80—and the model would not make sense. Signaling (like a job applicant proving they are skilled), bargaining when both sides hide their reservation prices, and most real-world contracts would all disappear from the model. The course would be describing a world that does not exist.

Types And Beliefs

Problem: When someone knows something you do not, you have to guess what they know and what they will do based on that hidden information. The problem is tracking both the secret and everyone's best guess about it in a way that explains behavior.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical object is a type space—the set of all possible private facts—paired with a probability distribution over types or histories for each player.

What breaks without it: Without types and beliefs, you cannot build a model where one side has secret information and the other side reacts to it. You could not explain why one bidder bids high and another low, or why a sender might lie and a receiver might not trust them. You would lose the ability to ask whether a guess can be updated or if lies can be caught.

Screening And Adverse Selection

Problem: Imagine you are setting a price or a rule for a deal you are offering. You think through what the average person would do, and you set your offer accordingly. But here is the catch: once people hear your offer, the ones who accept it are not the average anymore—they are the ones for whom your offer looked like a good choice. That means the people who show up are a filtered group, not the typical one you planned for.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical object is a payoff computed on a filtered group. You set a cutoff—a price, a contract clause, a rule—and only the types for whom accepting beats rejecting will take the offer. The types that accept form a new distribution, different from what you started with. Your payoff comes from serving only those accepted types, not the full population.

What breaks without it: Without this concept, the course would predict that whenever a buyer wants something more than the seller wants to keep it, they would simply agree on a price and trade. That is true when both sides know all the facts. But the concept shows why it is false when information is hidden: an offer attracts certain types and repels others, and the change in who accepts can destroy the gains from trade. The course would miss entire categories of real behavior—refusals to insure certain groups, haggling that breaks down, quality spirals in used-goods markets.
