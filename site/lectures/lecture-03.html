Lecture 3: Dominance

Introduces pruning: some strategies can be ruled out because another strategy does better across the relevant contingencies.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture introduces the first disciplined way to simplify a game. Instead of guessing what players believe, it asks whether one strategy is worse than another across the relevant possibilities and can therefore be removed.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as an inequality across contingencies. A strategy is dominated when another strategy gives at least as much payoff in every relevant case and more in some case.

The mathematical object has to exist because some choices can be rejected without forecasting the opponent. The dominance comparison formalizes “no matter what they do” as a set of inequalities across contingencies. That makes deletion a proof about payoffs rather than a guess about likely behavior.

Dominance Inequality

Worked Mini-Example

If one restaurant location earns less than another no matter what a rival does, the worse location can be removed before doing any equilibrium reasoning. The deletion comes from comparison, not intuition.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to delete strategies because they seem unlikely. Dominance is stronger: it requires a payoff comparison that survives all opponent actions covered by the model.

Students often get stuck by deleting strategies that are merely unattractive under their favorite prediction. That is not dominance. A strategy that is bad against one opponent move but useful against another is belief-dependent, not dominated.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for arguments that rule out actions before equilibrium is computed because one action is never a best answer.

Concepts In This Lecture

Dominance

Problem: The problem is that some choices are plainly bad no matter what the other side does. Keeping them in the analysis can distract from the strategic choices that actually need thought.

Mathematical handle: The object is a dominance comparison between two strategies across all possible actions by others.

What breaks without it: Without dominance, every listed strategy would look equally alive even when some choices have no defensible reason to be played. The analysis would be noisier and less disciplined before equilibrium reasoning even begins.
