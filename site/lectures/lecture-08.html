Lecture 8 · Time, Threats, And Credibility, Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 8: Backward Induction

Makes credibility calculable by solving later choices first and folding their values back into earlier decisions.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: should we solve a dynamic game by narrating it from the first move forward? The lecture says the last meaningful choice often determines what earlier moves really mean.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture reverses the natural storytelling order. Instead of starting at the first move, it solves the last decision first and uses that continuation value to judge earlier choices.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as recursive value assignment. At each decision node, the player chooses the branch with the best continuation payoff, and that value is passed backward through the tree.

A flat list of actions cannot encode time or information. It cannot say who moved first, what was observed, or which histories look identical to a player. The tree structure preserves those facts as explicit objects—nodes, edges, information sets, payoffs—so that later reasoning about credibility and belief updates has a home. The mathematical object has to exist because sequential choice creates a problem within a problem within a problem: the very last decision stands alone, but the second-to-last decision contains the last decision inside it, and the first decision contains all the rest. Without a notation for solved subtrees, you would have to carry every possible future story forward in your mind. A continuation value—a single number representing the best payoff from here onward—lets you replace an entire future subtree with one number. Without this object, the analyst would be stuck: you would have to write out every possible branch and every possible future to check whether a plan was credible. With continuation values, you just calculate once and reuse the answer. A strategy written down includes plans for futures that may never occur — branches of the tree the players hope to avoid. Those plans can look great on paper but be impossible to execute. Mathematics formalizes the requirement that every plan, even the ones hidden in branches that never happen, must still be a plan the player would choose if that branch actually arrived. Subgame perfection is the formal statement of this requirement. A known horizon changes incentives at every single period. The model must track how many rounds remain at each date so last-period logic can propagate backward. Finitely repeated games are not just one-shot games stacked in a list—they are a time-indexed strategic object where the endpoint shapes all earlier choices.

Worked Mini-Example

If a seller threatens to reject a future offer but would accept once that future node arrives, the threat should not shape earlier play. Solving backward exposes that problem.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to believe every announced future action. Backward induction asks whether the action will still be optimal when the future decision actually has to be made.

The tempting shortcut is to take announced threats and promises at face value. That fails because a future action matters only if the player will still want to take it when that future moment arrives.

Students often assume that a tree automatically means every player saw every earlier move. That is wrong. Information sets are the remedy. An information set groups nodes together because a player at those nodes cannot tell them apart — they might be at node A or node B, but they do not know which. So they must choose the same move at both. Students often get stuck by trying to read the tree the way a player would actually experience it—forward in time. The player is born at the start and encounters choices one by one, so it feels natural to solve from the start forward. But the analyst solving the game cannot do that because the analyst would have to guess what the future players will do. Backward induction flips the order: you solve from the end backward because the end is the only place where there is no ambiguity. Later incentives determine which earlier expectations are credible. Once you know what someone will definitely do at the end, you can work backward with certainty. Students get tripped up because they only check whether people want to deviate at the start. They ignore paths that 'should not happen' in equilibrium. But threats only work by affecting what players expect to happen if they deviate. Those off-path scenarios are where hollow threats hide, so the course forces you to look. Students count rounds and think more rounds mean more cooperation. They miss the question: at each date, does the relationship have a strategic future? A game ending at a known date is not the same as an ongoing relationship. The trap is confusing repetition with future consequences.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that time turns prediction into a recursion. Later optimal choices become continuation values, and those values are folded backward until earlier players can see the real consequences of moving now.

Reader Work

Start from the last decision nodes and write the action the future player will actually want there. Replace those nodes with values before moving earlier. The work is to make present threats answer to future incentives instead of narrative force.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: identify a threat in the lecture and solve the node where that threat would be executed. If the threatened action is not optimal there, explain how the earlier story changes. This is the core credibility move.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to negotiation: bargaining is a dynamic problem where offers, refusals, patience, and outside options all depend on what happens after today's choice.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for any situation where the order of moves changes what a person would do, or where a player does not have full information about what happened before. Look for threats or promises that depend on what happens later. Look for a claim about strategy or a prediction that assumes simultaneous moves when the actual sequence was different. Look for finite sequential games—situations where people move one at a time for a known number of steps and then the game ends. If you can identify the very last move and solve it, and if the player moving earlier can predict the later move, backward induction applies. Any situation where you need to check whether a threat is credible by asking will the person actually do it when they face that choice is a place where backward induction helps you think. Watch for any strategic situation where someone makes a threat or promise, and ask: would they actually follow through? Watch for games that play out in stages. Watch for plans that include moves 'just in case' on branches that should not happen. In all these cases, subgame perfection is the question: would the plan hold up even if that supposedly-impossible branch showed up? Look for settings where an endpoint is known and trace the logic backward. Watch for situations where a final date might undo the trust that would otherwise exist in a relationship.

Concepts In This Lecture

Extensive Form Games

Problem: In real conflicts, people move one at a time, not all at once. A choice made later can depend on what happened earlier. And sometimes you know more than the other person — or they know more than you. A flat table misses all of that.

Mathematical handle: The object is a tree: starting point, branches for each choice, nodes where decisions happen, a record of what happened so far, sets of nodes that a player cannot tell apart (because they do not know which one they are in), and numbers at the endpoints showing each player's score.

What breaks without it: Without a tree, you would write down all actions in rows and columns, like a table. But that table pretends everyone moved at the same time and saw nothing. It would hide whether a threat is really believable, whether later moves were made with information about earlier ones, and whether a player could distinguish between different situations.

Backward Induction

Problem: When one person moves today and another person moves tomorrow, today's person is trying to guess what tomorrow's person will do—but people often say they'll do something harsh later, then don't do it when the moment comes. You need a way to figure out which future threats are real and which are bluffing.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical object is a game tree: a diagram with nodes (decision points), branches (the choices available), and leaves (the final outcomes with scores). Backward induction assigns a strategic value to each node by starting at the leaves, figuring out what each player will choose at the nodes just before the leaves, and then replacing those pieces with their values, stepping back one layer at a time until the whole tree is solved.

What breaks without it: Without backward induction, a game tree—a picture showing every possible history and every choice that could be made—is just a picture. You can draw all the paths, but you have no way to tell which ones will actually happen and which are empty stories. You could watch a player write down a plan (I will do A now and then B later) and have no method to check whether that plan makes sense when the later moment arrives. You would accept threats that nobody would carry out.

Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

Problem: Sometimes a player says 'I will do something painful to punish you.' That threat only works if the player would actually carry it out if the moment came. But if the painful thing hurts the player too, they might not do it. A plan built on such a hollow threat falls apart once you realize the threat is fake.

Mathematical handle: Start with a Nash equilibrium — a plan where nobody wants to switch at the beginning. Now make a stronger requirement: that same plan must also be a Nash equilibrium inside every 'subgame' — every piece of the game tree that can stand on its own as a complete game at some later point. If a plan fails this test anywhere, reject it.

What breaks without it: Without it, you can build strategies on promises you would never keep. A player might say 'if you enter, I will fight back hard' — but if fighting back after entry would lose them money, they would never actually do it. The equilibrium survives only because it hides this broken promise.

Open concept page

Finitely Repeated Games

Problem: Does playing something several times instead of once change how people act? Does knowing when it will end change the answer?

Mathematical handle: A stage game (the single game people play) repeated T times, where T is known to everyone and is a fixed number.

What breaks without it: Without studying finite repetition, people mistakenly think repetition always builds trust. The finite case forces the question: what if everyone knows when the relationship ends? That question reveals why the endpoint is strategically powerful.
