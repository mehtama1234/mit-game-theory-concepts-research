Lecture 2: Representation of Games

Turns social situations into formal objects: players, histories, strategies, strategy profiles, payoffs, and the difference between trees and compact strategic form.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture asks how a messy interaction becomes an object that can be solved. It separates players, actions, strategies, histories, information, and payoffs, then shows why tables and trees reveal different parts of the same strategic situation.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through the game form. A strategic-form table compresses simultaneous choices; an extensive-form tree exposes order, histories, and information. The representation chooses which constraints the later solution concept can see.

The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary language can hide contradictions. People can say they like many things, but a model needs to know whether those comparisons can be used together. A preference relation is the smallest formal object that lets the course ask whether the choices are complete enough and consistent enough to support later payoff reasoning. The mathematical object has to exist because tables and equations cannot operate directly on vague rankings. Utility turns the ranking into a number while preserving the choice information the model needs. It is the handle that lets the course move from “preferred” to “has the larger payoff in this calculation.” The mathematical object has to exist because strategic dependence needs a container. If each payoff depends on everyone’s action, the model needs a way to list the whole action profile and the payoff consequences together. Strategic form is the simplest object that keeps those dependencies visible and checkable. The mathematical object has to exist because time and information change what an action means. A flat list of actions cannot say who moved first, what was observed, or which histories a player can distinguish. The tree structure preserves those facts so later credibility and belief questions have a place to attach. The mathematical object has to exist because sequential choice has nested future problems inside current decisions. A continuation value lets the solved future replace a whole subtree. Without that object, the analyst would have to carry every possible future story instead of using the later incentive calculation. The mathematical object has to exist because hidden information changes the identity of the decision problem. Types and priors let the model represent what each player knows privately and what others believe. Without them, private values and costs would be invisible to the equilibrium calculation. The mathematical object has to exist because hidden types need their own stability checks. A single action per player is not enough; each possible type needs a plan, and each plan must be optimal in expectation. Bayesian Nash equilibrium formalizes mutual consistency across those type-indexed plans.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to think representation is bookkeeping. It is a modeling claim. If the form hides timing or information, the equilibrium calculation can be exact and still answer the wrong question.

Students often get stuck by asking whether the preference is realistic for every human mood. The course is doing a modeling move: it asks whether the relevant choices in the problem can be treated as stable enough for strategic analysis. Students often get stuck by treating utility points like dollars or happiness units. Sometimes dollars enter utility, but utility itself is the model’s choice index, not necessarily a literal commodity. Students often get stuck by reading a row as if it has a single value. A row has several consequences, one for each possible opponent action, so a strategy cannot be judged until the contingency is named. Students often get stuck by assuming a tree automatically means everyone observed every earlier move. Information sets matter because the same physical history and the same known history are not always the same thing. Students often get stuck by reading the tree chronologically. Players live forward, but the analyst solves backward because later incentives determine which earlier expectations are credible. Students often get stuck by treating uncertainty as noise outside the model. In a Bayesian game, uncertainty is inside the model and has named objects: types, beliefs, strategies, and payoffs. Students often get stuck by checking only the average player. Bayesian Nash is stricter: a proposed strategy can fail because one particular type wants to deviate, even if the pooled behavior looks reasonable.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for the place where an economic model has to say what an actor counts as better before any strategic reasoning begins. Look for a paper turning goals, payoffs, welfare, or preferences into a number that can be compared or optimized. Look for simultaneous moves, payoff matrices, or models where the exact timing is suppressed so strategic dependence can be seen cleanly. Look for histories, decision nodes, information sets, or any claim where the order of moves changes what behavior can be believed. Look for finite sequential games where the last move can be solved and folded back into earlier choices. Look for models with types, priors, private values, costs, signals, or asymmetric information. Look for type-contingent strategies and expected-payoff best response conditions.

Concepts In This Lecture

Preferences

Problem: The problem is that before we talk about strategy, we need a way to say what one person is trying to get. A person may like one outcome more than another even when dollars, risk, effort, and timing are mixed together.

Mathematical handle: The object is a preference relation: a comparison rule over outcomes. When it is complete the person can compare any two outcomes; when it is transitive the comparisons do not cycle back on themselves.

What breaks without it: Without preferences, a payoff table has no disciplined source. The analyst could assign numbers, but there would be no explanation of why a player ranks one result over another or why a later best-response calculation should be trusted.

Open concept page

Utility

Problem: The problem is that rankings are awkward to compute with. A payoff table, expected value, or equilibrium check needs numbers, not only verbal statements like this outcome is preferred.

Mathematical handle: The object is a utility function u that assigns a number to each outcome while preserving the preference ordering. Higher utility means higher in the ranking, not necessarily more money or happiness.

What breaks without it: Without utility, later algebra would float free from choice behavior. The course could say one outcome is preferred, but it could not easily compute expected payoffs, best responses, or surplus divisions.

Open concept page

Strategic Form Games

Problem: The problem is representing a situation where everyone chooses without first seeing the others' choices, yet everyone's payoff depends on the whole combination.

Mathematical handle: The object is a normal-form game: players, action sets, and payoff functions collected into a table or payoff map.

What breaks without it: Without strategic form, simultaneous games would remain verbal scenarios. Students could argue about incentives, but they would lack the structured object needed to compare rows, columns, and deviations cleanly.

Open concept page

Extensive Form Games

Problem: The problem is that some games unfold over time. A later choice can depend on an earlier choice, and some players may know more about the history than others.

Mathematical handle: The object is a game tree: decision nodes, branches, histories, information sets, and terminal payoffs.

What breaks without it: Without extensive form, the course could mistake a sequential conflict for a simultaneous one. That would hide credibility, information sets, and the fact that later choices can make earlier announcements meaningful or empty.

Open concept page

Backward Induction

Problem: The problem is that early choices in a tree depend on what later players will actually do, not what they announce they might do.

Mathematical handle: The object is a game tree with continuation values assigned from the terminal nodes backward.

What breaks without it: Without backward induction, a game tree would be a picture of possible histories but not a method for evaluating them. The model could accept plans that later players would abandon once their decision node is reached.

Open concept page

Bayesian Games

Problem: The problem is strategic choice when players do not know exactly who they are facing or what payoffs the other side has.

Mathematical handle: The object is a game with types, priors over types, actions for each type, and payoff functions that may depend on type.

What breaks without it: Without Bayesian games, the course would treat hidden values, costs, and motives as if everyone knew them. Auctions, signaling, and incomplete-information bargaining would collapse into misleading complete-information models.

Open concept page

Bayesian Nash Equilibrium

Problem: The problem is predicting behavior when each possible type of a player may want a different action.

Mathematical handle: The object is a strategy for every type of every player, where each type maximizes expected payoff given beliefs about other types and their strategies.

What breaks without it: Without Bayesian Nash, the course could define types and beliefs but not check whether type-contingent strategies hold together. A proposed bidding or action rule might fail for one hidden type even if it sounds plausible overall.
