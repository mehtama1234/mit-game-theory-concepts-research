Lecture 19: Revenue Equivalence

Shows when different auction formats produce the same expected revenue because strategic bid adjustments offset rule differences.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture asks when different auction formats become equivalent from the seller's revenue perspective. It teaches that surface rules can differ while expected payments line up under strong assumptions.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through expected payment functions and allocation probabilities. Under the theorem's conditions, payment differences are pinned down by the same allocation rule and boundary condition.

The mathematical object has to exist because comparing auction formats requires more than comparing labels. Expected revenue depends on allocation probabilities, payment rules, and equilibrium bidding. Revenue equivalence formalizes the conditions under which those pieces line up across mechanisms.

Worked Mini-Example

A first-price auction and second-price auction feel different to bidders, but if the same type wins with the same probability and assumptions hold, expected revenue can match.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to remember only the equivalence. The theorem is also a list of conditions; changing risk attitudes, values, symmetry, or allocation rules can break it.

Students often get stuck by comparing the rules while holding bids fixed. Equilibrium bids change with the auction format, and those strategic adjustments are exactly what the theorem accounts for.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for auction comparisons where allocation sameness and bidder payoff normalization drive payment conclusions.

Concepts In This Lecture

Revenue Equivalence

Problem: The problem is comparing auction formats that look different: first-price, second-price, English, and others may create different bidding behavior.

Mathematical handle: The object is a theorem linking allocation rules and expected payments under assumptions such as risk neutrality, independent private values, and symmetric bidders.

What breaks without it: Without revenue equivalence, every auction comparison would be treated as an isolated case. The course would miss the deeper structure that makes some formats equivalent and the assumptions that make equivalence fragile.
