Lecture 5 · Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency, Time, Threats, And Credibility

Lecture 5: Nash Equilibrium

Closes the best-response loop: every player must be choosing optimally given the others' choices.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: does equilibrium mean everyone gets what they want? The lecture redirects the word equilibrium toward stability against one-person deviations, not happiness, fairness, or social success.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture closes the loop created by best responses. A proposed outcome must survive each player asking, given what the others do, whether changing alone would improve their payoff.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as mutual best response. For every player, the chosen action or strategy must lie in that player's best-response set against the other players' choices.

Mathematics must exist because words alone cannot track the condition. When you say an action is best, the best-response set stores the answer in a clear form: these are the actions that score highest when the opponent does that specific thing. The set is reusable. You can take it, plug it into a Nash equilibrium check, or use it in an auction mechanism. It keeps the condition visible. Strategic prediction is circular. Each player's best move depends on what others do, which depends on what each of them expects, which depends on what everyone expects the first player to do. The model needs a fixed point where all these expectations and moves fit together. Nash equilibrium is that fixed point—it is the formal answer to the question, "What profile survives the logical loop of mutual best response?" The mathematical object must exist because pure actions do not always allow stable predictions. A probability distribution over actions lets you control your own predictability. The distribution becomes your strategy because it decides your opponent's expected payoffs before the random roll happens. The math must exist because exact opposition creates a special structure. When one player's payoff is the negative of the other's, the model collapses two separate goals into one number—the value of the game—seen from opposite sides. That is why security, protection, and minimax—finding the safest move—become the right tools. In games where both sides can gain or lose together, those tools would overlook crucial possibilities. The mathematical object has to exist because dynamic games create huge spaces of possible strategies. A person's strategy in a game lasting many turns says what to do after countless different things that might happen — that is a massive object to write down and compare. You need a theorem that reduces the verification problem: instead of comparing everything, compare only one-shot deviations at each history. Without this theorem, verifying optimality in dynamic games by hand would be impossible for anything but tiny examples. The theorem makes the course teachable.

Worked Mini-Example

Two firms may each like a different price in isolation. A Nash check asks whether either firm would cut or raise price after seeing the rival's chosen price held fixed.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to read equilibrium as good, fair, or socially desirable. Nash only says no single player has a profitable one-person deviation.

The tempting shortcut is to pick the outcome with the largest total payoff or the most natural story. That fails because a high-surplus outcome can fall apart if one player can improve while everyone else holds still.

A trap is to discuss an action and forget what it is a best response to. You might say cutting price is good, but good against whom? If the rival cut first, cutting is best. If the rival did not cut, staying high might be better. The student has to train themselves to always ask: best response to what? A common trap is to confuse Nash equilibrium with a good outcome or a balanced state. Students hear equilibrium and imagine something stable and fair. But a Nash equilibrium can be the worst outcome available, unfair, or wasteful. It only guarantees one narrow thing: no single player can unilaterally improve. Do not expect it to be nice. Students ask: what does the player actually choose? The answer: the player chooses the distribution itself. Once the random device rolls—once an action happens—the distribution was already the commitment. The mix is the strategy, not a mistake. The trap: students call every rivalry zero-sum. But a price-cutting company might survive while a competitor exits, yet both could have done better by not cutting. A legal dispute costs both sides money; both might prefer settlement. Zero-sum means payoffs move in exact opposition, not just that one side wants to beat the other. Students often think the principle is automatic — usable everywhere — and forget that it depends on the game's structure. They memorize the shortcut but skip verifying that the assumptions (finite horizon, or constant discounting) actually hold in the game at hand. Then they apply it to a game with externalities or state-dependent payoffs and get a wrong answer. The principle is not free; it is only good when the structure justifies it.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that Nash equilibrium is a consistency check. Each player's action must be a best response to the others, so the prediction is not a wish; it is a point where unilateral regret has disappeared.

Reader Work

Take every proposed outcome and run a one-player deviation audit. Freeze everyone else, test one player, then move to the next player. The work is to stop asking whether the outcome feels good and ask whether unilateral regret remains.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: reject one tempting outcome by identifying exactly one profitable unilateral deviation. Then find one outcome where that same one-player switch test fails for every player. This separates Nash stability from efficiency or moral appeal.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to imperfect competition: the best-response loop becomes concrete when firms choose prices or quantities and each firm's profit depends on what rivals do.

How To Recognize This Later

When you read a paper or a new game, look for the step where someone says if player A does this, then player B should do that. Those words signal a best response. Look for reaction functions (curves showing what one player should do in response to another). Look for optimization steps that start with given. Whenever you see given, a best response is hiding inside. In new situations, look for a pattern of behavior where no single participant can improve their own outcome by changing their own move alone, given that everyone else keeps doing what they are doing. When you spot that pattern, you have identified a candidate Nash equilibrium. Look for indifference equations—places where the other player has no reason to prefer one response to another. Look for randomized play or models where any predictable behavior would be punished by someone who sees it. When you see a model where my score is always the negative of yours—my gain is your loss, my loss is your gain—you have found a zero-sum game. Spot these by checking: does one player's best outcome match the other player's worst outcome? Does cooperation create no new value? Then the tools of zero-sum games apply. When you see a proof that verifies a strategy in a dynamic game, look for whether it replaces a full strategy comparison with a finite list of one-shot deviations. If an author writes, 'Suppose player i deviates at history h by choosing action a' instead of a, holding the rest of the plan fixed, then...' and uses that to conclude the strategy is optimal, they are using this principle. It is a signature move in repeated games and dynamic settings.

Concepts In This Lecture

Best Response

Problem: When you are trying to decide what to do, you cannot just pick any move and say it is 'good.' A move that works beautifully against one person's choice can fail miserably against another. The same action is good or bad depending on what the other person does.

Mathematical handle: The best-response set is a collection: all the actions that maximize a player's payoff when everyone else's actions (or the player's beliefs about those actions) are pinned down. It stores the answer to the question: given that they do this, which of my moves is best?

What breaks without it: Without best response, there is no way to test whether an equilibrium is real. You could not check whether two strategies are stable against each other, you could not verify auction bids, you could not build mixed strategies, and you could not plan what to do in situations that have already partially unfolded.

Nash Equilibrium

Problem: You are trying to predict what a group of people will do when they are all trying to outthink each other. The problem is: your prediction only works if, after each person sees what you said the others would do, that person does not regret their own choice and decide to change.

Mathematical handle: The object is a profile—a list of each player's move—where every player's move is a best response to the others' moves. You can write it as a tuple or a list, but the key is that each component meets the best-response test.

What breaks without it: Without the Nash test, any outcome could be defended just by naming it. Someone could propose a pattern and offer no reason why each person would stick to it. Nash forces the question: if everyone else follows this pattern, does each individual want to follow it too?

Mixed Strategies

Problem: You face a jam: if you always pick one move, the other player learns this and beats you every time. But there might not be any single move you can stick to that is safe. So you need a way to stay unpredictable while still being smart about it.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical object is a probability distribution: a list of your pure moves, each with a number between 0 and 1 (the chance you pick it), and they add up to 1. Often, the equilibrium distribution makes your opponent indifferent—unable to gain by switching between the moves they use.

What breaks without it: The course would wrongly claim some games have no stable answer just because players cannot both pick fixed pure moves and stand still. It would also miss that randomness can be a choice, not just noise or confusion.

Zero-Sum Games

Problem: Pure conflict: one side's success means the other side's failure. Your win is my loss. There is no possibility to both do better together because the score is fixed and divides between us.

Mathematical handle: The object is a payoff function where player two's score is the negative of player one's score. When player one gets 100, player two gets -100. Their interests are perfectly opposite, locked together by the game's structure.

What breaks without it: Without recognizing zero-sum games, a student might apply methods from games where both players can gain together to games where they cannot. A poker hand is zero-sum; a business partnership is not. You need to know the difference or you will use the wrong tools.

One-Shot Deviation Principle

Problem: Imagine you wrote down a complete plan for how to behave in a game that plays out over many moments — what you'd do after every possible thing that could happen. Checking whether someone would actually want to stick to that plan sounds simple: does anyone want to cheat? But there are so many moments and so many ways to cheat that checking all of them seems impossible.

Mathematical handle: The object is a local change: you pick one decision point in the game, try a different action at that moment, and ask whether that one change (while everything else stays the same) improves the score. Formally: replace action a with action a' at history h, hold the rest of the plan fixed, and check if the new score is higher.

What breaks without it: If you do not have this shortcut, you face a choice: either skip checking whether people want to cheat (which is not safe), or write down every possible alternative plan and score them (which takes forever for games that last many turns). For a game where your choice today affects the scores available tomorrow, and tomorrow's scores affect the day after, the number of plans to check grows so fast that you cannot do it by hand.
