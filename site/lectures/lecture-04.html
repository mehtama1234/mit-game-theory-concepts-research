Lecture 4 · Strategic Dependence, Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency, Communication And Knowledge

Lecture 4: Rationalizability

Adds beliefs to pruning. A strategy can remain plausible if some coherent belief about others makes it a best response.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: if an action is not dominated, should we predict it? The lecture says survival is weaker than prediction because an action may be defensible under some belief without being part of a mutually consistent outcome.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture relaxes pure deletion and asks which strategies can be justified by some coherent belief about others. It turns the question from 'is this always bad?' to 'could this be optimal under a defensible belief?'

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through best responses to beliefs. A strategy survives if there is a belief over others' actions that makes it payoff-maximizing for the player.

Math exists here because some moves can be thrown out by proof, not by guess. Dominance formalizes 'no matter what the opponent does' as a list of inequalities. Each inequality is a check against one opponent move. If all the inequalities pass, you have a mathematical fact that the move is dominated, and no amount of belief-changing will fix it. The mathematical object exists because rationality can nest. Once you eliminate one choice as unreasonable, the remaining game changes, and what was reasonable before might become unreasonable now. Iterated deletion is the formal procedure that tracks this shrinking action set carefully and systematically, rather than relying on informal intuition about multiple rounds of thinking. It forces you to be precise about what is eliminated and why, and what that elimination means for the next round. Because plausibility depends on belief. A move can look odd from outside and still be rational for someone who expects opponents to behave differently. Normal language tends to lump surprising, wrong, irrational, and off-the-equilibrium-path into one bucket. Rationalizability separates them. The math must exist because the question—does there exist a probability distribution over surviving opponent strategies such that this action maximizes expected payoff—requires checking an infinite space of possible beliefs. Ordinary reasoning cannot track this precisely. The math asks for existence: there must be at least one coherent belief that supports the move. The belief does not have to be the analyst's best guess or shared by everyone. It just has to be coherent enough to make the action optimal. Mathematics must exist because words alone cannot track the condition. When you say an action is best, the best-response set stores the answer in a clear form: these are the actions that score highest when the opponent does that specific thing. The set is reusable. You can take it, plug it into a Nash equilibrium check, or use it in an auction mechanism. It keeps the condition visible. English hides the levels. When you say 'everyone knows', you could mean: each person knows it separately; each knows that the other knows it; or the fact is public in a way that supports unlimited reasoning by all parties. The math notation with repeated K's forces those meanings apart. It lets the analyst ask whether an equilibrium depends only on the fact itself or on the public assurance that everyone is reasoning from the shared fact.

Worked Mini-Example

A move that is not dominant may still be rationalizable if a player reasonably expects the rival to choose a particular action. The model asks whether some belief can make the move optimal.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to confuse rationalizable with predicted. Rationalizability says a move can be justified; equilibrium will later require everyone's justifications to fit together.

The tempting shortcut is to confuse 'could be rational' with 'will happen.' That fails because each player's private justification can point in directions that do not fit together across the whole game.

A common trap is deleting a move because it performs badly when you guess the opponent will do something specific. That is not dominance. If the move is bad only against one opponent choice but good against others, it is not dominated — you just dislike it under your own prediction of what the opponent will do. Students often gloss over whether the game uses strict or weak dominance and whether the order of deletions matters. These are not details—with weak dominance especially, different deletion orders can leave different strategies alive, so the lecture distinction between strict and weak is crucial. Saying 'we deleted' is not enough; you need to know which deletion rule was applied and what remained. Students often get stuck because rationalizability feels permissive—like anything goes. It is permissive on purpose: it widens the net before you lock down predictions. But it is not empty. A strategy still has to be a best response to some belief. You cannot just claim a move is rational because someone somewhere might pick it. It has to be optimal under that belief, which means it must yield a higher expected payoff than the alternative moves. A trap is to discuss an action and forget what it is a best response to. You might say cutting price is good, but good against whom? If the rival cut first, cutting is best. If the rival did not cut, staying high might be better. The student has to train themselves to always ask: best response to what? The infinite chain sounds like philosophy, so students dismiss it. But the course uses it for concrete results: people act one way when a fact is public and another way when only privately known. The chain is the bridge between the two.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that beliefs are part of rational choice. A strategy is rationalizable when there is some belief about others that makes it a best response, so the model separates individual defensibility from collective stability.

Reader Work

Pick one action that looks odd and try to rescue it with a belief about the other player. If no belief makes it optimal, identify the payoff comparison that blocks it. The work is to distinguish individual defensibility from final prediction.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: defend an action that is not your favorite prediction by constructing a belief that makes it optimal. Then state why that defense still falls short of equilibrium. This checks whether you understand survival under belief as weaker than mutual consistency.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to Nash equilibrium: once individual strategies can be justified by beliefs, the next demand is that those justifications close into one consistent best-response loop.

How To Recognize This Later

When you see an argument that crosses off a move before computing equilibrium — because one move always loses to another — you are seeing dominance at work. Look for elimination arguments that remove options in stages, where each stage uses the assumption that others are rational. When you see 'a player would not do X because that would only make sense if an opponent did Y, but the opponent would not do Y,' you are watching iterated reasoning. Find patterns where an action is ruled out based on what remains after earlier eliminations, not just a one-time comparison. Watch for models that separate clearly impossible behavior—strategies that are never best responses to any belief—from behavior that is one of several plausible responses under different expectations. Any analysis that asks whether a strategy could be justified by some coherent belief is invoking rationalizability. When you read a paper or a new game, look for the step where someone says if player A does this, then player B should do that. Those words signal a best response. Look for reaction functions (curves showing what one player should do in response to another). Look for optimization steps that start with given. Whenever you see given, a best response is hiding inside. Look for coordination failures, public announcements, or arguments that rest on what everyone knows about what everyone else knows about the world and about each other's knowledge.

Concepts In This Lecture

Dominance

Problem: In any game, some moves are just worse. They lose to another move no matter what the other person tries. But if you list all possible moves, the bad ones can hide the real choices that matter. Dominance lets you cross off the hopeless ones first.

Mathematical handle: The object is a pair of two moves, where one move wins against the other no matter what the opponent does. More formally: you can represent it as a relation, a set of ordered pairs (A, B) where move A dominates move B. To check if it works, look at your payoff table row by row (or column by column if you are the one being compared). If every number in row A is at least as large as the corresponding number in row B, and at least one number is strictly larger, then A dominates B. You can mark these pairs on the game matrix to see at a glance which moves are dead.

What breaks without it: If you keep every possible move alive, the board stays cluttered. Some moves have no reason to exist, but they still sit in the analysis like noise. Without dominance, you would not know which moves to ignore first, so finding the real strategic solution gets harder before you ever reach concepts like best response.

Iterated Deletion

Problem: When you remove one bad move, the game changes—what was once a decent move might now be a bad move in the smaller game. This keeps happening in waves: you delete, the game shrinks, new bad moves appear, you delete again.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical object is a game that gets smaller with each step—you start with all possible strategies, remove the dominated ones, and create a reduced game with only the surviving strategies, then repeat the dominance check on this reduced version.

What breaks without it: Without iterated deletion, you miss how reasoning about one round changes what's reasonable in the next round. Some games look very complicated until you remove the obviously bad moves—then the pattern of what makes sense becomes clearer. You'd be stuck looking at all possible choices at once instead of understanding how rationality can narrow them down step by step.

Open concept page

Rationalizability

Problem: Deciding which strategies make sense to consider without assuming everyone has reached one locked-in agreement about what will happen. You need a test that catches strategies people might reasonably choose before you check whether all their choices can fit together perfectly.

Mathematical handle: A set—a list—of strategies where each surviving strategy has the property that it can be a best response to some belief about which of the other surviving strategies the other players will use. You build this set step by step: keep only strategies that are best responses to some probability distribution over the remaining strategies.

What breaks without it: Without it, students jump straight from strategies that are never good to Nash equilibrium strategies—skipping the middle ground. They miss that a strategy can survive because one player could hold a coherent belief that makes it their best move, even if that belief turns out not to match everyone else's choices in the final prediction.

Open concept page

Best Response

Problem: When you are trying to decide what to do, you cannot just pick any move and say it is 'good.' A move that works beautifully against one person's choice can fail miserably against another. The same action is good or bad depending on what the other person does.

Mathematical handle: The best-response set is a collection: all the actions that maximize a player's payoff when everyone else's actions (or the player's beliefs about those actions) are pinned down. It stores the answer to the question: given that they do this, which of my moves is best?

What breaks without it: Without best response, there is no way to test whether an equilibrium is real. You could not check whether two strategies are stable against each other, you could not verify auction bids, you could not build mixed strategies, and you could not plan what to do in situations that have already partially unfolded.

Open concept page

Common Knowledge

Problem: Coordinating action sometimes needs more than just each person knowing a fact alone. People also need to know that others know it, and know that others know that others know it. Without that chain, people hesitate even though each one has the information.

Mathematical handle: An infinite hierarchy: the event is known; then everyone knows the event is known; then everyone knows that everyone knows the event is known; and this nesting continues with no end.

What breaks without it: Without it, the course would miss what stops coordination. Players might all know the same event, yet each might hold back because they are unsure whether others know too, or whether others know that they know. That doubt is a real barrier.
