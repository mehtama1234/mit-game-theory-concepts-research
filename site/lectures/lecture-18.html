Lecture 18 · Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Lecture 18: Auctions

Turns private values into strategic bids under allocation and payment rules.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: should a bidder simply bid what the object is worth to them? The lecture shows that the payment rule decides whether truth, shading, or distortion is strategically sensible.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture turns private values into strategic bids. Allocation and payment rules shape whether bidders reveal, shade, or distort their values, so the rules themselves become the object of analysis.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through allocation rules, payment rules, values, bids, and expected payoffs. A bidder compares the chance of winning with the surplus left after payment.

Allocation and payment are linked. If I bid high, I am more likely to win but I also might pay more. My payoff depends on both. I cannot analyze my choice unless I model: the values everyone has, the bid space, the allocation rule, the payment rule, my beliefs about others, and how payoffs work. Without all of these pieces, you cannot tell if someone bid their truth, shaded their value, or distorted a bid to manipulate what they believe others will do. Comparing auction formats is not a matter of opinion or intuition — it requires mathematics because the answer depends on how allocation, strategy, and payment timing interact. Revenue equivalence formalizes the conditions under which those pieces produce the same seller payoff across mechanisms. Without the theorem, you would have only case-by-case reasoning and no systematic principle.

Worked Mini-Example

In a first-price auction, bidding your full value may win but leaves no surplus. The bidder shades the bid because winning is useful only when the price is below value.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to think bids are transparent reports of value. A bid is a strategic action created by the auction format's payment consequences.

The tempting shortcut is to read bids as honest value labels. That fails because bids are actions chosen under rules; the same bidder may bid differently in first-price, second-price, or platform auctions.

A trap is thinking of bids as if they are confessions of value. They are not. Bids are actions that respond to incentives. The payment rule, the belief about what others will do, and the risk of losing all shape the bid. The same bidder will bid differently under different rules. Students often get stuck by comparing auction rules while pretending bidders use the same strategy in both formats. That is not how auctions work. Bidders change their bids when the rule changes. The theorem accounts for those strategic adjustments — it is not a comparison of fixed bids. Forgetting this leads to the false conclusion that different payment rules automatically create different revenues.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that mechanism rules manufacture incentives. Allocation and payment rules determine how private values become strategic bids and whether the bidder wants to reveal or conceal information.

Reader Work

Split each bid into chance of winning, payment if winning, and value if winning. Then change the auction format and repeat the split. The work is to see a bid as a strategic response to rules, not as a transparent value label.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: compare two bids by writing how each changes winning probability and surplus if winning. Then repeat after changing the payment rule. If the same bid logic survives every format, you are probably ignoring the mechanism. Name whether the bidder pays their own bid, another bid, or a platform price.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to revenue equivalence: after comparing auction formats, the next question is when different rules nevertheless produce the same expected revenue.

How To Recognize This Later

When you see a system where someone has private information and a rule converts their choice into an outcome and a payment, you are seeing an auction. Look for auction comparisons where the author concludes that two mechanisms produce the same revenue. Check whether allocation is identical across formats and whether bidder payoffs are normalized to be the same at the lowest types. If those conditions hold and the assumptions match (risk-neutral, independent private values, symmetric bidders), revenue equivalence is likely at work.

Concepts In This Lecture

Auctions

Problem: Suppose you want to sell your bicycle. Buyers know their own willingness to pay, but you do not. Also, each buyer does not know if someone else values the bike more. How do you run the sale so buyers bid honestly and you get a fair price?

Mathematical handle: An auction is described by four things: the values each bidder privately holds, the range of bids each bidder can make, the rule that decides who wins for each set of bids, and the rule that decides how much the winner pays.

What breaks without it: If we skip auctions, we miss the most direct example of how to take hidden information and turn it into visible bids through smart rule design. We also could not explain why bidders do not bid their true value in all auction types, or why different payment rules lead to different bids from the same person, or why some auction designs encourage honesty while others encourage bluffing.

Revenue Equivalence

Problem: You need to sell something. You could ask bidders to write down their best offer and pay what they bid (first-price). Or you could ask them to write down their best offer but then pay what the second-highest bidder bid (second-price). These rules look completely opposite. Do they produce different results, or can they actually be equivalent?

Mathematical handle: The object is a theorem that links the way objects are allocated to the payments bidders expect to make. It depends on three core assumptions: bidders are risk-neutral (they care only about the size of their payoff, not how uncertain it is), each bidder knows their own value privately and nobody else learns it, and all bidders are treated symmetrically by the auction rules. Under these assumptions, expected revenue can be the same across different auction formats.

What breaks without it: Without revenue equivalence, you would have to evaluate each auction type in isolation, comparing first-price to second-price to English to open-ascending one pair at a time with no systematic tool. You would miss the deeper structure that tells you which assumptions make formats equivalent and which breaks them. The course would become a memorization list instead of a set of principles.
