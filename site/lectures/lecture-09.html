Lecture 9: Negotiation

Treats bargaining as strategic surplus division shaped by offers, rejection, timing, patience, and outside options.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture treats bargaining as surplus division under timing pressure. Offers, rejection, patience, and outside options are not personality traits; they become payoff consequences inside a dynamic game.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through continuation values and discounting. Waiting can shrink payoffs, so an offer is judged against what each side expects from rejecting and continuing.

The mathematical object has to exist because surplus division depends on strategic alternatives, not only on the size of the surplus. Offers, rejection payoffs, delay costs, and outside options must be represented together. Bargaining models create that object so division can be explained by incentives rather than asserted fairness. The mathematical object has to exist because ongoing relationships make future consequences part of current payoff. The discount factor and continuation payoff let the course compare today’s temptation with tomorrow’s punishment. Without that object, “the future matters” would remain a slogan rather than an inequality.

Worked Mini-Example

A buyer and seller can split value today or wait for another offer tomorrow. If waiting is costly, the side that can credibly wait longer may receive a larger share.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to explain bargaining power as confidence or stubbornness. The model asks what each side can get by refusing now and moving to the next bargaining stage.

Students often get stuck by assuming the fair split is the predicted split. Game theory asks what division survives incentives, not what division sounds fair from outside the model. Students often get stuck by thinking infinite repetition guarantees cooperation. If players are impatient, cannot observe deviations, or cannot punish credibly, the cooperative path may fail.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for surplus division, alternating offers, take-it-or-leave-it offers, or incomplete information about values. Look for trigger strategies, continuation values, and inequalities comparing temptation today to punishment tomorrow.

Concepts In This Lecture

Bargaining

Problem: The problem is dividing gains from agreement when each side would like more of the surplus and can sometimes wait, reject, or walk away.

Mathematical handle: The object is a bargaining game with offers, acceptance rules, disagreement payoffs, and sometimes discounting.

What breaks without it: Without bargaining theory, the course could say agreement is efficient but not explain who gets what or why delay occurs. It would miss how threat points and waiting costs shape the split.

Open concept page

Infinitely Repeated Games

Problem: The problem is how cooperation can survive when each player is tempted to grab a short-run gain by cheating.

Mathematical handle: The object is a repeated game with continuation payoffs and a discount factor that measures patience.

What breaks without it: Without infinite repetition, the course would lack the clean model where ongoing relationships sustain behavior that fails in a one-shot interaction. It would also miss why patience is a strategic parameter.
