Lecture 9 · Time, Threats, And Credibility, Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 9: Negotiation

Treats bargaining as strategic surplus division shaped by offers, rejection, timing, patience, and outside options.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: does bargaining power come from toughness, confidence, or fairness? The lecture turns bargaining power into payoffs from accepting, rejecting, waiting, and walking away.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture treats bargaining as surplus division under timing pressure. Offers, rejection, patience, and outside options are not personality traits; they become payoff consequences inside a dynamic game.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through continuation values and discounting. Waiting can shrink payoffs, so an offer is judged against what each side expects from rejecting and continuing.

The mathematical model has to exist because you cannot guess the split from the size of the surplus alone. A huge surplus (say, 1 million dollars) can be split 999k-to-1k if one side has all the power. The split comes from outside options, impatience, timing, and information—not from how much value is available. To explain who gets what, you have to put these factors into a model and solve it. Without the math, you have only intuition, which misses how use (outside options, patience, timing) shapes the deal. In an ongoing relationship, today's choice has consequences for your future payoffs. To say 'the future matters' is true but vague. To make it precise, you need the discount factor (how much today's score compared to tomorrow's) and the continuation payoff (what you earn after today). These mathematical objects let you write down an inequality: gain from cheating ≤ discounted punishment loss. Without that math, 'the future will punish me' remains just a slogan, not a reason you can verify. The math turns the vague idea into a check: does this strategy actually work given these payoffs?

Worked Mini-Example

A buyer and seller can split value today or wait for another offer tomorrow. If waiting is costly, the side that can credibly wait longer may receive a larger share.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to explain bargaining power as confidence or stubbornness. The model asks what each side can get by refusing now and moving to the next bargaining stage.

The tempting shortcut is to explain negotiation outcomes as personality. That fails because a calm player with a good outside option can have more power than a loud player whose next-best alternative is bad.

The trap is thinking: the split ought to be fair, so that must be the answer. Game theory does not care about fair. It asks: given the outside options, the patience of each side, and the timing of offers, which split will actually happen and stay stable? A 30-70 split might be predicted if one side has all the power. Students have to let go of the idea that the answer should be nice or equal. A student hears 'infinite repetition' and thinks the problem is solved — cooperation will happen automatically. But that is wrong. Infinite repetition is necessary, but not enough. The cooperation strategy must also satisfy the payoff inequality. If the punishment payoff is too small, if the discount factor is too low (the other player only cares about right now), or if the one-time cheat gain is very large, then cheating still pays. The student must check the inequality, not just invoke repetition.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that bargaining is a dynamic comparison of continuation values. An offer is attractive or not because of what each side expects from delay, rejection, and future bargaining positions.

Reader Work

Separate total surplus, each side's outside option, and the cost of waiting. Then change one of those three inputs and trace how the bargaining position changes. The work is to translate toughness into continuation values rather than personality.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: change only one outside option or waiting cost and predict how the split should move. If your answer uses toughness without changing a continuation payoff, you have left the model and returned to informal personality language. Then state which side's threat point actually improved.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to subgame perfection: if a bargaining path uses future refusals or punishments, those continuation actions must be rational when the later subgame is reached.

How To Recognize This Later

When you see negotiations over a division (salary talks, merger price, splitting inheritance, peace treaty terms, or any situation where both sides gain from a deal but fight over the split), you are seeing bargaining. Look for these clues: one side makes an offer, the other accepts or rejects; there may be multiple rounds; delay is costly; one side has a backup plan (outside option); one side can wait longer than the other. When you see a real situation, look for these signs: trigger strategies (someone says 'I will do X as long as you do Y; if you cheat, I will do Z forever'), continuation values (someone mentions what a relationship is worth over time), and payoff comparisons (someone weighs today's gain against tomorrow's loss). These are the fingerprints of infinite-repetition logic.

Concepts In This Lecture

Bargaining

Problem: When a seller and buyer meet on a used car, both would prefer a deal to no deal. But the seller wants the price high, the buyer wants it low. Each can walk away. Each can wait. Each can reject offers. Bargaining asks: what price will actually stick, and how long will it take to agree?

Mathematical handle: The bargaining game has several parts: who can make offers (and in what order), what happens if an offer is rejected (disagreement payoff or the next person's chance to offer), how much delay costs each side (discounting—waiting this round gives you less value later), and sometimes private information (you do not know if the other side values the good at 5,000 or 20,000). The math model puts all these pieces together so you can solve which offers will be accepted.

What breaks without it: Without bargaining theory, you might say agreement is efficient (everyone is better off than if they quit) but then shrug about division. The course would skip the hard part: who gets 60% and who gets 40%? Why? Bargaining theory explains that the split comes from each side's outside option (what they get if talks fail), how much delay costs each side, and who has the power to make an offer. Leave out that tool and you have no way to predict what deal actually happens.

Infinitely Repeated Games

Problem: When you and someone else interact over and over, each of you wants to cheat today to get an instant reward — but you're worried the other person will punish you tomorrow if you do.

Mathematical handle: The object is a game played round after round, where your score today plus your discounted score tomorrow plus your discounted-twice score the day after is your total payoff. The discount factor delta measures how much you care about your future self.

What breaks without it: Without infinite repetition, you'd only have the case where the last round is known — and in that round, punishment fails because it cannot happen afterward. You would lose the simplest model showing how ongoing relationships can make people behave decently toward each other. You would also miss why impatience (a low delta) is a real threat to cooperation.
