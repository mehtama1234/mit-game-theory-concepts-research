Lecture 21 · Private Information And Beliefs

Lecture 21: Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium

Combines dynamic credibility with belief updating in games where histories can reveal private information and observed actions change what players infer.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: can we solve a dynamic hidden-information game with strategies alone? The lecture says beliefs are part of the solution because players act based on what histories make them think.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture combines time with hidden information. Strategies must be sequentially rational, and beliefs must be specified and updated where possible, because histories can reveal private information.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as a strategy profile plus a belief system. At each information set, actions must be optimal given beliefs, and beliefs must follow Bayes' rule on path.

Uncertainty has two faces: what the world actually is, and what someone thinks the world is. Types capture the first; beliefs capture the second. To analyze strategic moves—like lying, signaling, or updating—you must keep them separate. If you blur them, you cannot ask whether a move reveals truth or hides it. The math exists because you need a language for both what players do and what they believe. Strategies (rules for action) and beliefs (probability assignments to unseen facts) have to be tied together in a way that ordinary game solving does not require. Bayes' rule is the glue: it says how beliefs update when you observe data. By writing out both pieces and connecting them with Bayes' rule, the math forces you to check whether a proposed state is actually consistent—whether moves make sense given beliefs, and beliefs make sense given moves.

Worked Mini-Example

After observing a price or signal, a buyer forms a belief about seller type and chooses whether to buy. PBE checks both the seller's signal and the buyer's belief-driven response.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to solve only strategies and treat beliefs as commentary. In PBE, beliefs are part of the equilibrium object.

The tempting shortcut is to compute actions and leave beliefs implicit. That fails when an observed move changes what a receiver thinks about type, and that changed belief changes the receiver's best response.

Students mix up the statement: the player believes the type. But type is the reality; belief is the guess. The player knows their own type. The other player has a belief about what type the first player has. Conflating them breaks the model because you lose the distinction between what one person knows and what another person guesses. Many students solve the strategies first, then add beliefs as an explanation. But if off-path beliefs—guesses about what happens if the game goes somewhere unexpected—are left vague or arbitrary, the student misses that those beliefs control what happens if the game does reach that fork. A complete answer pins down both the strategies players follow and the beliefs they hold at every possible fork, including the ones they do not expect to reach.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that equilibrium now has two coupled parts: strategies must be optimal given beliefs, and beliefs must be updated consistently where the path of play gives evidence.

Reader Work

Write strategies and beliefs in pairs. After every observed action, ask whether Bayes rule pins down the belief and whether the action is optimal under that belief. The work is to keep off-path assumptions visible.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: after an observed action, write both the receiver's belief and the receiver's best response. Then mark whether the belief was pinned down by Bayes rule or chosen off path. This keeps belief discipline visible.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to signaling: once actions can reveal information, the course studies when informed players choose signals that separate, pool, or distort what receivers believe.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for any situation where behavior depends on something one side knows but the other does not: a company's profit or strength, a worker's ability, a customer's willingness to pay, the condition of an asset, or a person's type or preference. That is where types and beliefs live. Look for games that unfold over time and players have incomplete information—they do not know some fact about each other or the world. Watch for these signals: players observe some earlier move and must form a new belief about an unseen type or hidden state; later moves depend on those updated beliefs; the problem asks you to verify that both the strategy moves and the belief updates are correct. Common places: a sender can choose whether to signal their type (high quality picking expensive education, low quality picking cheap school); a buyer can make an opening offer but does not know the seller's reservation price (the minimum the seller will accept), so the offer revises the buyer's guess about that minimum; negotiators exchange offers over time, each one a signal about desperation or patience. If a model mentions beliefs alongside dynamic strategies, or asks you to check off-path beliefs, you are looking at this equilibrium concept.

Concepts In This Lecture

Types And Beliefs

Problem: When someone knows something you do not, you have to guess what they know and what they will do based on that hidden information. The problem is tracking both the secret and everyone's best guess about it in a way that explains behavior.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical object is a type space—the set of all possible private facts—paired with a probability distribution over types or histories for each player.

What breaks without it: Without types and beliefs, you cannot build a model where one side has secret information and the other side reacts to it. You could not explain why one bidder bids high and another low, or why a sender might lie and a receiver might not trust them. You would lose the ability to ask whether a guess can be updated or if lies can be caught.

Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium

Problem: When a game happens step by step and players have secret information: after you see someone's move, you must update your guess about what they know, then decide your own next move based on that updated guess. The puzzle is to find a state where everyone's guesses make sense with what they've seen, and everyone's actions are rational given their guesses.

Mathematical handle: A settled state is a pair: a strategy for each player (a complete plan for all moves) plus a belief system (the guesses each player holds after each possible sequence of events they might see). This pair must satisfy two conditions: given their beliefs, each player's moves are optimal—the best they can do; wherever the game path they actually follow gives enough history, their beliefs must update correctly by Bayes' rule.

What breaks without it: Without this frame, a game model might say 'this player sends a signal' without explaining what beliefs make the signal worth sending, or why the other player would answer that way. The moves look like they work, but the hidden guesses that justify them are never checked. You end up with advice that sounds good but has no foundation—the beliefs and moves do not actually support each other.
