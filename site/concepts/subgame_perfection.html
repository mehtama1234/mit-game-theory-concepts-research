Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

An equilibrium that remains sensible after every possible history.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that Nash equilibrium can allow plans based on threats that would not be carried out if the threatened moment arrived.

Why does this problem exist?

Subgame perfection exists because a strategy should be credible after every history, not only from the starting point.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Backward induction assigns optimal choices from terminal decision nodes back to the start. The useful relation is `V(node) = max_action V(next node).`. In plain language: V is continuation value; at each decision point the player chooses the next branch with the best continuation value.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version asks Nash equilibrium to pass inside the tree, not only at the root. A dynamic-game strategy describes what players would do after histories that may never happen on the predicted path. Those off-path prescriptions matter because they can support or undermine threats. Subgame perfection rejects equilibria whose continuation play would fail the Nash test in a proper subgame.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is equilibrium at every credible starting point. A subgame is a piece of the original tree that can be considered as a game on its own. The strategy profile must induce Nash behavior in each such piece.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because a dynamic strategy includes plans for histories that may not occur. Those plans can support threats, so they must be checked. Subgame perfection formalizes the demand that continuation behavior is itself equilibrium behavior wherever a real subgame begins.

Why is this concept important?

Subgame perfection matters because it repairs Nash equilibrium for dynamic games. It asks whether the equilibrium behavior remains rational after every history where the continuation game can be considered on its own.

What breaks without it?

Without subgame perfection, dynamic models can rest on noncredible threats. A strategy profile may pass the initial Nash test only because it prescribes off-path behavior that nobody would actually choose later.

Worked Mini-Example

An incumbent firm may threaten to fight entry, but if fighting loses money after entry occurs, the threat is not subgame perfect. The entrant should not be convinced by it.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by ignoring histories that do not occur in equilibrium. Those histories are exactly where empty threats hide, so the course makes students inspect them.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is subgames. Some dynamic information problems do not have proper subgames at the histories where beliefs matter, which is why PBE enters later. This boundary is why the course later needs belief-based refinements. Some histories are inside clean subgames, but hidden-information histories can require the analyst to specify what players believe before optimal continuation play can even be evaluated. The refinement is small in wording but large in what behavior it rules out.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to think any Nash equilibrium in a dynamic game is credible.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for dynamic games, off-path histories, or threats where the key question is whether the promised future action would really be optimal.

Connected Concepts

Subgame perfection connects Nash to backward induction and one-shot deviation. PBE later extends the same credibility demand when hidden information means beliefs must be specified too.

Worked Example Card

Setup: An entrant considers entering a market after the incumbent threatens a price war.

Walkthrough: The proposed strategy may look like a Nash equilibrium if the entrant is scared away. But subgame perfection asks what happens in the subgame after entry. If the incumbent would rather accommodate than fight there, the threat is not credible.

Lesson: Subgame perfection removes equilibria supported by empty threats.

Trap: Do not accept a threat merely because it keeps the game from reaching the place where the threat would be tested.

Model move: The modeling move is to test equilibrium behavior inside every subgame that could be reached. A strategy must remain optimal even after the history that was supposed to be deterred.

Reader action: Jump to the history the threat tries to prevent and solve from there. If the threatened action is not optimal once that history is reached, mark the original strategy profile as failing the subgame test. If no proper subgame starts there, flag that beliefs may be doing the work instead.

Boundary check: The example needs refinement when information sets prevent a proper subgame, or when off-path beliefs rather than subgame structure carry the prediction.
