Preferences

A way to say which outcomes a person ranks above others.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that before we talk about strategy, we need a way to say what one person is trying to get. A person may like one outcome more than another even when dollars, risk, effort, and timing are mixed together.

Why does this problem exist?

This exists because choice needs ordering. If the person cannot rank outcomes, then words like better, worse, rational, or optimal have nothing to attach to.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A preference relation ranks outcomes, often represented by a utility function u(x). The useful relation is `x preferred to y means u(x) > u(y).`. In plain language: x and y are possible outcomes; u assigns each outcome a number; the larger number records the option the person ranks higher.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version starts with a simple pressure: people choose among outcomes before any game begins. The course therefore needs a relation that says whether one outcome is ranked above, below, or tied with another. Completeness means the model can compare the relevant outcomes; transitivity means the comparisons do not cycle. Those assumptions are not moral truths about people. They are the minimum structure needed to turn choice into an object that can be used later. Once that object exists, utility can represent it numerically, and payoff tables can inherit the ranking.

Mathematical Intuition

The math is a compression of choice behavior. Instead of carrying the full story of each outcome, the preference relation keeps the ordering that matters for the decision. If x is preferred to y, a utility representation can assign x a higher number than y. The number is secondary; the preserved order is the point.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary language can hide contradictions. People can say they like many things, but a model needs to know whether those comparisons can be used together. A preference relation is the smallest formal object that lets the course ask whether the choices are complete enough and consistent enough to support later payoff reasoning.

Why is this concept important?

Preferences matter because they are the first act of compression in the course. The model cannot carry every emotional, legal, or social detail of an outcome, so it keeps the order of what the player would choose. That order becomes the base layer for utility, payoffs, and later welfare comparisons.

What breaks without it?

Without preferences, a payoff table has no disciplined source. The analyst could assign numbers, but there would be no explanation of why a player ranks one result over another or why a later best-response calculation should be trusted.

Worked Mini-Example

If someone prefers coffee to tea and tea to water, transitivity says they should prefer coffee to water. Without that, a model can chase its own tail because the person can be walked around a cycle of choices.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by asking whether the preference is realistic for every human mood. The course is doing a modeling move: it asks whether the relevant choices in the problem can be treated as stable enough for strategic analysis.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is that preferences describe rankings within the modeled choice problem; they do not explain where desires come from. If the lecture question is about persuasion, learning, or changing tastes, preference is only the starting input, not the full explanation.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to think preferences are emotions. In the course they are a disciplined choice ordering, not a psychological biography.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for the place where an economic model has to say what an actor counts as better before any strategic reasoning begins.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: before using payoffs, ask whether the model has a stable ordering over the relevant outcomes. If the same three choices create a cycle, utility numbers will only hide the inconsistency. Preferences are the ordering discipline underneath every later payoff table.

Connected Concepts

Preferences feed utility, expected utility, bargaining surplus, bidder values, and welfare comparisons. The same primitive is quiet in later lectures because the course has already installed it as the language of individual motivation.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A student must choose between coffee, tea, and water before class.

Walkthrough: Suppose they choose coffee over tea and tea over water. Transitivity says the model should also rank coffee above water. If the student instead ranks water above coffee, the model can create a cycle where every chosen option can be beaten by another option.

Lesson: Preference assumptions are not about proving what the student truly feels. They are the minimum ordering discipline needed before utility or payoffs can mean anything.

Trap: Do not treat a preference relation as a life story. It is the ranking the model needs for this decision problem.

Model move: The modeling move is to ignore everything except the ordering needed for the choice. Before assigning numbers, ask whether the ranking is complete enough and consistent enough to support later payoff comparisons.

Reader action: Write the three pairwise comparisons on separate lines, then check whether they form a cycle. Do not assign utility numbers until the order itself is coherent; otherwise the numbers hide the broken choice logic.

Numbers / table check: Make a three-row table: coffee versus tea, tea versus water, and coffee versus water. If the first two rows point upward but the third row points downward, the ranking cannot support one stable utility scale for this decision problem.

Boundary check: The example stops working if the student's choice changes with context, framing, or uncertainty that the simple ranking does not represent.
