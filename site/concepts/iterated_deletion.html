Iterated Deletion

A process where you remove strategies that are clearly bad, then look at the smaller game left behind and remove new strategies that are now bad in this smaller game, and keep repeating until nothing more can be removed.

What real-world problem is this about?

When you remove one bad move, the game changes—what was once a decent move might now be a bad move in the smaller game. This keeps happening in waves: you delete, the game shrinks, new bad moves appear, you delete again.

Why does this problem exist?

This exists because when people think strategically, they think recursively. You can reason: I won't pick this move because it's bad. Then I know they won't pick it, so I can ignore it. But now my own choices look different because some of their options are gone. Then they can reason that you see this, and your choices look different to them. Reasoning about each other's reasoning creates layers.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Dominance is comparing two strategies by checking whether one is always at least as good as the other across all the moves the other players might make. The technical check is: strategy s dominates strategy t if the score from s is always greater than or equal to the score from t, no matter what the other players choose, and sometimes strictly greater. In plain terms: one strategy beats another in every single scenario the other players create.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The deep version adds reasoning about reasoning. Dominance compares strategies directly, but after you delete some of them, the remaining game is psychologically different—your opponent has fewer real options. A strategy that wasn't dominated in the original game might become dominated in the smaller game because some of your opponent's options disappeared. This creates a chain: I eliminate your bad moves, you understand I did that, so you eliminate my moves that were only reasonable when your bad moves were possible, and so on. The process is mechanical—apply a rule, shrink the game, repeat—but its meaning depends on players understanding rationality and understanding that others understand rationality too.

Mathematical Intuition

Think of the game as a shrinking action set. Start with all possible moves for all players. Apply dominance to find and remove bad strategies. Now the game is smaller. Apply dominance again to this reduced version—the comparisons change because fewer opponent options exist. Apply once more. Each round, the domain of what the other players might do gets smaller, so the comparison of whether your strategy is good shifts. This is repeated trimming of a shrinking problem.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object exists because rationality can nest. Once you eliminate one choice as unreasonable, the remaining game changes, and what was reasonable before might become unreasonable now. Iterated deletion is the formal procedure that tracks this shrinking action set carefully and systematically, rather than relying on informal intuition about multiple rounds of thinking. It forces you to be precise about what is eliminated and why, and what that elimination means for the next round.

Why is this concept important?

Strategic reasoning has layers when people think about what others will do. Removing one unreasonable choice can make another choice unreasonable in the resulting smaller game, so understanding what's reasonable can spread through the whole table like a chain reaction. This is how you narrow down from many possible strategies to a few plausible ones.

What breaks without it?

Without iterated deletion, you miss how reasoning about one round changes what's reasonable in the next round. Some games look very complicated until you remove the obviously bad moves—then the pattern of what makes sense becomes clearer. You'd be stuck looking at all possible choices at once instead of understanding how rationality can narrow them down step by step.

Worked Mini-Example

Imagine a guessing game where players guess numbers from 0 to 100. A guess of 100 is unreasonable because no rational person would guess that. Remove all guesses of 100. Now 99 looks unreasonable for the same reason—no one will guess 100, so no one can justify guessing 99. Remove it. Keep removing guesses from the top downward. The process spirals inward, showing why only very low guesses survive rational reasoning.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often gloss over whether the game uses strict or weak dominance and whether the order of deletions matters. These are not details—with weak dominance especially, different deletion orders can leave different strategies alive, so the lecture distinction between strict and weak is crucial. Saying 'we deleted' is not enough; you need to know which deletion rule was applied and what remained.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is which dominance rule you use—strict or weak—because they have different logical force. Strict dominance can never create a different result based on deletion order, but weak dominance can. Common knowledge assumptions also matter: how many rounds of 'everyone knows everyone is rational' can you assume? These boundary choices affect what gets eliminated and what survives. The lecture distinguishes the procedure from equilibrium selection because surviving iterated deletion does not mean a strategy will be played; it only means the strategy has not been ruled out by this particular rationality test.

Common Misunderstanding

A big mistake is ignoring the difference between strict dominance and weak dominance. Strict dominance means one strategy is strictly better in every scenario. Weak dominance means one strategy is better-or-equal in every scenario and strictly better in at least one. With weak dominance, the order in which you delete strategies can change what survives, so you have to be careful about which version you're using.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for elimination arguments that remove options in stages, where each stage uses the assumption that others are rational. When you see 'a player would not do X because that would only make sense if an opponent did Y, but the opponent would not do Y,' you are watching iterated reasoning. Find patterns where an action is ruled out based on what remains after earlier eliminations, not just a one-time comparison.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: iterated deletion is a reasoning chain, not one big eraser. Each round must be justified by the smaller game produced by earlier justified deletions. The surviving set means not eliminated by that test, not necessarily predicted.

Connected Concepts

Iterated deletion connects to dominance (the idea of one move being worse than another) and to rationalizability (what beliefs about others' moves allow you to justify a move). It also shows what common knowledge means: if everyone knows everyone is rational, and everyone knows everyone knows this, the deletion process can go many rounds. But iterated deletion is different from Nash equilibrium—deletion can narrow down the game before you find an equilibrium, but it doesn't always point to one unique answer for what will happen.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Players in a guessing game choose numbers, and very high guesses cannot be optimal once everyone understands the target rule.

Walkthrough: First remove guesses that cannot win against any rational play. After those are gone, the upper bound of reasonable guesses falls. That can make the next-highest guesses impossible too, so the deletion repeats on the smaller game.

Lesson: Iterated deletion shows how one round of rationality can create the next round's reasoning.

Trap: Do not treat surviving deletion as the same thing as being played in equilibrium.

Model move: The modeling move is to let one round of eliminated impossibilities change the next round's strategic environment. Each deletion narrows what rational players need to consider.

Reader action: Keep a deletion log with round number, deleted action, and the payoff comparison that allowed it. Never use a later deletion to justify an earlier one, because the order is part of the argument. When the process stops, name what survived and what the method still has not predicted.

Numbers / table check: Create a deletion ledger with columns round, action removed, and reason. Round 1 might remove guesses above 67; round 2 then evaluates the smaller set. If a round uses a fact not yet established, the iteration is out of order.

Boundary check: The example stops proving prediction when multiple strategies survive, when weak deletion changes the result, or when common knowledge of rationality is not plausible.

Iterated Deletion As Repeatedly Cleaning The Choice Set

Start From Removing One Bad Move

Iterated deletion begins with the same everyday thought as dominance: if one choice is never worth taking, remove it. Then the important part happens. Once that move is gone, the game has changed. A choice that was not clearly bad before may become bad after the first cleanup. The process repeats until no more moves can be removed by the rule being used.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Strategic reasoning often works in layers. A player may rule out an action for one opponent, then reason that the opponent will also rule out something else, and so on. Iterated deletion exists because beliefs about other players' rationality can shrink the set of possible behavior step by step. It gives the course a way to show how simple comparisons can have larger consequences after repeated use.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to delete carelessly and then treat the final survivor as unquestionably predicted. The result depends on the deletion rule. Strict dominance is safer than weak dominance. Weak deletion can depend on order and can remove behavior that matters under other beliefs. Iterated deletion is a reasoning tool, not a license to erase choices just because they look unlikely.

Why It Matters Beyond Small Games

Iterated deletion matters in market entry, voting, auctions, security planning, and algorithmic search because it shows how impossible or indefensible choices can be removed in rounds. In topology-heavy work, deletion can simplify the strategy space before deeper equilibrium reasoning begins, but it must not destroy the assumptions needed for existence or hide the behavior the model was supposed to explain. The method is useful only when every deletion is explained in everyday terms: which choice has lost its defense, and why no belief in the remaining model supports it.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Algorithmic search

A search procedure can prune options, then prune again after the remaining options change. The same caution applies: the pruning rule must be justified.

Market strategy

A firm may first rule out a price, then rule out another price after rivals are expected to rule out their own bad choices.
