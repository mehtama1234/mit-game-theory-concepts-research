Iterated Deletion

Repeatedly removing bad strategies and seeing what remains.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that once obviously bad moves are removed, new moves may become obviously bad in the smaller game.

Why does this problem exist?

This exists because reasoning about rational players is recursive. I can remove my dominated action, then you can reason that I removed it, then your own comparison may change.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Dominance compares one strategy against another across possible opponent choices. The useful relation is `s dominates t if u_i(s, a_-i) >= u_i(t, a_-i) for all a_-i, with strict inequality somewhere.`. In plain language: The inequality checks every possible action by others; dominance means one strategy is never worse and sometimes better.

Why is this concept important?

This concept matters because it turns a loose strategic story into a checkable claim about what someone would actually choose.

What breaks without it?

Without this concept, the analysis can name the players and choices but cannot tell whether the proposed behavior survives individual incentives. The important caveat is that the result depends on the deletion rule. Strict dominance gives a cleaner foundation; weak dominance may remove strategies whose removal changes later comparisons.

Worked Mini-Example

In a guessing game, very high guesses may be impossible for a rational winner; once those are removed, the next-highest guesses become impossible too. The deletion process walks inward.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to ignore whether the deletion uses strict or weak dominance. Weak deletion can depend on order and needs more care.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for step-by-step elimination arguments where rationality of others is used more than once.

Connected Concepts

The same primitive returns whenever the course asks whether a proposed behavior is compatible with incentives, beliefs, timing, or information. The deeper idea is shared reasoning. Each deletion says not only that a move is bad, but that other players can understand it is bad and adjust their own reasoning to the smaller game.
