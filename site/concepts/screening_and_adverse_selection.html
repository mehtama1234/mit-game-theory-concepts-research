Screening And Adverse Selection

A hidden-information problem where a rule or price changes who chooses to participate, sometimes making trade collapse.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that a price or offer can change who shows up. A seller, insurer, or bargainer may set a rule thinking about an average customer, but the people who accept are not average; they are selected by the rule itself.

Why does this problem exist?

Screening and adverse selection exist because one side has hidden information about value, cost, quality, health, or risk. The uninformed side can only offer terms, and those terms filter the informed side. That filtering changes the group being served.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a probability distribution over states, types, or histories. The useful relation is `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).`. In plain language: theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version begins with the clean complete-information benchmark: if the buyer value exceeds seller cost, there is room for a price that helps both sides, so agreement should happen without delay. Incomplete information breaks that logic. A price or offer does not merely divide surplus; it also sorts types. In insurance, people who buy at a given price are the people for whom the product is worth at least that price, and those people may be the most expensive to serve. In bargaining, an offer or rejection can reveal something about value or cost. The mathematical lesson is that the payoff must be computed after selection, not before.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is conditional composition. Start with a population of possible types. A rule selects a subset of those types. The payoff calculation must use the selected subset, because the rule itself changed who participates.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary average reasoning lies in selected populations. A cutoff, participation condition, or posterior distribution is needed to say who accepts, who rejects, and what the uninformed side should believe after observing that choice.

Why is this concept important?

This concept matters because it explains delay, disagreement, insurance problems, used-good markets, and bargaining failures even when trade would create value under full information. It shows why hidden information can block mutually beneficial exchange.

What breaks without it?

Without this concept, the course would predict trade whenever buyer value exceeds seller cost. It would miss the reason some trades fail: the terms that attract one type may drive away another or reveal information that changes future bargaining.

Worked Mini-Example

An insurer sets one price for a policy. People who expect low medical costs may walk away, while people who expect high costs are more likely to buy. Raising the price can bring in a worse risk pool, so the insurer cannot reason from the average person before the price was set.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by treating hidden information as just another probability. The lecture point is sharper: the action or price changes which hidden types remain in the game, so the probability distribution after participation is not the original distribution.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the source of selection. Not every failed trade is adverse selection; the mechanism requires hidden type information and terms that select different types differently. The evidence supports the screening/adverse-selection logic in the lecture's buyer-seller and insurance examples. Broader claims about real insurance or legal bargaining require evidence about actual information, costs, offers, and participation.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to treat demand as only fewer people buying when price rises. In adverse selection, the identity of the buyers changes too, and that composition change can matter more than the number of buyers.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for models where an offer, price, contract, or test makes different hidden types sort themselves. If changing the rule changes the pool of participants, the screening/adverse-selection primitive is active.

Connected Concepts

Screening and adverse selection connect Bayesian games, types and beliefs, bargaining, signaling, auctions, and PBE. Signaling asks what a privately informed first mover reveals through action; screening asks how an uninformed first mover designs a rule that makes hidden types reveal themselves by accepting or rejecting.

Worked Example Card

Setup: An insurer sets one price for people with different hidden health risks.

Walkthrough: At a low price, both low-risk and high-risk buyers may buy. At a higher price, low-risk buyers may leave because the policy is not worth it to them. The remaining pool can become riskier, so raising price can worsen the seller's problem.

Lesson: Adverse selection appears when participation reveals hidden type and changes the group being served.

Trap: Do not assume a higher price simply means more revenue per customer; it can change who the customers are.
