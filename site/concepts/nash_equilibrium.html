Nash Equilibrium

Read the deep dive — seen on a real run →

A situation where each person's chosen move is the best they can do given what everyone else is doing, so nobody has a reason to switch alone.

What real-world problem is this about?

You are trying to predict what a group of people will do when they are all trying to outthink each other. The problem is: your prediction only works if, after each person sees what you said the others would do, that person does not regret their own choice and decide to change.

Why does this problem exist?

A prediction about what rational people will do has to be self-consistent. If you predict a pattern and then one person, looking at that pattern, says "Wait, I could do better by switching," then your prediction breaks. Nash equilibrium is a prediction that survives this test.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

The formal condition is: for every player i, their action a_i must be in the set BR_i(a_-i), meaning their action is among the best responses they could pick given what everyone else is doing. In other words, each player's move is the highest-scoring option available to them, given the others' moves.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

To build understanding from the ground up: First, recognize that each player picks a move that scores best given what they expect others to do—that is best response. Now imagine a profile where best response closes the loop: everyone's action is a best response to everyone else's action. Each person reasons, "If they are all doing this, then my best choice is that." And that reasoning is true for every player at once. That is a Nash equilibrium. The course uses this idea to make a prediction that the course can stand behind. A proposed outcome is only a sound forecast if it survives each player asking in private, "Given what everyone else will do, do I want to stick to my part, or switch?" When you find a Nash equilibrium, you have found a prediction where every player, alone, has no reason to regret their move.

Mathematical Intuition

Think of it as a mutual best-response loop. Each player's action sits inside their best-response set given everyone else's actions. Formally, a_i ∈ BR_i(a_-i) for all i. Intuitively, imagine you hold everyone's moves still and ask each player in turn, "Would you change?" If nobody would change, the profile is Nash. The condition is symmetric across players: nobody has an escape route.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

Strategic prediction is circular. Each player's best move depends on what others do, which depends on what each of them expects, which depends on what everyone expects the first player to do. The model needs a fixed point where all these expectations and moves fit together. Nash equilibrium is that fixed point—it is the formal answer to the question, "What profile survives the logical loop of mutual best response?"

Why is this concept important?

Nash equilibrium gives you a way to make a disciplined forecast about what will happen when each person is trying to do best for themselves. It does not say the outcome is fair or efficient—only that each person, acting alone, has no better move. This is the game-theory baseline for a prediction that holds up.

What breaks without it?

Without the Nash test, any outcome could be defended just by naming it. Someone could propose a pattern and offer no reason why each person would stick to it. Nash forces the question: if everyone else follows this pattern, does each individual want to follow it too?

Worked Mini-Example

Two trucks arrive at a narrow bridge from opposite ends at the same time. If each truck driver thinks the other will wait, but each would rather go forward if the other waits, then neither will actually wait—both will try to cross and collide. A stable pattern is when each driver expects the other to do something specific and, based on that expectation, that driver's own choice makes sense. A stable pattern is one where each driver's move is their best response to what they expect.

Where Students Get Stuck

A common trap is to confuse Nash equilibrium with a good outcome or a balanced state. Students hear equilibrium and imagine something stable and fair. But a Nash equilibrium can be the worst outcome available, unfair, or wasteful. It only guarantees one narrow thing: no single player can unilaterally improve. Do not expect it to be nice.

Where The Idea Stops Working

Nash equilibrium checks one thing: unilateral deviation. It does not handle threats that are not credible, does not account for players learning and changing beliefs over time, does not allow groups of players to conspire together, and does not evaluate whether the outcome is fair without additional work. Know what it does and does not do.

Common Misunderstanding

Many people hear equilibrium and think it means a good, fair, or efficient outcome. Nash equilibrium has no such promise. It can describe very bad outcomes—even prisoner's dilemma, where everyone loses—because it only cares about one thing: whether each person, acting alone, can do better. The outcome can be wasteful or unfair and still be Nash.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

In new situations, look for a pattern of behavior where no single participant can improve their own outcome by changing their own move alone, given that everyone else keeps doing what they are doing. When you spot that pattern, you have identified a candidate Nash equilibrium.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: test stability one player at a time. Freeze everyone else and search for a profitable switch. If none exists for any player, the profile is Nash. That says unilateral regret is gone, not that the outcome is good.

Connected Concepts

Nash equilibrium is where several earlier ideas come together. It uses best response (the idea that each player picks their highest-scoring move given the others). It connects forward to mixed strategies (where players sometimes roll dice to stay unpredictable), subgame perfection (equilibrium in every piece of a game tree), Bayesian Nash (equilibrium when players have private secrets), and perfect Bayesian equilibrium (combining both refinements). Each of these keeps the same core—no player wants to deviate alone—while handling a new complication like time, randomness, or hidden information.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Two coffee shops each choose a price, and each shop's best choice depends on the other's price.

Walkthrough: Start with a proposed pair of prices. Ask whether shop A wants to change while shop B's price stays fixed. Then ask the same question for shop B. If neither shop wants a one-sided change, the pair passes the Nash test.

Lesson: Nash equilibrium is mutual best-response consistency, not a promise that the outcome is good for everyone.

Trap: Do not read equilibrium as fairness, efficiency, or happiness; it only means no profitable unilateral deviation.

Model move: The modeling move is to test the proposed outcome from each player's private point of view. Every player gets a unilateral deviation audit while the others are held fixed.

Reader action: For each player, freeze everyone else's choice and search for one profitable switch. Write 'no switch pays' only after the check is done for every player; one profitable switch rejects the candidate.

Numbers / table check: For candidate high, high, write shop A's payoff from staying high and switching low while shop B stays high. Then repeat for shop B. If either switch raises payoff, cross out the candidate; do not average the two shops' welfare.

Boundary check: The example does not rule out coordinated deviations, renegotiation, repeated adjustment, or a different equilibrium that the same game might also support.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Nash card closes a loop made from best-response checks. Start with a proposed outcome, freeze everyone except one player, and ask whether that player wants to switch. Repeat for every player. The mathematical condition means each chosen action belongs to that player's best-response set. It does not mean the outcome is fair, efficient, or collectively desirable; it only means no single player has a profitable one-person escape.

Nash Equilibrium As A No-One-Wants-To-Move Test

Start From A Settled Situation

Nash equilibrium begins with a proposed situation, not with a promise that the situation is good. Imagine two firms choosing prices, two drivers choosing routes, or two roommates choosing chores. The question is simple: if everyone else keeps doing what the proposal says, does any one person want to change alone? If the answer is no for every player, the situation passes the Nash test. If even one player wants to move, the proposed story is not stable in this sense.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Strategic stories are easy to tell and hard to trust. Someone can say prices will stay high, both sides will cooperate, or each player will choose a certain action. Nash equilibrium forces that story through a personal incentive check. Each player gets to ask whether a different available action gives a better payoff, holding the others fixed. The concept exists because prediction in a game needs more than a nice story; it needs to survive the pressure of individual deviation.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to hear equilibrium and think fair, peaceful, efficient, or morally right. Nash equilibrium does not say any of that. A traffic jam can be stable because no one driver can improve by switching routes. A bad market outcome can be stable because each firm is already doing its best against the others. The test is narrow: given what everyone else is doing, does one player want to change alone?

Why It Matters Beyond The Course

The idea matters because many systems settle into patterns that are hard to change one person at a time. In politics, a voting rule can hold a poor outcome because each voter faces the same incentives. In networks, people may stay on a platform because others are there. In topology, Nash existence proofs often use fixed-point reasoning: a best-response map points from expected behavior to chosen behavior, and a fixed point is a place where the map sends the behavior back to itself.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Platform markets

A platform can be hard to dislodge because each user chooses based on where the other users already are. Stability can come from dependence, not quality.

Topology and fixed points

The fixed point says there is a self-consistent profile under the assumptions. It does not say the profile is efficient, unique, or easy to find.
