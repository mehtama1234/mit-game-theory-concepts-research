Strategic Form Games

A compact table of players, actions, and payoffs chosen at the same time.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is representing a situation where everyone chooses without first seeing the others' choices, yet everyone's payoff depends on the whole combination.

Why does this problem exist?

This exists because strategic dependence needs a shared object. A verbal story hides who can choose what and what each combination produces.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A payoff function u_i(a_i, a_-i) maps everyone's actions to player i's payoff. The useful relation is `u_i(a_i, a_-i).`. In plain language: i is one player; a_i is that player's action; a_-i means everyone else's actions; u_i is the payoff assigned to the whole action profile.

Why is this concept important?

This concept matters because it turns a loose strategic story into a checkable claim about what someone would actually choose.

What breaks without it?

Without this concept, the analysis can name the players and choices but cannot tell whether the proposed behavior survives individual incentives. The student trap is forgetting that the table is a model choice. If timing, observation, or repeated interaction matters, the normal form may still be mathematically valid but no longer the clearest explanation.

Worked Mini-Example

In prisoner's dilemma, each person chooses confess or stay silent. The payoff in a cell is not caused by one choice alone; it is caused by the pair of choices.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to read one row or one column as if the other player were irrelevant. The whole point of the table is joint dependence.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for simultaneous moves, payoff matrices, or models where the exact timing is suppressed so strategic dependence can be seen cleanly.

Connected Concepts

The same primitive returns whenever the course asks whether a proposed behavior is compatible with incentives, beliefs, timing, or information. This representation deliberately throws away timing so the simultaneous strategic dependence is visible. That is why it fits dominance and Nash equilibrium so well, but also why later lectures need game trees when order and credibility matter.
