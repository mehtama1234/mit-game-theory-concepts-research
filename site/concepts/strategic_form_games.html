Strategic Form Games

A table where you write down all the players, all the moves each one can make, and the score each player gets from every possible combination of moves.

What real-world problem is this about?

Imagine you and your friend each need to choose something at the exact same moment, without knowing what the other chose. But once you both decide, what you get depends on both choices together. How do you show what's really going on? You need a table that captures the fact that nobody gets to see the other's choice before picking their own.

Why does this problem exist?

Strategic dependence means your payoff hangs on everyone's move at once, not just your own. A story told in words hides what each player can control and what result each combination of moves creates. A clear record — who chooses what, and what each combination delivers — is the only way to compare what each person should do.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A payoff function u_i(a_i, a_-i) assigns a score to player i. Here is what each symbol means: i is one player; a_i is that player's action; a_-i means all the other players' actions taken together; u_i is the number (payoff) that player i receives. The full action profile is (a_i, a_-i) — everyone's action at once — and that determines what score player i walks away with.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The deep version builds this slowly. A strategic-form game lists all players, all actions each can take, and a payoff number for each player at each action profile. This works because it forces you to write down the interaction: my move and your move together. Once written down in a table, the interaction becomes visible and checkable. You can ask: is my move a best response to your likely move? Is there a move pair where nobody wants to switch? Can I survive by mixing my choices randomly? The table is not optional when every payoff depends on everyone's action at once.

Mathematical Intuition

Think of a payoff function as a machine: you feed it everyone's action (the full profile), and it spits out a score for each player. The input is not a single choice; it is a complete picture of what each player does. The output can be different for each player — the same cell might look wonderful to player 1 but terrible to player 2. That is why you cannot evaluate a move in a vacuum; you need to see it inside the table.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

You cannot avoid this representation. Once every payoff depends on every player's action at once — on the full profile — you need a thing that holds all those dependencies in one place and makes them visible. Words scatter the information across a story. A table collects it so you can ask a clear question: if player 1 changes their move from A to B while player 2 stays the same, does player 1 do better or worse? The table lets you check this without confusion.

Why is this concept important?

By putting all players, all available moves, and all payoffs into one table, the course can ask sharp questions: Does one move beat another no matter what others do? If you think others will choose a certain way, what move gives you the highest score? Can everyone settle on choices where nobody wants to switch? The table holds all these questions at once without losing track of who affects whom.

What breaks without it?

If you only describe a game in words — a story about two people who want different things — you can talk about what seems good in theory, but you lack the structure to check your claim. You cannot easily compare what happens when player 1 chooses A versus B, or see which payoff pairs are possible. A table lets you check these things row by row and cell by cell.

Worked Mini-Example

Prisoner's dilemma: two players, each chooses confess or stay silent. One cell shows both chose confess; another shows both stayed silent; another shows player 1 confessed while player 2 stayed silent. Each cell holds two payoffs, one for each player. The payoff inside a cell comes from the pair of moves, not from one move in isolation.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often think a row of the table has one value and one meaning. That is wrong. A row shows what happens to player 1 across all of player 2's possible moves. Player 1's payoff in that row changes depending on which column you are in — which move player 2 picked. So a move cannot be judged as good or bad until you ask: good or bad against which opponent move? The table has multiple values per row because the opponent has choices.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The edge of what strategic form can show clearly is timing and hidden information. If one player moves before another and can see their choice, or if a player has private information that changes their payoffs, the table hides those wrinkles unless you carefully write them into your definition of what a move is. Later representations like game trees and Bayesian games handle those explicitly.

Common Misunderstanding

A common trap is to look at one row of the table and think the other player does not matter. That is wrong. Each row has multiple cells because the other player has multiple choices. You cannot say a move is good until you specify: good against which opponent move? The whole point of the table is to show that every payoff comes from both players choosing at once.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you see a problem where two or more agents choose at the same time (or nearly so), where each agent's payoff depends on everyone's choice, and where the exact order of moves or who knows what information is not the main point, you are looking at a situation that fits strategic form. Look for payoff matrices, simultaneous moves, or models where timing is compressed so that strategic dependence — the fact that my payoff hangs on your choice — becomes the main thing to understand.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: if a payoff is being discussed without naming every player's action in that cell, the strategic form is being misread. The table's purpose is to make interdependence explicit: each outcome is produced by a full action profile.

Connected Concepts

Strategic form is the foundation. Dominance (one move beats another for you no matter what they do), best response (the move that gives you the highest score against their expected choice), Nash equilibrium (a move pair where neither player wants to switch), and mixed strategies (flipping a coin on purpose to stay unpredictable) can all be checked by reading the table. When timing matters or you have information your opponent lacks, later chapters use trees and Bayesian games, which are extensions of this table representation.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Two suspects each choose confess or stay silent without seeing the other's move.

Walkthrough: One row alone does not determine either person's payoff. A payoff cell is reached only after both choices are named. The table forces the analyst to evaluate every pair of choices rather than telling a one-person story.

Lesson: Strategic form is the smallest object that keeps simultaneous interdependence visible.

Trap: Do not read a strategy as good or bad without naming what the other player does.

Model move: The modeling move is to make interdependence explicit. Write the players on different dimensions of the table and treat each payoff cell as the result of a full action profile.

Reader action: Fill one payoff cell at a time by saying both players' actions out loud. When you make a claim about an action, point to the column or row being held fixed so the one-person shortcut does not sneak back in.

Numbers / table check: Draw a 2 by 2 table with confess and silent for each suspect. Pick one cell and name both actions before reading either payoff. If you cannot identify both coordinates, the payoff is being read as a single-person outcome.

Boundary check: The table is the wrong tool if one suspect observes the other's choice, can revise later, or has private information that changes which rows and columns are available.

Strategic Form Games As A Clean Table Of Mutual Dependence

Start From Choices Made Without Seeing Each Other

A strategic form game is the course's compact table for situations where each player's payoff depends on the combination of everyone's choices. The everyday picture is two firms setting prices, two students choosing whether to contribute to a project, or two countries choosing policies without first seeing the other's exact move. The table is not decoration. It is the model's claim about who the players are, what choices they have, and what each outcome gives them.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Game theory needs a way to hold the whole strategic situation in one place. If my payoff depends only on my action, ordinary individual choice may be enough. If my payoff also depends on your action, the model needs a table or function that records the joint effect. Strategic form exists because dependence must be made visible before concepts like dominance, best response, and Nash equilibrium can be applied. The table turns a story into something that can be checked.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to fill in a payoff matrix as if it were obvious. The hardest part is often deciding what belongs in the table. Are players choosing prices, quantities, entry, effort, votes, or messages? Are payoffs money, utility, market share, reputation, or some mix? A strategic form game is only as good as those choices. A neat table can still be wrong if it hides timing, information, or a missing option.

Why It Matters Beyond Simple Matrices

Strategic form matters in economics, security, traffic routing, platform design, voting, and biology because it provides the first clean picture of mutual dependence. It also matters for topology because many equilibrium existence proofs start with a strategy space and payoff functions defined over action profiles. The fixed point is meaningful only after the strategic form has named the space and payoffs. A poor representation gives a poor map, even if the theorem is correct.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Security planning

A defender's best plan depends on the attacker's plan, and the attacker's payoff depends on the defense. A table makes that dependence inspectable.

Biology

Competing traits can be modeled by payoffs that depend on the traits around them. The table records fitness consequences from combinations, not isolated actions.
