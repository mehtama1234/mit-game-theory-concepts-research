choice and payoff

Utility

A number attached to each outcome that records how much someone wants it.

What real-world problem is this about?

When several people each chase what is best for them, you need to know whether one person will prefer outcome A or outcome B. But you cannot write down a math formula using the words 'I like this more' — math works with numbers. So you have to turn 'A is better than B' into numbers you can actually use in a calculation.

Why does this problem exist?

Utility exists because strategy and choice need calculation, and calculation needs numbers. If you can write down a single number for each outcome, then you can use arithmetic to find the best move, compare payoffs, or predict what rational people will do.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A preference is a ranking — 'I like pizza more than salad.' A utility function is a way to turn that ranking into numbers so math can handle it. If u is a utility function, then u(pizza) = 10 and u(salad) = 5 means 'pizza ranks higher.' The only rule is: a higher number must mean a higher ranking. The actual numbers are up to you — you could use 100 and 20 instead, and it works the same way for ordinary choice.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The full picture is this: the course starts with preferences (qualitative — 'I like A better'). Then it needs to do math, so it assigns numbers to outcomes (quantitative — 'A gets a 7, B gets a 3'). That mapping from outcomes to numbers is utility. Once numbers are in place, the course can use algebra: set up payoff tables, calculate expected values, find best responses by maximizing. The key constraint is that utility is a representation chosen for the job at hand. It is not a universal truth. Different problems might assign different utilities to the same outcome. When only choice matters (no risk), the numbers are arbitrary — you only need the ranking. When risk enters (lotteries, betting, insurance), the gaps between numbers start to matter, and the utility function has to preserve more structure so that averaging (expected value) works the way you want.

Mathematical Intuition

Utility is the machine that lets you swap a ranking into a number, so arithmetic can take over from words. Once you have numbers, you can ask: which move gives the highest expected payoff? The danger is forgetting what utility does and does not do — it preserves order and (under risk) preserves the right averages, but it does not tell you which utilities are 'real' or comparable across people.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

Tables, graphs, and equations need numbers to work. You cannot plug words into a formula. Utility is the translator from 'I want this' to a number, so the course can calculate what rational behavior looks like. It is the handle that moves from 'prefer' to 'has the larger payoff.'

Why is this concept important?

Once you turn a ranking into a number, you can put it in a table, calculate averages, compare payoffs, or find the best move when facing risk. Without numbers, the course could only describe outcomes in words. With numbers, the course can actually solve for what rational players do.

What breaks without it?

Without numbers, you cannot write a payoff table, calculate expected payoffs when outcomes are uncertain, or find best responses in a game. The course would be stuck describing outcomes without predicting behavior.

Worked Mini-Example

Suppose you rank: pizza first, salad second, soup third. You could assign utilities 10, 5, 0 (gap of 5). Or 100, 20, −7 (uneven gaps). For ordinary choice, both work the same — each ranks outcomes in the same order. But if you face a 50-50 bet between pizza and soup, the numbers matter: with 10 and 0 you get expected utility 5, but with 100 and −7 you get expected utility 46.5. So once risk enters, the gaps between your numbers carry meaning.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often think utility is money or happiness — something real that you can add up. Then they get confused when a utility of 100 does not mean 'twice as happy' as 50, or when one person's utility of 50 has nothing to do with another person's. The cure is to remember: utility is a number you pick to represent a ranking and make it computable.

Where The Idea Stops Working

Utility is a tool for one game or one problem at a time. It preserves your ranking and (when risk is involved) your preferences over bets. It does not automatically transfer to other people's preferences, and changing the numbers arbitrarily can break the model if risk is part of the question.

Common Misunderstanding

Students often think utility is like height or weight — a real quantity you measure with a ruler. The truth is that utility is whatever numbers you assign to preserve order. An outcome with utility 100 is not 'really' twice as good as one with 50; it just ranks higher. If you never involve risk or comparison across people, the scale is arbitrary.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Watch for any time a paper or problem turns a goal, payoff, preference, or outcome into a number that can be compared, added up, or optimized. That is utility at work.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: when a paper writes payoff numbers, ask what comparisons those numbers are allowed to support. If only ranking matters, many scales can represent the same choices. If spacing matters, the model has added stronger assumptions that need to be named.

Connected Concepts

Utility becomes the entry in a payoff table, the value at the end of a decision tree, the expected value in a lottery where outcomes are risky, or the private benefit in an auction. It is the glue between what someone wants and the math that predicts what someone will do.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A diner ranks pizza above salad above soup, but the course needs numbers for a payoff table.

Walkthrough: Utilities 10, 5, and 0 preserve the ranking. So do 100, 20, and -7 if the model only needs ordinary choice order. The exact units are less important than the fact that higher numbers stand for higher-ranked outcomes.

Lesson: Utility turns rankings into a computable handle while preserving the choice information the model needs.

Trap: Do not compare utility points like inches or dollars unless the model has added assumptions that justify that stronger interpretation.

Model move: The modeling move is to choose numbers that preserve the needed comparison while refusing extra meaning the model has not earned. Utility is a handle for the game, not a psychological measurement device.

Reader action: Create two different number scales that preserve the same ranking, then say which claims survive both scales. If a conclusion depends on the spacing between numbers, name the extra assumption that made spacing meaningful.

Numbers / table check: Put pizza, salad, and soup beside utilities 10, 5, 0, then beside 100, 20, -7. The ranking is unchanged in both columns. Any claim that pizza is exactly twice salad depends on spacing, not just ordinal utility.

Boundary check: The example changes when lotteries, risk attitudes, transfers, or interpersonal welfare comparisons matter, because then the spacing of numbers can carry real assumptions.

Utility As A Number That Carries A Ranking

Start From Ranking Before Measuring

Utility is easy to misunderstand because it uses numbers. The first idea is not that happiness has been measured like weight or height. The first idea is simpler: if a person ranks one outcome above another, the model can sometimes use numbers to carry that ranking. Higher utility means the model treats the outcome as better for that person inside the problem being studied. The number is a tool for comparison, not a claim that the person's inner life has been fully measured.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Game theory needs payoffs because strategic reasoning compares actions. A firm asks whether one price gives more profit than another. A bidder asks whether winning at a price is better than losing. A negotiator asks whether waiting is better than accepting. Utility gives the model a compact way to make those comparisons. Without it, later tools like best response and equilibrium would have nothing to maximize, and the model would only have loose words like wants or likes.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to treat utility as literal pleasure points. In this course, utility is usually a representation of ranked outcomes or payoffs. That difference matters. If the same ranking can be represented by many different number scales, the reader should not over-read the units. The important question is whether the numbers preserve the comparisons the model needs. A utility number is useful when it makes choices checkable without pretending to describe the whole person.

Why It Matters Beyond The First Model

Utility matters in economics, political science, law, computer science, public health, and design because models often need to compare tradeoffs. It also matters in topology and fixed-point existence proofs. A best-response map usually comes from players maximizing payoff over a choice set. If the utility function is continuous and the choice set has the right shape, the mathematics may prove a stable point exists. But the meaning of that point still depends on whether utility captured the right tradeoff.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Product design

A design model must say what users are trading off, such as speed, privacy, cost, or ease. Utility records the tradeoff so choices can be compared.

Topology and optimization

Existence results often need a well-behaved payoff function. The proof may be formal, but the payoff meaning comes from the modeled ranking.
