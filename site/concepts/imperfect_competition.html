Imperfect Competition

A market where a firm's choice of price or how much to sell affects its payoff—the score it gets—because customers respond, rivals respond, and both change what the firm earns. In a truly competitive market, one seller's choice doesn't move the price. Here, it does.

What real-world problem is this about?

Many real markets fall between two extremes: a street market with hundreds of sellers who can't move price alone, and a single monopolist who has no rivals. In between are markets with a handful of firms—each one big enough to matter, but each still facing pushback from the others. A coffee shop can raise its price and lose customers; an airline can add flights and depress fares. The question is: how do you figure out what each firm will do when all of them are playing this game at once?

Why does this problem exist?

Imperfect competition exists because the real world puts obstacles between firms and total control. A firm might have a patent, a unique location, a trusted brand, search costs that trap customers, or a product slightly different from rivals. Any of these can give one firm the power to move price or demand without losing all customers. Once a firm is not completely at the mercy of price—that is, once it can't be undercut by a rival with no consequence—the firm's payoff depends on both its own move and the other firms' moves. That is why you can't just ask what one firm wants in isolation; you have to model the interaction.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

You write a payoff function u_i(a_i, a_-i). Here is what each piece means: i is one player, one firm. a_i is what that firm chooses—a price, or a quantity to sell. a_-i means every other firm's choices bundled together. u_i is the payoff score that firm i gets from the whole situation. The reason this notation is necessary is that each firm's profit depends on both what it does and what all its rivals do at the same time. You cannot write down the profit formula without naming both parts.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The full version builds up from two edges. Perfect competition: one firm is so tiny that it cannot move the market price no matter what it does. Monopoly: one firm faces all the market demand alone. Imperfect competition is the real middle ground: a few firms each have some power to move price or demand, but they still have rivals whose choices push back. The lecture starts with demand curves and markups—single-firm topics—then moves to Cournot and Bertrand models, where each firm is a player whose payoff depends on both its own action and every rival's action. The mathematical step is turning the market into a game among strategic players.

Mathematical Intuition

The core intuition is strategic profit maximization. Profit is not just revenue minus cost—that is incomplete. Revenue depends on the market price, and market price depends on all firms' choices combined. A best-response curve shows the quantity or price that maximizes one firm's profit for each possible choice by its rival. If you plot both firms' best-response curves, their intersection is where each firm is picking the move that is best against what the other is doing.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

Words like competition and market power are too slippery. The math object forces precision. It must say: What do firms choose? How do those choices determine price or demand? How does profit change when a rival acts? A best-response rule makes the strategic interdependence explicit and checkable. Without it, you are just telling a story.

Why is this concept important?

Imperfect competition is the reason firms can charge a price above their cost. It explains why firms undercut each other. It explains entry, exit, and quantity wars. It shows why switching the model from quantity to price—seemingly just a naming choice—can change what price ends up being. This concept bridges from the math of payoff tables to the real-world behavior of firms that have market power but are not alone.

What breaks without it?

Without this idea, the course could not explain why a firm charges more than cost, why price wars happen, or why the representation matters—whether a firm chooses price or quantity. Everything would collapse into either a firm with zero power in perfect competition or absolute power in monopoly. The strategic layer would disappear.

Worked Mini-Example

Two coffee shops stand a block apart. Each can set its own price or decide how many drinks to brew. If one shop raises its price, some customers walk to the other shop. If one shop brews extra cups, the total supply rises and the price both shops can charge may fall. Neither shop can ignore the other. The best choice—whether to raise, lower, or hold price—is not just a cost calculation. It depends on what the other shop is doing and how customers split between them.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students get tangled up by mixing the story with the math. The story—coffee shops, tech firms, internet providers—matters for intuition. But the math object is abstract: a payoff map from firm choices to profits. The story is easy; the interlock of payoffs and choices is what matters. Students often want to solve a coffee-shop problem without setting up the payoff formulas first.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is model choice. Cournot and Bertrand are not laws of nature. They are different ways to represent what firms choose in a market. The lecture flags this by showing how the prediction can flip when you switch from quantity to price. In real markets, capacity constraints, product differences, customer-search friction, contracts, and regulation all affect which representation fits. The concept is strongest when you can defend why price, quantity, entry, capacity, or another variable is what firms actually choose.

Common Misunderstanding

The common mistake is to treat imperfect competition as monopoly with extra firms. The real point is that each firm has some power but not all power. The strategic object is not the demand curve facing one seller in a vacuum. The object is the interplay among firms: what each one does depends on what it expects the others to do, and what the others do depends on what each one expects them to do.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for a model where firms choose prices, quantities, entry decisions, capacity, product features, or some other action—knowing that rival choices will shift the payoff they get. If a paper asks for what one firm will do given rivals' choices—that is, a best response, not a single monopoly optimum—this concept is in play.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: if a firm is large enough that its choice changes rivals' payoffs or market price, single-firm optimization is too small. The key object becomes a reaction problem: each firm's best move depends on the other's move.

Connected Concepts

Imperfect competition connects to strategic-form games, best response, Nash equilibrium, and how changing the model representation affects the answer. The Cournot model—where firms choose quantity—and the Bertrand model—where firms choose price—show why the choice of variable matters. This also previews implicit cartels and the idea that firms might find a way to coordinate without talking.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Two coffee shops choose quantities or prices in the same neighborhood.

Walkthrough: If one shop expands output, market price may fall and change the other's best quantity. If one shop raises price, customers may shift. Each firm's choice is evaluated against the rival's choice, not in isolation.

Lesson: Imperfect competition turns market power into a strategic best-response problem.

Trap: Do not use the perfect-competition story when each firm is large enough to move the other firm's payoff.

Model move: The modeling move is to replace price-taking with reaction. Each firm's output or price is chosen while anticipating how the rival's choice affects demand, price, and profit.

Reader action: Write the firm's profit as depending on both its own choice and the rival's choice. Derive the best response against a named rival action before discussing market outcome. Then plot or list the rival's best response too, because the market prediction comes from their intersection, not from either firm alone.

Numbers / table check: List rival quantity 10, 20, 30 in one column and your profit-maximizing quantity beside each. That list is your reaction curve in table form. The market outcome needs both firms' reaction tables to point to each other.

Boundary check: The example changes with entry, capacity constraints, product differentiation, repeated competition, or regulation that makes the two-firm best-response picture too small.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Best Response card shows why a firm with market power cannot solve its problem as if rivals were scenery. Profit depends on the firm's own price or quantity and on rivals' choices, so each firm has a response curve rather than a standalone optimum. The market prediction comes from response curves fitting together. If entry, capacity, product differences, or repeated interaction matter, the two-firm calculation is only a first pass.

Imperfect Competition As Firms With Market Power And Rivals

Start From The Middle Between Monopoly And Perfect Competition

Imperfect competition is the middle case where a firm has some power over price or quantity, but it is not alone. A monopoly can act without a strategic rival inside the market. A perfectly competitive firm is too small to move the price. Imperfect competition is messier and more common: each firm matters, and each firm must think about how rivals respond.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

The course needs imperfect competition because real markets often have a few important firms. Profit depends on demand, cost, and rival behavior. A firm's best quantity in Cournot competition differs from its best price in Bertrand competition because the strategic variable changes. The concept exists to show that market power is not a single story. The model must say what firms choose and how those choices affect one another.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to say competition means price equals cost or monopoly means one firm, then force every market into those extremes. Many markets sit between them. Another mistake is to treat Cournot and Bertrand as interchangeable labels. Quantity competition and price competition can give different predictions. The representation is not cosmetic; it carries the strategic pressure. The analyst has to defend the market model before trusting the prediction that comes out of it.

Why It Matters Beyond Industrial Organization

Imperfect competition matters in tech platforms, hospitals, airlines, retail, labor markets, and energy markets. It also links to topology because equilibrium existence often depends on firms' strategy sets and payoff functions. The mathematical proof of a stable profile is useful only after the analyst has defended whether firms choose prices, quantities, capacity, quality, or some other strategic variable. That choice is a first-principles decision, not a technical afterthought, because it decides what rivalry means in the model.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Platform markets

A platform may choose fees, ranking rules, or access terms while rivals do the same. Market power and strategic dependence operate together.

Healthcare markets

Hospitals or insurers may have local market power while still facing rivals. Policy analysis must model both power and response.
