Mixed Strategies

Choosing randomly on purpose so opponents cannot exploit a predictable move.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is what to predict when choosing one pure action would make a player exploitable or when no pure-action equilibrium exists.

Why does this problem exist?

Mixed strategies exist because unpredictability can itself be strategic. Randomizing can make the opponent indifferent, preventing them from targeting a predictable move.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a probability distribution over states, types, or histories. The useful relation is `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).`. In plain language: theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version explains why randomization can be chosen deliberately. In some games, every predictable action can be exploited. A mixed strategy turns choice into a probability distribution over actions, and the equilibrium mixture often makes the opponent indifferent among the actions they are willing to use. The randomness is therefore strategic. It changes the opponent’s payoff comparison, which changes what can be stable.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is expected payoff under probabilities. If the opponent mixes, each of my pure actions has an expected payoff. In equilibrium, actions used with positive probability must usually earn the same expected payoff, otherwise I would shift probability toward the better one.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because pure actions cannot always carry stable predictions. A probability distribution over actions lets the player control predictability itself. The distribution becomes the strategy because it determines opponents’ expected payoffs before the random action is realized.

Why is this concept important?

Mixed strategies matter because predictability can be strategically costly. When no fixed action can be stable, deliberate randomization can make opponents indifferent enough that the overall behavior survives best-response checks.

What breaks without it?

Without mixed strategies, the course would falsely conclude that some games have no stable prediction simply because no pure action profile works. It would also miss why randomness can be chosen as discipline rather than noise.

Worked Mini-Example

In penalty kicks, always shooting left invites the goalie to dive left. Mixing left and right makes the goalie's guessing problem hard enough that neither side has a simple pure advantage.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by asking what the player “really” chooses. The answer is that the random device is part of the strategy; after it realizes an action, the distribution was still the planned commitment.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is interpretation and commitment. A mixed strategy is useful when randomization is credible and payoff-relevant, but not every observed frequency is an equilibrium mixture. The boundary is that the model predicts a distribution, not necessarily a visible sequence that looks random in a small sample. Interpreting observed play requires care about commitment, learning, and repeated sampling.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to think mixed strategy means confusion. In the model, randomization can be deliberate and exactly calculated.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for indifference equations, randomized play, or models where predictable behavior would be punished.

Connected Concepts

Mixed strategies connect Nash to zero-sum games, minimax, and expected utility. The player is not choosing one payoff; they are choosing a distribution whose expected consequences shape the opponent response.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A penalty kicker can shoot left or right, and the goalie can dive left or right.

Walkthrough: If the kicker always shoots left, the goalie can exploit that pattern. Randomizing makes the goalie indifferent when the probabilities are chosen correctly. The probabilities become part of the strategy because predictability itself is costly.

Lesson: Mixing is a way to make the opponent unable to target a fixed action.

Trap: Do not treat mixed strategy as a player being confused; it can be the disciplined solution.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Nash and Expected Utility cards explain why randomization is not noise. A player may mix only when the expected payoffs make several actions equally defensible against the opponent's own strategic uncertainty.
