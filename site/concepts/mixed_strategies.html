Mixed Strategies

Read the deep dive — seen on a real run →

You deliberately flip a coin or roll dice to decide your move, so the other player cannot predict what you will do and use that prediction against you.

What real-world problem is this about?

You face a jam: if you always pick one move, the other player learns this and beats you every time. But there might not be any single move you can stick to that is safe. So you need a way to stay unpredictable while still being smart about it.

Why does this problem exist?

Randomness itself becomes a weapon. If you pick randomly from a few moves in exactly the right proportions, you can make your opponent face a choice where neither of their moves is better than the other. They cannot exploit you because there is no pattern to find.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

When you do not know what the other player will do, you assign a probability—a chance—to each possibility. Then you add up your score across all those possibilities, weighted by how likely each is. In symbols: your expected payoff = (probability of outcome A) times (score from A) plus (probability of outcome B) times (score from B), and so on. This way you can compare your options by their average payoff, not just their best or worst case.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The deep lecture asks why someone would randomize on purpose. The answer: in some games, any pure move—any single action—can be exploited. A mixed strategy turns your decision into a probability distribution over actions. The equilibrium mixture often makes your opponent indifferent among the moves they are willing to use. Randomness is therefore a choice, not noise. It changes your opponent's payoff arithmetic, which changes what can stay stable.

Mathematical Intuition

The intuition comes from expected payoff under probabilities. If your opponent mixes, then each pure action you might pick has an expected payoff. In equilibrium, actions that you use with positive probability must usually earn the same expected payoff. If one action were better, you would shift your probability weight toward it, which would make your opponent want to shift too, breaking equilibrium.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object must exist because pure actions do not always allow stable predictions. A probability distribution over actions lets you control your own predictability. The distribution becomes your strategy because it decides your opponent's expected payoffs before the random roll happens.

Why is this concept important?

If you are predictable, people punish you. When no pure action—no single move you always pick—can be stable, deliberate mixing is the answer. You randomize in the exact right way so that your opponent has no profitable switch, because you have made all their options equally good.

What breaks without it?

The course would wrongly claim some games have no stable answer just because players cannot both pick fixed pure moves and stand still. It would also miss that randomness can be a choice, not just noise or confusion.

Worked Mini-Example

In a penalty kick, if the shooter always shoots left, the goalie dives left and saves it. If the shooter mixes—sometimes left, sometimes right—in the right ratio (say 50-50), then diving left saves as many as diving right does, on average. The goalie cannot gain by picking a side, so both the shooter's left-shot and right-shot remain part of the equilibrium.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students ask: what does the player actually choose? The answer: the player chooses the distribution itself. Once the random device rolls—once an action happens—the distribution was already the commitment. The mix is the strategy, not a mistake.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is about what a mixed strategy really means and when it applies. A mixed strategy makes sense when randomization is credible and affects payoffs, but not every random-looking sequence is an equilibrium mixture. The model predicts a distribution, not necessarily a visible pattern in a few plays. Applying this to real play requires you to think about whether people can actually commit to the strategy, learn over time, and how many attempts you observe.

Common Misunderstanding

Many people think mixing means you do not know what you are doing. In the model, you deliberately choose the distribution and commit to it. The randomness is calculated and intentional, not accidental.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for indifference equations—places where the other player has no reason to prefer one response to another. Look for randomized play or models where any predictable behavior would be punished by someone who sees it.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: randomization is doing strategic work when predictability would be punished. The usual clue is an indifference equation: probabilities are chosen so the opponent cannot profit by targeting one pure action over another. Always check which player's payoffs are being equalized.

Connected Concepts

Mixed strategies tie together Nash equilibrium (a stable resting point where no single player wants to move), zero-sum games (one player's gain is the other's loss), minimax (the worst average result you can guarantee yourself), and expected utility (what you expect to win on average). When you mix, you are not picking one payoff; you are choosing a distribution whose average results shape what the opponent can do.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A penalty kicker can shoot left or right, and the goalie can dive left or right.

Walkthrough: If the kicker always shoots left, the goalie can exploit that pattern. Randomizing makes the goalie indifferent when the probabilities are chosen correctly. The probabilities become part of the strategy because predictability itself is costly.

Lesson: Mixing is a way to make the opponent unable to target a fixed action.

Trap: Do not treat mixed strategy as a player being confused; it can be the disciplined solution.

Model move: The modeling move is to make predictability itself part of the payoff problem. Probabilities are chosen so the opponent cannot gain by targeting a known pure action.

Reader action: Identify whose payoffs must be made equal, then solve for the other player's probability. After solving, check that the probability lies between zero and one and that it equalizes the intended player, not the wrong side. Finally, ask what pure action would become exploitable if the probabilities drifted.

Numbers / table check: Write the goalie's expected payoff from diving left and diving right as functions of the kicker's left probability p. Set those two expressions equal. If the solution is p = 1/2, check that both goalie actions really give the same payoff at that probability.

Boundary check: The example changes if one side has better information, unequal payoffs, commitment power, or physical limits that make the randomization probabilities unavailable.

Equation Walkthroughs

The calculation usually asks which probability makes the other player indifferent between two actions. Indifference is the signal that neither pure action can be targeted. Mixed Strategies As Randomness With A Strategic Job

Start From Being Too Predictable

A mixed strategy means choosing randomly according to a planned probability. The everyday reason is not confusion. It is that predictability can be exploited. A tennis player who always serves left lets the returner prepare. A security team that always patrols the same route invites avoidance. Randomness can be useful when the other side benefits from knowing exactly what you will do.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Some games have no stable prediction in pure actions. If one player picks rock, the other wants paper; if paper, then scissors; if scissors, then rock. The cycle means no fixed pure move survives. Mixed strategies let the model search for stability in probabilities instead. A player may randomize so that the opponent is kept indifferent among certain actions, removing the profitable predictable response.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to treat mixing as a player being careless or unable to decide. In the model, mixing can be deliberate. Another mistake is to think any random probabilities work. Equilibrium mixing has a reason: the probabilities must make opponents willing to play the actions they are supposed to use. The exact numbers carry incentive work. They are not decorative uncertainty pasted onto a deterministic story.

Why It Matters Beyond Games Of Chance

Mixed strategies matter in sports, inspections, cybersecurity, military planning, pricing, and platform moderation. They also matter for topology because probability mixtures can make strategy sets convex, which is one reason fixed-point existence theorems can apply. The mathematical convenience has a plain meaning: the player is allowed to choose a probability plan rather than only a single action. That expanded space can create a stable point where no pure action profile could.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Inspections

A regulator may randomize inspection times so firms cannot safely hide violations around a known schedule. The randomness creates discipline because the firm cannot make one clean plan around a predictable visit.

Cyber defense

Rotating defenses can prevent attackers from exploiting a fixed pattern. The mix must still fit the cost and protection tradeoff, because random movement without purpose can waste effort and leave weak spots exposed.
