Types And Beliefs

Types store hidden information; beliefs describe what others think those types might be.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is keeping track of hidden facts and what players think those facts might be.

Why does this problem exist?

Types and beliefs exist because private information has to be represented somewhere. A type stores what one player knows; a belief stores what others think is likely.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a probability distribution over states, types, or histories. The useful relation is `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).`. In plain language: theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version separates the hidden fact from the probability story about that fact. A type is the private information that changes payoffs or available actions. A belief is another player’s probability distribution over possible types or states. This distinction is crucial because a sender may know the type while a receiver only has a belief, and strategic behavior can be designed to change that belief.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is a state space with probability weights. The type indexes which payoff function or information situation is true; the belief says how likely each index seems to another player. Updating changes the weights after observations.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because uncertainty has two sides: the hidden state and someone’s probability over it. Types represent the state; beliefs represent the uncertainty. Keeping them separate lets the course analyze signaling, updating, and type-contingent strategy without confusing truth with perception.

Why is this concept important?

Types and beliefs matter because they split hidden information into two objects: what the world could be and what players think the world could be. That separation keeps uncertainty from becoming a vague verbal patch.

What breaks without it?

Without types and beliefs, private-information models would not say who knows what or how uncertainty enters payoffs. The analyst could not distinguish a high-value bidder from a low-value bidder or a receiver belief from sender truth.

Worked Mini-Example

In an auction, a bidder's value is their type. Other bidders do not see it, but they hold beliefs about the distribution of possible values.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by saying “the player believes the type” as if belief and type were the same thing. One is the hidden state; the other is someone’s uncertain view of it.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is discipline on beliefs. Beliefs are necessary, but they should come from priors, signals, observations, or equilibrium restrictions rather than being chosen to rescue any story. The lecture boundary is that beliefs should be inspectable. A model that can support any action by inventing a convenient belief has stopped explaining. The belief structure must connect to priors, information, or equilibrium restrictions.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to blur type and action. A type is hidden information; an action is a choice that may reveal or conceal it.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for any model where behavior depends on values, costs, quality, or information that only one side observes.

Connected Concepts

Types and beliefs connect Bayesian games, Bayesian Nash, auctions, signaling, cheap talk, and PBE. They are also the reason expected utility keeps reappearing in private-information settings.
