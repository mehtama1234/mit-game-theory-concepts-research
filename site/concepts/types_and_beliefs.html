Types And Beliefs

Types are hidden facts about a player—like their value, cost, or strength—that only they know. Beliefs are what the other players think about those hidden facts, written as probabilities. In strategic reasoning, we use types to record the secret, and beliefs to record the guess.

What real-world problem is this about?

When someone knows something you do not, you have to guess what they know and what they will do based on that hidden information. The problem is tracking both the secret and everyone's best guess about it in a way that explains behavior.

Why does this problem exist?

Types and beliefs come from a simple fact: information in real choices is often one-sided. One player knows their own strength or value; everyone else does not. To model that, we need an object for what one player privately knows (the type) and an object for what others think they know (the belief).

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a list of probabilities—what is the chance the hidden fact is one value versus another? If theta stands for the hidden fact and p(theta) is the probability, then expected payoff is the sum: for each possible fact, multiply its payoff by its probability and add them all up. This averages the payoff over all the guesses.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture separates a hidden fact from the guess about it. A type is the private information you own—a value, a cost, a strength. A belief is another player's probability story about what that type might be. The distinction matters because you might know your type is high-value, but others might believe it is low, and you can use an action to shift their belief. Signaling and updating both depend on this split.

Mathematical Intuition

Think of a type as an index into a list of payoff functions. Each index (each type) gives a different payoff for the same action. A belief says: this index is likely, that index is less likely. When players observe actions, they update the likelihoods. The math is just: assign each index a weight (probability), compute the expected payoff, and play the action that maximizes it.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

Uncertainty has two faces: what the world actually is, and what someone thinks the world is. Types capture the first; beliefs capture the second. To analyze strategic moves—like lying, signaling, or updating—you must keep them separate. If you blur them, you cannot ask whether a move reveals truth or hides it.

Why is this concept important?

Types and beliefs split private information into two clear pieces: what is actually hidden, and what players think is hidden. Without this split, you would have no way to distinguish whether a player is strategic (hiding information) or just unlucky (information has not arrived yet), and no way to ask if a guess is incentive-compatible.

What breaks without it?

Without types and beliefs, you cannot build a model where one side has secret information and the other side reacts to it. You could not explain why one bidder bids high and another low, or why a sender might lie and a receiver might not trust them. You would lose the ability to ask whether a guess can be updated or if lies can be caught.

Worked Mini-Example

In an auction, each bidder secretly knows what the item is worth to them—that is their type. Everyone else sees a bidder but not their value. Other bidders form a belief: maybe 50% of the time a bidder values it at $10, and 50% at $20. This belief determines their bidding strategy.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students mix up the statement: the player believes the type. But type is the reality; belief is the guess. The player knows their own type. The other player has a belief about what type the first player has. Conflating them breaks the model because you lose the distinction between what one person knows and what another person guesses.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary on beliefs is accountability. Beliefs are required, but they must come from a prior belief, signals observed, information received, or equilibrium logic—not invented to fit a conclusion. A model that supports any outcome by choosing beliefs at will is not explaining, it is storytelling. Beliefs must connect to a source.

Common Misunderstanding

A common mix-up is treating a type as an action. A type is the secret you carry; an action is the move you choose. A type might be your value or your strength, which does not change during the game. An action is what you do, which reveals or hides your type.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for any situation where behavior depends on something one side knows but the other does not: a company's profit or strength, a worker's ability, a customer's willingness to pay, the condition of an asset, or a person's type or preference. That is where types and beliefs live.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: type is the hidden payoff-relevant fact; belief is someone else's probability story about that fact. Keep them separate. A model becomes informative when observations or equilibrium restrictions discipline how beliefs move. If beliefs can be chosen freely, the explanation is too loose. Ask what pins them down and which observed action should actually change them.

Connected Concepts

Types and beliefs connect many topics: Bayesian games (games with private types), Bayesian Nash equilibrium (equilibrium when types are uncertain), auctions (bidders have private values), signaling (you choose actions to reveal or hide your type), cheap talk (words that may or may not be true), and PBE (perfect Bayesian equilibrium—beliefs and strategy both satisfy rationality).

Setup: In an auction, each bidder privately knows how much the object is worth to them.

Walkthrough: That private value is the bidder's type. Other bidders do not observe it, so they hold beliefs over possible types. Their choices respond to that probability story rather than to a known payoff table.

Lesson: Types name hidden payoff-relevant facts, and beliefs describe how others reason about those facts.

Trap: Do not treat a type as a personality label; it is the private information that changes payoffs or feasible choices.

Model move: The modeling move is to name the hidden payoff-relevant fact and then ask how other players form beliefs over its possible values. Type is the model's explicit name for that fact.

Reader action: Separate the hidden fact from the belief about that fact. Then ask what observation would move the belief and whether the model has actually included that observation. If nothing can move the belief, say whether it is a prior assumption rather than learned information.

Numbers / table check: Use three columns: hidden fact, prior belief, possible observation. For an auction, value is the hidden fact, the distribution is the prior, and a bid may be an observation. Do not put personality labels in the type column unless they change payoffs or information.

Boundary check: The example breaks if the supposed type does not actually change incentives, or if beliefs over types are chosen only to support the desired conclusion.

Types And Beliefs As Naming Hidden Facts And Guesses

Start From What One Side Cannot See

Types and beliefs separate two everyday things that are often mixed together. A type is the hidden fact: a bidder's value, a seller's quality, a worker's ability, or a firm's cost. A belief is someone else's probability story about that hidden fact. The distinction matters because truth and guess are not the same. The model needs both the hidden state and the way players reason about it.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Private information changes strategy. If a player knows their own type, their action may depend on it. If others do not know that type, they must act using beliefs. Types and beliefs exist because the course needs a clean place to store hidden information and a clean way to represent uncertainty about it. Without them, Bayesian games, signaling, screening, and perfect Bayesian equilibrium would collapse into vague talk about not knowing.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to treat type as a personality category or belief as a casual opinion. In the model, a type is the payoff-relevant private information, and a belief is a probability distribution used for choice. Another mistake is to choose types after seeing the desired answer. If the type space is badly chosen, the equilibrium may be internally consistent but explain the wrong hidden fact.

Why It Matters Beyond Economics

Types and beliefs matter in medicine, hiring, insurance, cybersecurity, law, and machine learning because decisions often turn on hidden states. They also matter in topology-based equilibrium proofs because beliefs can be part of the strategy space or state space. A fixed point over beliefs and actions is only meaningful when the type model honestly captures the uncertainty people face. The reader should always ask whether the hidden fact is real, payoff-relevant, and visible to the person whose type the model names.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Medicine

A doctor may not know a patient's hidden condition and must use test results as evidence. The condition is like a type; the diagnosis is a belief.

Cybersecurity

A defender may not know whether an observed actor is a casual user, bot, or attacker. The response depends on beliefs about that hidden type.
