Auctions

Read the deep dive — seen on a real run →

A system of rules for deciding who gets an object when several people want it and each person secretly knows how much they value it.

What real-world problem is this about?

Suppose you want to sell your bicycle. Buyers know their own willingness to pay, but you do not. Also, each buyer does not know if someone else values the bike more. How do you run the sale so buyers bid honestly and you get a fair price?

Why does this problem exist?

Auctions solve a problem: when people have private information about what something is worth to them, how do you make them reveal it? The answer is rules. You set up a rule that connects bids to payment and winning. That rule changes what each bidder's best move is. Bids then become the actual competition—they reveal hidden preferences through the incentives the rule creates.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

If someone bids truthfully, they should get at least as much satisfaction as they would get by lying. In symbols: `u_i(truth) >= u_i(lie)` for any lie they might tell. In plain terms: when the rules are set up right, telling the truth should make you happier than any false bid would.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

Here is the full picture. A seller has an object and wants to allocate it fairly and collect payment. Buyers know their own values but the seller does not. The seller cannot ask buyers directly because buyers would lie if it helped them. So the seller designs an auction rule. The rule specifies: for each possible set of bids, who wins and what do they pay? That rule creates an incentive structure. In a first-price auction, your own bid determines your payment if you win, so you shade your bid below your true value. In a second-price auction, you pay the second-highest bid, which separates the winning choice from the payment, and under typical assumptions this can make truthful bidding attractive. The lecture evaluates auctions by whether bidders actually want to deviate from the proposed behavior—not by whether the designer's story sounds nice.

Mathematical Intuition

Think of each bidder's choice as an optimization problem. When I bid higher, I win more often, but I pay more if I win. When I bid lower, I lose more often, but I pay less if I do win. I choose the bid that maximizes my expected happiness—the average happiness weighted by my belief about whether I will win. My best bid depends on my value, my belief about others, and the payment rule.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

Allocation and payment are linked. If I bid high, I am more likely to win but I also might pay more. My payoff depends on both. I cannot analyze my choice unless I model: the values everyone has, the bid space, the allocation rule, the payment rule, my beliefs about others, and how payoffs work. Without all of these pieces, you cannot tell if someone bid their truth, shaded their value, or distorted a bid to manipulate what they believe others will do.

Why is this concept important?

Auctions show how to design rules that make people's private information come out in their bids. This is one of the clearest examples in the course of how a rule system actually changes behavior. Without understanding auctions, you cannot see how a seller uses rules to get what they want: the object goes to the person who values it most, or the seller makes the most money, or people bid truthfully.

What breaks without it?

If we skip auctions, we miss the most direct example of how to take hidden information and turn it into visible bids through smart rule design. We also could not explain why bidders do not bid their true value in all auction types, or why different payment rules lead to different bids from the same person, or why some auction designs encourage honesty while others encourage bluffing.

Worked Mini-Example

Imagine you value a coffee maker at 100 dollars. In a first-price auction, if you bid 100 and win, you get zero joy—you paid exactly its value. If you bid 70 and win, you get 30 dollars of joy left over. But if you bid 70, someone else might win by bidding 75. So you face a trade-off. In a second-price auction, something different happens: you pay what the second-highest bidder bid, not your own bid. So if you bid 100 and the next-highest bid is 60, you win but pay only 60. That changes everything. Now truthful bidding might be your best move because winning and paying a lower price are separate. The format itself reshapes your incentive.

Where Students Get Stuck

A trap is thinking of bids as if they are confessions of value. They are not. Bids are actions that respond to incentives. The payment rule, the belief about what others will do, and the risk of losing all shape the bid. The same bidder will bid differently under different rules.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The assumptions matter. This model assumes private values—each person knows their own value and it does not depend on what others think. But the real world has common values—the same object is worth more to everyone if it turns out to be rare. Risk aversion, budget limits, entry fees, collusion, and shared signals all change the incentive. A claim that truthful bidding works in one setting may break in another. The rule is: always check the payment rule and what bidders know before you guess how they will bid.

Common Misunderstanding

A common mistake is to think the bidder who truly values the object most will always bid the highest amount in every auction type. That is false. The payment rule changes how much they want to win and at what cost.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you see a system where someone has private information and a rule converts their choice into an outcome and a payment, you are seeing an auction.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: a bid has two effects at once: it changes the chance of winning and the surplus if winning. Read the payment rule before drawing conclusions, because first-price, second-price, and platform formats change incentives. A bid is an action under rules, not a value confession.

Connected Concepts

Auctions tie together several course concepts. They use Bayesian games (games where people have private information), expected utility (the math of choosing when outcomes are uncertain), and incentive compatibility (designing rules so people want to follow them). When platforms run online ad auctions, they use the exact same logic: instead of allocating one object, they allocate ranked positions, but the bidding and payment rules still shape who wins and what they pay.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A bidder in a first-price auction values the object at 100.

Walkthrough: Bidding 100 may win often but leaves no surplus when it wins. Bidding lower wins less often but leaves positive surplus when it does win. The bidder compares both the probability of winning and the payoff conditional on winning.

Lesson: Auction strategy is a tradeoff between chance of winning and surplus if winning.

Trap: Do not analyze the bid without the payment rule; first-price and second-price formats change incentives.

Model move: The modeling move is to split a bid into two effects: changing the chance of winning and changing the payoff conditional on winning. Auction format determines that tradeoff.

Reader action: For a small bid increase, write what happens to the chance of winning and what happens to surplus if winning. If you cannot name both effects, you are not yet analyzing the auction rule. Repeat the exercise after changing the payment rule to see why formats matter.

Numbers / table check: Compare bid 70 and bid 80 for value 100. If bid 70 wins with chance 0.4, surplus if winning is 30. If bid 80 wins with chance 0.6, surplus is 20. The bid comparison needs both probability and surplus columns.

Boundary check: The example is too clean if bidders are budget constrained, risk averse, asymmetric, colluding, or uncertain about whether the object has common value.

Auctions As Rules For Turning Private Values Into Bids

Start From A Simple Sale With Hidden Values

An auction is a rule for deciding who gets an object and what they pay when bidders know their own values better than the seller does. The everyday picture is familiar: people want the same item, but each person has a different willingness to pay. The auction rule turns those private values into public bids. The important point is that a bid is not the same thing as a value. It is an action shaped by the payment rule and by competition.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

If a seller knew everyone's value, selling would be easier. With private values, the rule must draw information out through incentives. In a first-price auction, a high bid raises the chance of winning but also raises the price paid if the bidder wins. In a second-price auction, the payment rule changes that tradeoff. Auction theory exists because allocation, payment, belief, and strategic shading have to be studied together.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to think the highest-value bidder simply says the true value in every auction. That can be wrong. A bidder asks what bid gives the best expected surplus, not what bid sounds honest. Different rules change that calculation. Truthful bidding may be a result under special rules, not a natural default. The course makes the rule visible so the reader can see why bidding changes when payment changes.

Why It Matters Beyond Selling Objects

Auctions matter anywhere a scarce thing must be assigned under private information: spectrum licenses, online ads, school seats, organ exchange priorities, electricity markets, procurement contracts, and platform rankings. The larger lesson is that rules create behavior. A designer who changes the payment rule, reserve price, ranking formula, or information shown to bidders may change both revenue and who receives the object.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Public procurement

Governments use bidding rules to buy work from firms with private costs. The rule affects both the winning firm and how much cost information the bids reveal.

Online platforms

Ad slots and rankings are sold through repeated auctions. The design must account for clicks, quality, private value, and strategic bid shading.
