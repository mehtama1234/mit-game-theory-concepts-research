Expected Utility

Averaging possible payoffs by how likely they are.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is choosing when the outcome is uncertain: a bid might win or lose, a risky action might pay off or fail, and a type may be one of several possibilities.

Why does this problem exist?

This exists because people must act before uncertainty is resolved. A choice must be judged by the whole lottery of possible outcomes, not only by the result that happens afterward.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a probability distribution over states, types, or histories. The useful relation is `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).`. In plain language: theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

Why is this concept important?

This concept matters because it turns a loose strategic story into a checkable claim about what someone would actually choose.

What breaks without it?

Without this concept, the analysis can name the players and choices but cannot tell whether the proposed behavior survives individual incentives. What breaks without it is the ability to compare risky strategies before the uncertainty is resolved. Auctions, Bayesian games, and signaling all need this averaging move because players act while still unsure which world they are in.

Worked Mini-Example

A bidder may win with probability 1/3 and earn 90, or lose with probability 2/3 and earn 0. Expected utility asks whether that gamble beats another bid once all possible results are counted.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to average dollars automatically. If people care about risk, the utility of money may not rise one-for-one with dollars.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for private information, lotteries, risk, or unknown types where the model has to average payoffs across possible worlds.

Connected Concepts

The same primitive returns whenever the course asks whether a proposed behavior is compatible with incentives, beliefs, timing, or information. This is the bridge from individual decision-making to Bayesian games and auctions. Once types or values are hidden, players choose against a probability-weighted menu of possible worlds rather than a single known payoff table.
