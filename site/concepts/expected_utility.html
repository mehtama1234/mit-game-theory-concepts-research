Expected Utility

Averaging possible payoffs by how likely they are.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is choosing when the outcome is uncertain: a bid might win or lose, a risky action might pay off or fail, and a type may be one of several possibilities.

Why does this problem exist?

This exists because people must act before uncertainty is resolved. A choice must be judged by the whole lottery of possible outcomes, not only by the result that happens afterward.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a probability distribution over states, types, or histories. The useful relation is `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).`. In plain language: theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version begins when a player chooses before knowing which outcome will occur. The course cannot use the best or worst possible payoff alone, because the action leads to a lottery. Expected utility says: list the possible outcomes, attach probabilities, translate each outcome into utility, and average. That operation gives the player one comparison number for an uncertain action. Later private-information models use the same move: a bidder or player chooses while averaging over possible types or rival values.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is weighted bookkeeping. Each possible payoff counts in proportion to how likely that state is. The expectation symbol is not magic; it is the sum of utility in each possible world multiplied by the probability of that world.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because uncertainty creates bundles of possible outcomes. A player choosing now cannot optimize separately in each future state; they need one comparison for the whole risky action. Expected utility is that comparison, built by weighting each consequence by the probability assigned before the outcome is known.

Why is this concept important?

Expected utility matters because strategic choice often happens before uncertainty resolves. A player must compare actions that lead to several possible outcomes, so the course needs a way to combine payoff and likelihood in one decision object.

What breaks without it?

Without expected utility, Bayesian games and auctions would have no disciplined way to evaluate actions under unknown types or rival values. The model would either ignore risk or smuggle in an unspecified attitude toward it.

Worked Mini-Example

A bidder may win with probability 1/3 and earn 90, or lose with probability 2/3 and earn 0. Expected utility asks whether that gamble beats another bid once all possible results are counted.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by averaging dollar outcomes when the model needs utility outcomes, or by forgetting that the probabilities are beliefs from the player’s point of view rather than facts known after the uncertainty resolves.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary appears when probabilities or utility over risk are misspecified. Expected utility is a disciplined average, but it does not by itself prove the player has correct beliefs or risk-neutral attitudes.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to average dollars automatically. If people care about risk, the utility of money may not rise one-for-one with dollars.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for private information, lotteries, risk, or unknown types where the model has to average payoffs across possible worlds.

Connected Concepts

Expected utility is the arithmetic behind mixed strategies, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, auctions, and belief updating. Whenever the course says a type or action is chosen before hidden information is known, this primitive is working underneath.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A bidder can submit a bid that wins with probability one third and earns 90 if it wins.

Walkthrough: The expected utility calculation counts both possible outcomes before the uncertainty resolves. Winning contributes one third times the utility of 90. Losing contributes two thirds times the utility of 0. The action is compared by the weighted bundle, not by the best possible result alone.

Lesson: Expected utility is the arithmetic that lets a player choose while still admitting they do not know which state will happen.

Trap: Do not average raw dollars when risk attitudes matter; the formula averages utility in each possible state.
