Finitely Repeated Games

Playing a stage game a known limited number of times.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is whether repeating a game for a known number of rounds changes behavior relative to playing once.

Why does this problem exist?

Finite repetition exists because the end of the relationship can unravel incentives. If everyone knows the last round has no future punishment, that affects the round before it, and so on.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Backward induction works by thinking about the end first. Imagine the last period: no rounds follow it, so there are no future consequences. Once we know what happens in the last period, we can figure out what happens in the period before—knowing the endpoint lets us reason backward. The value at any point (call it V) is the best outcome you can get from making the smartest choice moving forward from that moment. At each decision point, a player picks the action that leads to the branch with the highest value of what comes next. This backward reasoning from the known end is the core idea.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version is a lesson about known endings. Repeating a stage game may seem like it should allow punishment and trust, but if the final period is known, the last period has no later punishment. If the last period unravels to the one-shot equilibrium, the next-to-last period can lose its punishment power too, and the logic can roll backward.

Mathematical Intuition

The key intuition is that the end anchors everything else. The final period is a fixed point: it happens the same way regardless of what came before (since nothing comes after it). That outcome in the final period becomes the baseline that shapes all earlier periods. When a player is deciding what to do in period 5 of a 10-round game, they have to think: what will happen in period 6 and beyond? But if they can trace the logic all the way to period 10 and see it's locked in, then periods 6–10 look predictable. That predictability means the punishment threats you might try to make ("if you defect now, I'll punish you later") sound unconvincing—later is already determined. The backward chain from a known endpoint can therefore turn what seems like future discipline into an empty promise.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because a known horizon changes every period’s incentives. The model needs to preserve the number of remaining rounds so last-period logic can be pulled backward. A finite repeated game is not just many one-shot games; it is a time-indexed strategic object.

Why is this concept important?

Finitely repeated games matter because they teach a negative lesson: repeating a game does not automatically create cooperation. If the last round is known, the absence of future punishment can unravel incentives backward.

What breaks without it?

Without finite repetition, students might overgeneralize from ongoing relationships and assume history always disciplines behavior. The course needs the finite case to show why the endpoint is strategically powerful.

Worked Mini-Example

If two firms know they will compete for exactly ten days, cheating on day ten cannot be punished on day eleven. That can make day nine punishment less credible too.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by counting repetitions rather than asking whether the relationship has a strategic future at each date. Ten known rounds and an ongoing relationship are different objects.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the known endpoint. The finite repeated-game unraveling logic changes when the horizon is uncertain, reputational types exist, or players cannot observe deviations. The classroom value of the finite case is that it blocks a lazy intuition about repetition. More rounds do not automatically mean more cooperation; what matters is whether each round still has a future consequence that can discipline current behavior. That backward pressure is the lecture’s main warning about finite horizons.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to assume repetition automatically creates cooperation. A known last round can destroy that logic.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for known horizons and unraveling arguments that start at the final period.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: a known final round can erase discipline before the final round arrives. Solve the last period first, then move backward. Repetition helps only when future rewards or punishments still have force at the moment of temptation. If cooperation survives, identify the extra post-game or reputation force. Otherwise the endpoint logic returns.

Connected Concepts

Finite repetition connects backward induction to infinite repetition. It is the contrast case that makes discounting and no-known-final-round assumptions meaningful later.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Two firms compete on price for exactly ten days, and both firms know the relationship ends after day ten.

Walkthrough: On day ten, cheating cannot be punished on day eleven because there is no day eleven. Once day ten cooperation loses discipline, day nine threats become weaker too. The logic can unravel backward through the finite horizon.

Lesson: A known endpoint can destroy future punishment before the endpoint arrives.

Trap: Do not assume repetition automatically creates cooperation when the ending is common knowledge.

Model move: The modeling move is to start at the known final round and reason backward. If the last round has no future discipline, earlier threats can lose force by the same logic.

Reader action: Solve the last round first and write what future punishment is unavailable there. Then move to the next-to-last round and ask whether the solved final round can still discipline behavior. Continue backward until you find where cooperation loses support or why an added assumption stops the unraveling.

Numbers / table check: Create a ten-row timeline and start at row 10. If defection beats cooperation in row 10, mark row 10 solved. Then ask row 9 whether punishment in row 10 is still available. The table shows the unraveling instead of merely naming it.

Boundary check: The unraveling conclusion changes if the endpoint is uncertain, players care about reputation after the model ends, or punishment can occur outside the repeated game.

Finitely Repeated Games As The Last Round Pushing Backward

Start From A Relationship With A Known End

A finitely repeated game is a repeated relationship with a known last round. People meet more than once, but everyone knows when the interaction stops. That known ending changes the logic. In the last round, there is no future punishment left inside the model. If cooperation requires fear of later punishment, the last round may unravel first, and earlier rounds can be affected by that future endpoint.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Repeated play can support cooperation when the future matters, but a known final period weakens that force. The concept exists because the course needs to separate ongoing relationships from relationships with a fixed end. A worker near final review, a firm near the last contract, or a player near the final round may face different incentives. The end of the relationship can reach backward into present behavior.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to assume that repetition always helps cooperation. If everyone knows the final round and the stage game has a unique noncooperative equilibrium, backward reasoning can make cooperation unravel. Another mistake is to apply the stark unraveling result without checking the assumptions. Reputation, incomplete information, social norms, outside enforcement, or multiple equilibria can change the conclusion. The result is strongest when the endpoint is truly known, behavior is observed, and no outside relationship carries consequences beyond the modeled last round.

Why It Matters Beyond Repeated Prisoners Dilemmas

Finitely repeated games matter in employment contracts, supply relationships, political terms, school projects, and temporary alliances. They also clarify the boundary of fixed-point existence thinking. A stable plan in a repeated game must respect the known time horizon. The topology of the strategy space cannot make future punishment meaningful after the modeled future has ended. The lesson is practical: when a relationship has a visible end, the analyst should ask which incentives survive the end and which incentives vanish.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Term-limited politics

A politician facing a final term may have weaker future electoral discipline. The known endpoint changes present incentives.

Project teams

A group with a final deadline may cooperate early but slack near the end if no future response remains inside the relationship.
