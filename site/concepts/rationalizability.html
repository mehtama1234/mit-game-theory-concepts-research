Rationalizability

A strategy is plausible if it can be optimal for some reasonable belief about others.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is deciding which strategies can be defended as sensible without requiring everyone to land on one exact equilibrium.

Why does this problem exist?

This exists because a strategy can be rational under some belief even if it is not part of the final equilibrium prediction. The course needs a wider plausibility test than Nash equilibrium.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a probability distribution over states, types, or histories. The useful relation is `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).`. In plain language: theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version sits between dominance and Nash equilibrium. A rationalizable strategy does not need to be part of a mutually consistent equilibrium. It needs to be optimal for some belief the player could hold about others. This shifts the question from what must everyone be doing together to what could make this one player’s action sensible. The concept keeps more possibilities alive than Nash while still rejecting behavior that cannot be a best response to any coherent belief.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is support by belief. Instead of asking whether strategies match each other exactly, ask whether there exists a probability distribution over others’ actions that makes the strategy maximize expected payoff.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because plausible behavior depends on beliefs. A strategy can be reasonable even when it is not part of a Nash equilibrium, provided some coherent belief makes it optimal. Rationalizability formalizes that support relation between beliefs and best responses.

Why is this concept important?

Rationalizability matters because not every plausible strategy must belong to a Nash equilibrium. The course needs a way to ask whether a move could be optimal under some coherent belief about others.

What breaks without it?

Without rationalizability, students may jump too quickly from dominance to Nash. They would miss the middle ground where a strategy survives because a player can hold a belief that makes it a best response.

Worked Mini-Example

A move that looks odd may still be rational if the player believes the opponent is likely to choose a certain action. Rationalizability asks whether such a belief can make the move best.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck because rationalizability feels permissive. It is permissive on purpose, but not empty: a strategy still has to be a best response to some belief, not just a move someone might randomly choose.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is mutual consistency. A rationalizable strategy may be defensible under some belief, but the beliefs across players may not fit together as an equilibrium.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to treat rationalizable as meaning correct or predicted. It means defensible under some belief, not uniquely expected.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for models that want to separate impossible behavior from behavior that is merely one of several plausible responses.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: ask whether an action can be made optimal by some coherent belief. If yes, it may be defensible even outside Nash equilibrium. If no belief can rescue it, the action is not merely surprising; it is strategically unjustified. The audit is belief existence, not final coordination.

Connected Concepts

Rationalizability shares the best-response primitive with Nash but relaxes mutual consistency. It also previews Bayesian thinking because beliefs become explicit inputs to choice rather than background intuition.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A move looks strange at first, but it might be a best response to some belief about the opponent.

Walkthrough: Instead of asking whether the move is part of a mutual equilibrium, ask whether the player could hold a belief that makes the move optimal. If such a belief exists and the belief itself is not ruled out by rationality, the move survives. If no coherent belief can justify it, the move is eliminated.

Lesson: Rationalizability separates unjustifiable choices from choices that merely look odd under your favorite prediction.

Trap: Do not confuse 'not Nash' with 'irrational.' A move can be rationalizable without being in the final equilibrium prediction.

Model move: The modeling move is to ask whether some coherent belief can make the action optimal, instead of demanding that all players' beliefs and actions already close into equilibrium.

Reader action: Try to build a belief that makes the action optimal. If no belief works, identify the exact payoff comparison blocking it; if one does work, check whether that belief relies on already-rejected opponent behavior.

Numbers / table check: Write two belief cases: rival plays left with probability 1, and rival plays right with probability 1. If the action is best in at least one coherent case, it may be rationalizable; if another action beats it in every case, it is not.

Boundary check: The example weakens if the belief used to justify the move depends on opponents playing strategies that have already been ruled out by the same rationality logic.

Equation Walkthroughs

Use Expected Utility and Bayes Rule to understand rationalizability as a belief test, not a prediction test. The question is whether some coherent belief over the opponent's possible actions can make this action optimal. If yes, the action may survive even when it is not part of a Nash equilibrium. The math keeps the belief explicit so the analyst cannot dismiss an action merely because it looks strange under one favorite forecast.
