Dominance — a strategy that beats your alternatives no matter what others do

In most situations your best move depends on what the other person does. But sometimes one of your options beats all your others regardless — in every scenario the other player chooses. That is a dominant strategy. Rational players play it, often to everyone's loss. Here is the idea, the Prisoner's Dilemma that reveals it, why dominance is a rock-solid prediction, the sting it carries, and why it is the entry point to everything harder in game theory.

1 · The problem — your best move usually depends on theirs

When you decide alone, ‘best’ is simple: pick the option that gives you the most. But when the other person's choice affects your payoff, the tables turn. What price you should set depends on your rival's price; whether to invest in training depends on what skill the job market demands; whether to defect on an agreement depends on whether they will. Your best choice is a moving target, pinned to theirs.

So you face a puzzle: if your best move depends on theirs, and theirs depends on yours, how do you reason about what to do? What if one of your options was always best, regardless of their choice? That would be different — you would not have to guess what they are doing. You could simply play it.

2 · The reframe — check if one strategy beats your others in every column

Here is a simple check: for each choice the other player might make, ask which of your options pays you the most against it. Write down that option for each of their choices. If the same option of yours wins in every one of their choices — every column of the payoff table — then that option is dominant. It beats your alternatives no matter what.

Dominant strategies are rare, but when they exist they are powerful: you do not need to know what the other person will do, or to imagine their thinking. A dominant strategy is simply better, full stop. Rational players play dominant strategies. If all players have one, the outcome is completely determined — no guessing, no need to know the other's mind.

3 · Seen on a real run — the Prisoner's Dilemma

We took the classic Prisoner's Dilemma and computed payoffs to see which strategies are dominant. Here are your options (rows): you can Cooperate or Defect. The other person has the same two options (columns). The table below shows your payoff (the row payoffs) for each combination.

The discovered fact: Defect dominates. It beats Cooperate in every column — 5 is better than 3 when they cooperate, and 1 is better than 0 when they defect. So Defect is best no matter what they do.

4 · Why dominance is a rock-solid prediction

A dominant strategy is bulletproof because it does not depend on a guess. You do not need to predict what the other person will do, or to believe they are rational, or to know the payoffs from their viewpoint. You simply compare your own payoffs in each row. If one of your rows is bigger in every column, you play it — end of reasoning.

If both players have a dominant strategy, the outcome is locked in. Both will play theirs, so the result is determined. You predict it without knowing anything about the other's thinking or reasoning, which makes dominance the most certain prediction in game theory — a prediction that survives even if your beliefs about the other person are wrong.

5 · The one real weakness — both playing dominant strategy lands on mutual disaster

Here is the sting. Both players play their dominant strategy — Defect in the Prisoner's Dilemma — and both receive 1. But if both had cooperated, each would get 3. The outcome from playing dominant strategies (1, 1) is worse than the outcome from mutual cooperation (3, 3).

This is why it is called a dilemma. Individual rationality — each player playing their best response to what the other does — produces a collectively terrible outcome. Nobody is making a mistake. Nobody could do better alone. Yet together they end up worse off than if they had both chosen differently. That gap between individual best choice and collective good is the root of countless failures: arms races where nations spend more and more on weapons but no one gets safer, overfishing where each boat catches more but the stock collapses, price wars where rival firms cut prices but profits vanish.

6 · The surprise — this is the entry point to everything harder

Dominance is the cleanest case in game theory. It requires no belief about the other player, no assumptions about rationality beyond self-interest, no common knowledge of payoffs. When it exists, it solves the game completely. That simplicity is why it is the starting point.

But most games do not have dominant strategies — and that is what makes game theory necessary. When no dominant strategy exists, you have to reason about what the other person will do, which means reasoning about what they think you will do, and so on infinitely. That recursive thinking is what births every richer idea: best response, Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, bargaining, signaling, reputation. All of them are ways to break the circle when dominance alone is not enough. Learn to spot the dominant strategy first, and you have the master key to spotting when the puzzle deepens.
