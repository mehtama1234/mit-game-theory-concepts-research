Common Knowledge

A fact that everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, and so on.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that coordinated action may require more than everyone privately knowing a fact. People may also need to know that others know it, and know that others know that others know it.

Why does this problem exist?

Common knowledge exists because higher-order uncertainty can block coordination. A public fact can support behavior that a private fact cannot.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Common knowledge means everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, and the chain continues. The useful relation is `E, KE, KKE, KKKE, ...`. In plain language: E is the event; K means known by the players; repeated K's represent higher-order knowledge.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version turns a philosophical-sounding infinite chain into a practical coordination object. Knowing a fact is first-order knowledge. Knowing that others know it is a higher level. Knowing that others know that others know it is another level. Strategic action can depend on these levels because each player is choosing partly in response to what they expect others to understand. The transcript anchor that distinguishes mutual knowledge from first- and second-order mutual knowledge is therefore not a side definition. It is the ladder that lets the course explain why public announcements, shared rules, and conventions can support behavior that private information cannot.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is recursive knowledge. Start with an event E. Apply the “everyone knows” operator repeatedly: everyone knows E, everyone knows that everyone knows E, and so on without a finite stopping point.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary language hides the levels. Saying everyone knows can mean many different things: each person separately knows, each knows that the others know, or the fact is public in a way that supports unlimited mutual reasoning. The recursive knowledge notation forces those levels apart. It lets the analyst ask whether a proposed equilibrium relies only on the fact itself or on the public assurance that everyone can reason from the same fact.

Why is this concept important?

Common knowledge matters because coordination can require publicness, not just truth. A fact can be known by everyone individually but still fail to coordinate action if people are unsure whether others know it too.

What breaks without it?

Without common knowledge, the course would miss higher-order uncertainty. Players might all know the same event, yet hesitate because they do not know whether others know, or whether others know that they know.

Worked Mini-Example

Suppose a rule change matters only if firms expect rivals to follow it too. If every firm privately receives the rule, each may still wonder whether others received it, whether others know they received it, and whether others know that everyone is reasoning this way. A public announcement does more than deliver information. It creates shared assurance. The common-knowledge ladder explains why the same factual content can have different strategic effects depending on whether it is privately known or publicly known to be shared.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck because the infinite chain sounds philosophical. The course uses it for concrete coordination: people act differently when a fact is publicly shared than when each person only privately receives it.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is public assurance. A fact can be true, widely known, and still not common knowledge if players doubt whether others received it, understood it, or recognized that everyone else received it. The model also assumes a level of idealized reasoning that real people may not satisfy. That does not make the concept useless; it tells the reader what kind of coordination failure the concept is isolating.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to treat true, known, and common knowledge as the same. They are different levels of shared awareness.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for coordination failures, public announcements, or arguments that depend on what everyone knows about everyone else's knowledge.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: coordination may require shared awareness, not just shared facts. Ask whether players know, know that others know, and know that this is mutual. Publicness is the strategic object; private knowledge may not coordinate behavior.

Connected Concepts

Common knowledge connects iterated deletion, rationalizability, equilibrium selection, cheap talk, and public communication. It is the knowledge-side analogue of mutual consistency: everyone reasoning must line up at many levels.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Every firm privately receives the same rule change, but each firm is unsure whether others received it.

Walkthrough: Private knowledge may not move behavior because each firm worries others may still act under the old rule. A public announcement changes more than information; it tells everyone that everyone heard it. That recursive awareness can coordinate action.

Lesson: Common knowledge is the publicness structure that lets players reason about others' reasoning.

Trap: Do not confuse many people knowing a fact with everyone knowing that everyone knows it.

Model move: The modeling move is to climb the belief ladder. Ask not only who knows the rule change, but who knows that others know, and whether that publicness continues far enough to coordinate action.

Reader action: Label the fact as private, mutual, public, or common knowledge. Then name the coordinated action that requires higher-order confidence; this prevents treating shared information as enough when shared awareness is the real issue.

Numbers / table check: Make levels: E, everyone knows E, everyone knows everyone knows E. Private emails may satisfy the first two levels but fail the public ladder. A public announcement fills the higher rows because each player can reason about the same shared observation.

Boundary check: The example fails if delivery is private, noisy, delayed, or targeted, because then the fact may be widely known without becoming a shared basis for strategic reasoning.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Common Knowledge Recursion card shows why coordination can require more than everyone knowing the same fact. The sequence E, KE, KKE, and so on is a ladder of publicness: the fact, everyone knowing the fact, everyone knowing that everyone knows, and so forth. The math matters when action depends on confidence about others' confidence. Private notice can spread information without creating the shared basis for coordinated behavior.
