Common Knowledge

Something everyone knows, and also everyone knows that everyone knows it, and everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows it, on and on forever. Not just true or privately known — shared in a way that everyone can reason from it together.

What real-world problem is this about?

Coordinating action sometimes needs more than just each person knowing a fact alone. People also need to know that others know it, and know that others know that others know it. Without that chain, people hesitate even though each one has the information.

Why does this problem exist?

Higher-order uncertainty — not knowing what others know — can stop coordination cold. A fact told publicly can trigger action that the same fact told in private cannot, because public telling creates a chain where everyone can see everyone else has it.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Write it as: E (the event), then K (known by players). The chain is E, KE, KKE, KKKE, going forever. In words: the event happens, then people know it; people know that people know it; people know that people know that people know it; and this chain never stops. Each time you add K, you go up one level of mutual awareness.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture builds the infinite chain into a practical tool. First-order knowledge: you know a fact. Second-order knowledge: you know that others know it. Third-order knowledge: you know that others know that others know it. Strategic choices depend on these levels because each player is guessing partly from what they expect others will understand. The difference between mutual knowledge and first-order, second-order, and third-order mutual knowledge is not a side definition — it is the core explanation for why public rules, shared conventions, and announced facts can move people to coordinate in ways that private information alone cannot.

Mathematical Intuition

Recursive knowledge: repeated application of 'everyone knows'. Start with an event E. Apply 'everyone knows' once: everyone knows E. Apply it again: everyone knows that everyone knows E. Apply it again: everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows E. No finite stopping point.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

English hides the levels. When you say 'everyone knows', you could mean: each person knows it separately; each knows that the other knows it; or the fact is public in a way that supports unlimited reasoning by all parties. The math notation with repeated K's forces those meanings apart. It lets the analyst ask whether an equilibrium depends only on the fact itself or on the public assurance that everyone is reasoning from the shared fact.

Why is this concept important?

Coordination often needs publicness, not just truth. A fact can be known by each person individually yet fail to move them to act together if each one doubts whether the others know, or whether the others know that everyone is aware. The chain breaks the doubt.

What breaks without it?

Without it, the course would miss what stops coordination. Players might all know the same event, yet each might hold back because they are unsure whether others know too, or whether others know that they know. That doubt is a real barrier.

Worked Mini-Example

Suppose firms get a new rule and will only follow it if they expect rivals to follow it too. If each firm gets the rule through private email, each one might still wonder: Did everyone else get it? Does everyone else know that I got it? Does everyone else know that everyone got it? A public announcement does something private email cannot — it shows the rule to everyone at once, so each firm can reason that all other firms are reasoning from the same shared fact. The common-knowledge ladder explains why the same factual content has different strategic weight depending on whether it is private or public.

Where Students Get Stuck

The infinite chain sounds like philosophy, so students dismiss it. But the course uses it for concrete results: people act one way when a fact is public and another way when only privately known. The chain is the bridge between the two.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is public assurance. A fact can be true, widely known, and still not common knowledge if players doubt whether others received it, understood it, or saw that everyone else received it. The model also assumes a high level of clear reasoning that real people may not always achieve. That does not erase the concept — it shows what kind of coordination failure the concept is targeting and why it matters.

Common Misunderstanding

The trap is treating 'true', 'I know it', and 'common knowledge' as the same. They are layers. A fact can be true and even widely known without being common knowledge if people doubt whether everyone else knows and trusts that everyone knows.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for coordination failures, public announcements, or arguments that rest on what everyone knows about what everyone else knows about the world and about each other's knowledge.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: coordination may require shared awareness, not just shared facts. Ask whether players know, know that others know, and know that this is mutual. Publicness is the strategic object; private knowledge may not coordinate behavior.

Connected Concepts

Common knowledge connects iterated deletion (removing moves that can never be played), rationalizability (a strategy people could defend by their beliefs), equilibrium selection (which equilibrium will actually happen), cheap talk (communication that costs nothing), and public communication. It is the knowledge-side mirror of mutual consistency — when everyone reasons at the same level, their reasoning aligns at many levels at once.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Every firm privately receives the same rule change, but each firm is unsure whether others received it.

Walkthrough: Private knowledge may not move behavior because each firm worries others may still act under the old rule. A public announcement changes more than information; it tells everyone that everyone heard it. That recursive awareness can coordinate action.

Lesson: Common knowledge is the publicness structure that lets players reason about others' reasoning.

Trap: Do not confuse many people knowing a fact with everyone knowing that everyone knows it.

Model move: The modeling move is to climb the belief ladder. Ask not only who knows the rule change, but who knows that others know, and whether that publicness continues far enough to coordinate action.

Reader action: Label the fact as private, mutual, public, or common knowledge. Then name the coordinated action that requires higher-order confidence; this prevents treating shared information as enough when shared awareness is the real issue.

Numbers / table check: Make levels: E, everyone knows E, everyone knows everyone knows E. Private emails may satisfy the first two levels but fail the public ladder. A public announcement fills the higher rows because each player can reason about the same shared observation.

Boundary check: The example fails if delivery is private, noisy, delayed, or targeted, because then the fact may be widely known without becoming a shared basis for strategic reasoning.

Common Knowledge As Shared Assurance

Start From A Fact Everyone May Need To Act On

Common knowledge is about public assurance, not just information. A fact can be true, and each person can know it, but that may still be too weak for coordination. Each person may wonder whether others know it, whether others know that others know it, and whether everyone is reasoning from the same starting point. Common knowledge is the strong shared state where this ladder keeps going.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Coordination often requires more than private awareness. If a public rule change is announced to everyone at once, people can act knowing that others heard it too. If the same rule is sent privately to each person, the factual content may be identical, but the strategic effect can differ. Common knowledge exists because strategic action can depend on what people believe about other people's beliefs, not only on the first fact.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to say everyone knows and stop there. Everyone knowing can mean each person separately received the fact. It does not always mean each person knows that the others received it, or that this is itself known, and so on. The higher levels sound abstract, but they answer a practical question: can people safely coordinate because they know the fact is shared?

Why It Matters Beyond Classroom Puzzles

Common knowledge matters in financial markets, legal announcements, software protocol changes, traffic conventions, political protests, and team work. Public announcements can move behavior because they change the shared reasoning environment. In topology and fixed-point models, common knowledge can affect which game is being solved: the set of strategies, beliefs, and feasible coordination patterns may change when information is public rather than private. The fact itself is only one part of the object; the public status of the fact can change the strategic space.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Software protocols

A protocol update works only when participants can treat the rule and its adoption as shared. Private notice may leave coordination risk.

Financial markets

Public news can move prices not only because traders learn the fact, but because they know other traders learned it too.
