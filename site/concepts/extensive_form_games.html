Extensive Form Games

A game drawn as a tree diagram instead of a table, because the timing of moves and what each player knows at each moment matter.

What real-world problem is this about?

In real conflicts, people move one at a time, not all at once. A choice made later can depend on what happened earlier. And sometimes you know more than the other person — or they know more than you. A flat table misses all of that.

Why does this problem exist?

Time and information are real. If you move first, you cannot take back your choice. If you move second, you might have seen what the first person did. These facts change whether a threat is believable or a promise is honest.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Backward induction starts at the end. At the final decision point, a player chooses the branch that gives the best score. Then move back one step: knowing what the later player will do, what should the earlier player choose? Keep going backward to the start. The formula is `V(node) = max_action V(next node)`, which means: the value of where you stand is the highest value you can reach by picking one of your moves.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The deep version asks what a table cannot show. If players move in sequence, the tree marks it. If a player observes an earlier choice, the tree marks it—different branches lead to nodes that the player can tell apart. If a player moves blind (does not know what happened), the tree groups nodes into an information set so the player must choose the same move at both nodes. Terminal nodes show payoffs. This is not just a picture. It is the structure that lets you ask whether a threat is believable (will it be chosen later?) and whether beliefs must shift (what would a player infer from an unexpected move?).

Mathematical Intuition

The key idea is nesting: one game sits inside another. A node represents a history (the path taken so far). An action picks a branch. A terminal path gives scores. This structure lets you reason recursively — solve the end first, then work backward, knowing that each earlier player predicts the smart moves of later players.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

A flat list of actions cannot encode time or information. It cannot say who moved first, what was observed, or which histories look identical to a player. The tree structure preserves those facts as explicit objects—nodes, edges, information sets, payoffs—so that later reasoning about credibility and belief updates has a home.

Why is this concept important?

A tree lets you see credibility. If you threaten to do something costly if the other person acts, the tree shows whether you would actually do it when that moment arrives. It also shows what each player observes: did you see the other person's move, or did you move blind? That shape of information changes everything.

What breaks without it?

Without a tree, you would write down all actions in rows and columns, like a table. But that table pretends everyone moved at the same time and saw nothing. It would hide whether a threat is really believable, whether later moves were made with information about earlier ones, and whether a player could distinguish between different situations.

Worked Mini-Example

A new firm thinks about entering a market. The incumbent (the company already there) says, 'If you enter, I will cut prices and hurt us both.' The tree shows two paths: the new firm enters, then the incumbent chooses between a price war and peaceful coexistence. The tree asks: if entry happened, would the incumbent really cut prices, or does the threat fall apart because a price war now costs the incumbent too much?

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often assume that a tree automatically means every player saw every earlier move. That is wrong. Information sets are the remedy. An information set groups nodes together because a player at those nodes cannot tell them apart — they might be at node A or node B, but they do not know which. So they must choose the same move at both.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is honesty about information and sequence. A tree works only if its nodes, information sets, and payoffs truly reflect what each player can observe and choose at each point in time.

Common Misunderstanding

A common mistake is to collapse the tree into a simple table and lose the question of credibility. If the tree showed a threat made at the start, the table might treat it as binding forever. But the tree reveals that threats can become non-credible as the game unfolds.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for any situation where the order of moves changes what a person would do, or where a player does not have full information about what happened before. Look for threats or promises that depend on what happens later. Look for a claim about strategy or a prediction that assumes simultaneous moves when the actual sequence was different.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: whenever order, observation, or later choice matters, ask whether a flat table has hidden the real problem. A tree earns its keep by showing who moves when, what they know, and where credibility is tested.

Connected Concepts

Extensive form connects to backward induction (reasoning from end to start), subgame perfection (each subgame must be solved correctly), bargaining with time (offers and counteroffers), one-shot deviation (checking whether a player can gain by changing only one move), and PBE (perfect Bayesian equilibrium, which updates beliefs as the tree unfolds). All of these need a tree to make sense.

Worked Example Card

Setup: An incumbent firm threatens a price war if a new firm enters.

Walkthrough: A flat table can record the threat, but a tree asks when the threat would actually be chosen. After entry happens, the incumbent reaches a later decision node. If fighting loses money at that node, the threat is not credible.

Lesson: The tree matters because timing changes what a promise or threat means.

Trap: Do not assume a drawn branch is a believable future action; later incentives still have to be checked.

Model move: The modeling move is to preserve order and observation. Draw who moves first, who sees what, and which later choices remain possible after each earlier branch.

Reader action: Draw the timeline before writing payoffs. Mark each decision node with who moves and what they know, then circle the later node where any threat or promise would actually be tested. If two nodes look separate but the player cannot tell them apart, group them before solving.

Numbers / table check: Put the incumbent's later choice in a small two-row table: fight gives 1, accommodate gives 4 after entry. If accommodate is larger at that node, the earlier price-war threat cannot be treated as credible without adding another payoff consequence.

Boundary check: The example breaks if the tree hides uncertainty, reputation, renegotiation, or information sets that make two visually separate nodes indistinguishable to the moving player.

Equation Walkthroughs

Use the Backward Induction card to read a game tree as a machine for testing future credibility. A branch is not a promise that the player will take it; it is an option that must still be attractive when that node is reached. The math compresses each solved future choice into a value and passes that value backward. If the tree hides what a player observes, the calculation can look clean while solving the wrong situation.

Extensive Form Games As Keeping Time Visible

Start From A Situation That Unfolds

An extensive form game is a way to draw a strategic situation as a tree. The everyday reason is simple: sometimes people do not choose all at once. One person moves, another person sees something, a later choice becomes available, and the meaning of an action depends on where the history has gone. A table can hide that timing. A tree keeps the order, the possible histories, and the later decision points visible.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Timing changes incentives. A threat, promise, offer, refusal, or signal can matter only because it happens before or after something else. Extensive form exists because the model needs to know who moves when, what each person sees, and which later choices remain possible. Without that structure, the course could confuse a simultaneous choice with a sequential one and then apply the wrong solution idea.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to think the tree is only a picture of the same information in a prettier form. It is not. A tree can represent facts that a payoff table may hide, such as whether a player observed an earlier move or whether two decision points are indistinguishable to that player. If the information sets or timing are wrong, backward induction and subgame perfection can answer the wrong question.

Why It Matters Beyond Game Diagrams

Extensive form matters in bargaining, trials, product launches, hiring, military planning, and software protocols because choices happen after histories. It also matters for topology and fixed-point work because the object being solved changes when histories and information sets are included. A fixed point in a strategic form representation may miss credibility problems that only appear once the time path is drawn. That is why the first modeling question is often not how to solve the game, but whether the tree has preserved the real order of action and observation.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Software protocols

A protocol is a sequence of actions and observations. Drawing the order helps show which participant knows what before sending the next message.

Courtroom strategy

Settlement, discovery, trial, and appeal happen in order. A legal strategy can only be judged after the later choices and costs are kept visible.
