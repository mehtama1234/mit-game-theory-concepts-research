Revenue Equivalence

Different auction rules can produce the same expected seller revenue under specific assumptions.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is comparing auction formats that look different: first-price, second-price, English, and others may create different bidding behavior.

Why does this problem exist?

Revenue equivalence exists because different rules can sometimes generate the same expected payment once bidders adjust their strategies.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Truthful behavior must give at least as much payoff as each profitable lie. The useful relation is `u_i(truth) >= u_i(report) for every alternative report.`. In plain language: The inequality says a person should not gain by replacing the truthful action with another report.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version asks why very different auction rules can sometimes give the seller the same expected revenue. The answer is not that the rules are identical. It is that under specific assumptions, bidders adjust strategies so that allocation probabilities and expected payments line up. This teaches students to inspect the theorem’s conditions rather than memorize a surprising slogan.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is that expected payment can be pinned down by allocation rules plus boundary conditions under the standard assumptions. If the same type has the same chance of winning across mechanisms, expected revenue can match even when payment timing differs.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because comparing auction formats requires more than comparing labels. Expected revenue depends on allocation probabilities, payment rules, and equilibrium bidding. Revenue equivalence formalizes the conditions under which those pieces line up across mechanisms.

Why is this concept important?

Revenue equivalence matters because it teaches students to look past surface format. Different auction rules can produce the same expected seller revenue when allocation rules and assumptions line up in the right way.

What breaks without it?

Without revenue equivalence, every auction comparison would be treated as an isolated case. The course would miss the deeper structure that makes some formats equivalent and the assumptions that make equivalence fragile.

Worked Mini-Example

First-price and second-price auctions induce different bids, but under the theorem's assumptions the seller's expected revenue can match because bidders strategically shade in one format and bid truthfully in another.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by comparing the rules while holding bids fixed. Equilibrium bids change with the auction format, and those strategic adjustments are exactly what the theorem accounts for.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the theorem assumptions. Once values are affiliated, bidders are risk averse, reserve prices change, or allocation rules differ, equivalence may fail. The teaching payoff is that failure is informative. When revenue equivalence breaks, the reason usually points to a real economic force such as risk aversion, common values, entry, asymmetry, or a changed allocation rule. The theorem is a map of assumptions as much as a revenue result.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to quote revenue equivalence without the assumptions. Change risk, affiliation, reserve prices, or asymmetry and the result can fail.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for auction comparisons where allocation sameness and bidder payoff normalization drive payment conclusions.

Connected Concepts

Revenue equivalence connects auctions, Bayesian Nash, expected payments, and incentive compatibility. It also prepares students to read mechanism papers by checking distributional and valuation assumptions before trusting a revenue claim.
