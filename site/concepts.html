choice and payoff

Preferences

A way to say which outcomes a person ranks above others.

Problem
The problem is that before we talk about strategy, we need a way to say what one person is trying to get. A person may like one outcome more than another even when dollars, risk, effort, and timing are mixed together.
Math
A preference relation ranks outcomes, often represented by a utility function u(x). The useful relation is `x preferred to y means u(x) > u(y).`. In plain language: x and y are possible outcomes; u assigns each outcome a number; the larger number records the option the person ranks higher.

Expected Utility

Averaging possible payoffs by how likely they are.

Problem
The problem is choosing when the outcome is uncertain: a bid might win or lose, a risky action might pay off or fail, and a type may be one of several possibilities.
Math
A belief is a probability distribution over states, types, or histories. The useful relation is `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).`. In plain language: theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

strategic dependence

Strategic Form Games

A compact table of players, actions, and payoffs chosen at the same time.

Problem
The problem is representing a situation where everyone chooses without first seeing the others' choices, yet everyone's payoff depends on the whole combination.
Math
A payoff function u_i(a_i, a_-i) maps everyone's actions to player i's payoff. The useful relation is `u_i(a_i, a_-i).`. In plain language: i is one player; a_i is that player's action; a_-i means everyone else's actions; u_i is the payoff assigned to the whole action profile.

strategic dependence

Dominance

A way to rule out choices that are never better than another choice.

Problem
The problem is that some choices are plainly bad no matter what the other side does. Keeping them in the analysis can distract from the strategic choices that actually need thought.
Math
Dominance compares one strategy against another across possible opponent choices. The useful relation is `s dominates t if u_i(s, a_-i) >= u_i(t, a_-i) for all a_-i, with strict inequality somewhere.`. In plain language: The inequality checks every possible action by others; dominance means one strategy is never worse and sometimes better.

equilibrium reasoning

Best Response

The move that works best against a specified belief or opponent action.

Problem
The problem is that a strategy is not good in the abstract. It is good against a particular opponent action or belief.
Math
BR_i(a_-i) is the set of actions for player i that maximize u_i given others' actions. The useful relation is `BR_i(a_-i) = argmax_a u_i(a, a_-i).`. In plain language: argmax means choose the action a that makes player i's payoff as large as possible when others choose a_-i.

  • ev-best_response-02: Lecture 5: Nash Equilibrium

equilibrium reasoning

Nash Equilibrium

A profile of strategies where no player wants to change alone.

Problem
The problem is making a prediction that does not immediately fall apart when each participant asks, given what the others are doing, should I switch?
Math
Equilibrium requires each strategy to be a best response to the others. The useful relation is `a_i in BR_i(a_-i) for every player i.`. In plain language: For each player i, their chosen action a_i must belong to the set of best responses against the other players' actions.

Infinitely Repeated Games

A relationship with no fixed last round, where future punishment can shape present behavior.

Problem
The problem is how cooperation can survive when each player is tempted to grab a short-run gain by cheating.
Math
A discount factor delta weights future payoffs in an intertemporal sum. The useful relation is `U = u_0 + delta u_1 + delta^2 u_2 + ...`. In plain language: u_t is payoff in period t; delta between 0 and 1 controls how strongly the future matters.

Auctions

Problem
The problem is allocating an object when people know their own values but the seller and rival bidders may not.
Math
Truthful behavior must give at least as much payoff as each profitable lie. The useful relation is `u_i(truth) >= u_i(report) for every alternative report.`. In plain language: The inequality says a person should not gain by replacing the truthful action with another report.

communication and knowledge

Common Knowledge

A fact that everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, and so on.

Problem
The problem is that coordinated action may require more than everyone privately knowing a fact. People may also need to know that others know it, and know that others know that others know it.
Math
Common knowledge means everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, and the chain continues. The useful relation is `E, KE, KKE, KKKE, ...`. In plain language: E is the event; K means known by the players; repeated K's represent higher-order knowledge.
