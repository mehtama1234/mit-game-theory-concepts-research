Mathematical Primitives

Preference

A way to say which outcome a person would rather have.

Plain-Language Principle

Preference is the course’s way of saying that a person can compare outcomes before any strategic machinery starts. The primitive matters because a game cannot explain choice unless there is some stable sense in which one outcome is better for that player than another.

Symbol By Symbol

x and y are possible outcomes; u assigns each outcome a number; the larger number records the option the person ranks higher.

Where It Appears

Preferences appear first in the opening choice lecture, then reappear whenever payoffs, utilities, outside options, bidder values, or expected utilities are used as inputs to strategic reasoning.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The common misuse is treating the utility representation as a literal psychological ruler. In this course, different numerical representations can describe the same ordering unless uncertainty or expected utility requires stronger comparisons.

Payoff Mapping

A table that says what each person gets from each combination of choices.

Plain-Language Principle

Payoff mapping says that one person’s result is produced by the whole situation, not by that person’s action alone. A game table or tree is a compact machine that turns everyone’s choices into each player’s consequence.

Symbol By Symbol

i is one player; a_i is that player's action; a_-i means everyone else's actions; u_i is the payoff assigned to the whole action profile.

Where It Appears

Payoff mapping appears in strategic-form games, extensive-form games, bargaining, ad auctions, and any lecture that asks whether a deviation changes a player’s payoff.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

A payoff map can smuggle in the conclusion if the modeler omits costs, outside options, information, or future consequences. The table is only as honest as the situation it represents.

Best Response

The choice that works best given what someone believes others will do.

Plain-Language Principle

Best response is the one-player optimization step inside game theory, but in everyday words it means: given what I think the others are doing, what should I do?

Symbol By Symbol

argmax means choose the action a that makes player i's payoff as large as possible when others choose a_-i.

Where It Appears

Best response appears in rationalizability, Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, minimax reasoning, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, and the deviation checks behind dynamic concepts.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is naming a best response without naming the belief, opponent action, or information condition. That hides the actual assumption doing the work.

Mutual Consistency

A situation where each person's plan makes sense given everyone else's plan.

Plain-Language Principle

Mutual consistency turns a collection of individual choices into a stable strategic prediction. Everyone’s plan has to make sense given everyone else’s plan.

Symbol By Symbol

For each player i, their chosen action a_i must belong to the set of best responses against the other players' actions.

Where It Appears

Mutual consistency appears in Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, zero-sum games, subgame perfection, infinitely repeated games, folk theorem logic, and Bayesian Nash equilibrium.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is reading equilibrium as good, fair, optimal for society, or morally desirable. It only says that unilateral deviation is not profitable under the modeled conditions.

Pruning

Remove choices that are never worth taking, then reason with the smaller problem.

Plain-Language Principle

Pruning removes actions that cannot be justified before solving the whole game. It is the mathematical version of clearing away choices that are never worth taking.

Symbol By Symbol

The inequality checks every possible action by others; dominance means one strategy is never worse and sometimes better.

Where It Appears

Pruning appears in dominance, iterated deletion, rationalizability, and as background for equilibrium reasoning because eliminated strategies cannot be serious best responses.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is deleting too aggressively, especially with weak dominance or when beliefs matter. A deletion rule can change the remaining game, so the strength and order of the rule must be explicit.

Backward Recursion

Solve the future first, then use that answer to decide what should happen earlier.

Plain-Language Principle

Backward recursion says that later incentives determine earlier meaning. To understand a promise or threat now, solve what the player will want when the promised moment arrives.

Symbol By Symbol

V is continuation value; at each decision point the player chooses the next branch with the best continuation value.

Where It Appears

Backward recursion appears in extensive-form games, backward induction, subgame perfection, one-shot deviation, bargaining, finitely repeated games, and perfect Bayesian equilibrium.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is solving backward through a tree that leaves out information, uncertainty, or renegotiation. Backward logic is powerful only after the tree is an honest representation.

Discounting

Future rewards count, but usually count less than rewards right now.

Plain-Language Principle

Discounting is the course’s way to compare now with later. Future payoffs matter, but the discount factor says how much weight the future has relative to the present.

Symbol By Symbol

u_t is payoff in period t; delta between 0 and 1 controls how strongly the future matters.

Where It Appears

Discounting appears in bargaining, infinitely repeated games, folk theorem arguments, implicit cartels, and the repeated-game parts of cooperation.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is treating future discipline as automatic. If players are impatient, cannot observe cheating, or know the game ends, the discounting inequality can fail.

Belief

A probability story about what kind of situation or opponent you are facing.

Plain-Language Principle

Belief is a probability story about hidden facts. It lets a player choose while admitting that they do not know the opponent’s type, value, history, or information.

Symbol By Symbol

theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

Where It Appears

Belief appears in expected utility, rationalizability, mixed strategies, Bayesian games, types and beliefs, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, auctions, PBE, signaling, and cheap talk.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is using beliefs as arbitrary story glue. In dynamic games especially, beliefs must be tied to information, Bayes rule where possible, and the incentives that produced observed behavior.

Incentive Compatibility

Design rules so people want to reveal or do what the rule needs them to reveal or do.

Plain-Language Principle

Incentive compatibility asks whether the rule makes desired behavior privately worthwhile. A mechanism cannot simply request honesty, cooperation, or truthful bidding; it must make those actions attractive.

Symbol By Symbol

The inequality says a person should not gain by replacing the truthful action with another report.

Where It Appears

Incentive compatibility appears in implicit cartels, auctions, revenue equivalence, ad auctions, signaling, and cheap talk, with related logic in folk-theorem punishment constraints.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is designing by intention rather than incentives. If misreporting, deviation, or manipulation pays more, the mechanism fails no matter how elegant the rule looks.

Knowledge Recursion

Not only knowing a fact, but knowing that others know it, and so on.

Plain-Language Principle

Knowledge recursion captures the difference between a fact being true, people knowing it, and people knowing that others know it. Coordination often needs the higher levels, not just the first one.

Symbol By Symbol

E is the event; K means known by the players; repeated K's represent higher-order knowledge.

Where It Appears

Knowledge recursion appears most directly in common knowledge, and indirectly in iterated deletion, rationalizability, equilibrium selection, and communication because players reason about others’ reasoning.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is treating widespread knowledge as common knowledge. If players are unsure whether others saw the same fact, the recursive chain can break and coordination may fail.
