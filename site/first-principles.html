Course-wide first principles

Game Theory In Everyday Words

These essays explain the course as a connected way of thinking: start with ordinary choice under pressure, then add timing, information, repetition, rules, knowledge, and applications outside economics.

The Whole Course In Plain Language

Game theory starts from a simple problem: what happens to me depends on what someone else does, and that person is also thinking. The course is about making that loop clear enough to reason with.

Start with the ordinary problem

Imagine two people choosing at the same time. One person cannot decide well by asking only what they like. They also have to ask what the other person may do. The other person faces the same problem. This is the root of the course. A choice is not good in isolation. A choice is good relative to other choices around it. That is why the course keeps returning to players, actions, payoffs, information, timing, and rules. Those words sound formal, but they name everyday things: who is involved, what each person can do, what each person cares about, what each person knows, when decisions happen, and what setting turns those decisions into results.

Why equilibrium appears

An equilibrium is not a claim that the result is fair, wise, or socially good. It is a check for mutual fit. If each person looked at what the others are doing and still would not want to switch, the choices fit together. That matters because many bad outcomes are stable. Two firms may keep prices high. Two drivers may both wait. Two countries may keep arming. Two bidders may shade bids. The model asks why the pattern can persist even when nobody planned the whole pattern from above.

Why the course keeps adding structure

A one-shot simultaneous game is a clean starting point, but it hides too much. Some decisions happen in order. Then timing matters. Some relationships repeat. Then the future can reward or punish present behavior. Some people know facts that others do not. Then beliefs matter. Some institutions set rules before people act. Then design matters. Some messages are cheap and some signals are costly. Then communication has to be read through incentives. The course is not a pile of separate topics. It is one question repeated under richer conditions: when each person reacts to others, what can we honestly predict, design, or explain?

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Everyday life

A roommate agreement, a salary negotiation, a group project, and a repeated friendship all have the same bones. There are people, choices, consequences, information, and memory. Game theory matters because it warns you not to explain behavior only by personality. Sometimes the setting makes distrust, delay, bluffing, or cooperation a predictable response.

Politics

Campaigns, coalitions, threats, promises, and public goods problems all involve people reacting to expected reactions. A promise matters only if it will still be worth keeping later. A threat matters only if carrying it out will still make sense when the time comes.

Choice, Payoff, And The Trap Of A Single Number

Before the course can talk about strategy, it needs a way to say what each person is trying to get. A payoff is a compact way to record that comparison, not a complete description of a human being.

What a payoff is doing

A payoff number says how one person ranks outcomes inside the model. It does not mean money unless the model says money is the right object. It does not mean happiness in a full human sense. It is a tool for comparison. If one outcome gives a player 5 and another gives 2, the model says the player prefers the first outcome. That small move lets the course ask which action is better once other actions are fixed.

Why this matters before any equilibrium

If the payoff story is wrong, the later solution can be perfectly calculated and still useless. A model of bidding that ignores budgets may predict bids people cannot afford. A model of cooperation that ignores reputation may miss why someone sacrifices short-run money. A model of policy that hides fairness inside a welfare score may make a political conflict look like a technical calculation. First-principles reading means asking what the payoff is supposed to represent before trusting the answer.

The beginner mistake

The common mistake is to treat the payoff table as if it came from nature. It did not. Someone chose what counts. The table is useful because it forces the writer to be explicit. It is dangerous when the writer forgets what was left outside the table.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Law and institutions

A fine, rule, or contract changes behavior only through the comparisons people face. If breaking a rule is profitable after expected punishment, the rule may look strict on paper and weak in practice.

Computer science

Online platforms often turn user actions into scores, bids, rankings, or rewards. The same warning applies: if the score does not match the behavior the platform actually wants, strategic users can optimize the score while harming the system.

Stable Prediction Is Not A Good Outcome

Equilibrium is a stability test. It asks whether each person's choice makes sense given the other choices. It does not promise that the result is good for the group.

The plain idea

Suppose everyone can see the proposed pattern of behavior. If one person would rather change while the others stay put, the pattern is not stable. If nobody wants to change alone, the pattern passes the equilibrium test. This is useful because it separates wishful thinking from a prediction that survives individual incentives.

Why bad outcomes can be stable

People often expect rational choice to produce a good result. The course pushes against that. A stable outcome can waste resources, block cooperation, keep prices high, or leave everyone worse off than another possible outcome. The reason is simple: a better group result may require several people to move together, while each person deciding alone may be afraid to move first. That is why the model separates individual logic from group welfare. It lets the reader say, without contradiction, that each person is responding sensibly and the final pattern is still harmful.

What changes when the setting changes

In a simultaneous game, each person chooses without seeing the current move of the other. In a game with timing, a later person can react. In a game with hidden information, each person reacts to beliefs. In a repeated game, each person reacts to the future as well as the present. The word equilibrium stays, but the object being checked changes.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Biology

A trait can persist because it does well against the traits around it, not because it is best in every possible world. This is the everyday meaning behind evolutionary stability: success is measured inside a population of interacting types.

Networks

Traffic routes, platform choices, and communication standards can get stuck because each user responds to where others already are. A different arrangement may be better, but no single user can reach it alone.

Time Turns Plans Into Promises That Must Survive Later

Once decisions happen in order, a plan is not enough. The later action must still be worth taking when the later moment arrives.

Why a threat can fail

A person may announce a punishment to shape someone else's behavior. But if carrying out the punishment later would hurt the punisher, the announcement is weak. The other person may ignore it. This is the basic reason for backward induction and subgame perfection. The model starts at the later decision and asks what will actually be chosen there. Then it uses that answer to understand earlier choices.

Why repetition changes the problem

If people meet only once, the present choice carries the whole weight. If they meet again and again, a present action can change future rewards. Cooperation can become possible because cheating today may cause punishment tomorrow. But repetition does not magically create trust. The future must matter enough, bad behavior must be noticed enough, and the punishment must be strong enough. A repeated-game explanation should always name the reward for cooperating, the short-run gain from cheating, the chance cheating is detected, and the cost of the punishment that follows.

The last-period lesson

A known final round can unravel cooperation. In the last round, future punishment is gone. If cooperation needs future punishment, it may fail there. Once the last round is solved, the next-to-last round may also lose its discipline, and the logic can move backward. This is why finite and infinite repetition teach different lessons.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Business and markets

A firm can promise low prices, high quality, or harsh retaliation. The question is whether the firm will still want to do that later. Repeated customers, reviews, and reputation can make future losses large enough to discipline present behavior.

International relations

Arms control, sanctions, and alliances all depend on credibility. A public promise is weak if the later government would prefer to walk away. A commitment device matters because it changes later incentives, not because it sounds serious.

Hidden Information Makes Beliefs Part Of The Game

When people do not know the same facts, they do not only choose actions. They also form beliefs about the situation they are in.

The plain problem

A buyer may not know product quality. A firm may not know a worker's skill. A bidder may not know another bidder's value. A platform may not know what a user really wants. In each case, behavior carries information. A high price, a costly education choice, a bid, or a message may change what others believe. The action does two jobs at once: it may directly affect payoffs, and it may tell other people what kind of situation they are facing.

Why probability enters

A belief is a disciplined guess. The model uses probabilities so the guess can be updated and used in payoff comparisons. This does not mean people are perfect calculators. It means the writer has to say what uncertain fact matters, what each person initially thinks, what signal is observed, and how that observation should change the story. Without that discipline, hidden information becomes a vague excuse. With it, the reader can ask whether the assumed prior, signal, or update is carrying too much of the conclusion.

Messages are not all the same

A costly signal can separate types because different people face different costs or benefits from sending it. Cheap talk is different. If a message is free and cannot be verified, it persuades only when incentives make truthful speech possible. The model asks when words change beliefs and when they are only noise.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Biology

Signals such as displays, calls, or costly traits can matter because they change what others infer. The key question is why a low-quality type cannot cheaply mimic a high-quality type.

Online systems

Ratings, badges, verification marks, bids, and recommendations all act as signals. They are useful only if the system makes manipulation costly enough or designs incentives so the signal still means something.

Rules Shape Behavior Before Anyone Chooses

Mechanism design and auctions ask a different question: instead of predicting behavior under fixed rules, how should the rules be built when people will respond strategically?

The design question

A market rule is not neutral. It tells people what actions are allowed, what information is requested, how winners are chosen, and how payments are made. Once people know the rule, they adapt. The designer has to expect that adaptation. A good rule is not one that works when everyone behaves nicely. It is one that still works when each person follows their own incentives. This is why the course treats rules as part of the model rather than background scenery. The rule changes the meaning of each action.

Why auctions are central

An auction is a compact laboratory for hidden values, incentives, information, and rules. A first-price auction, second-price auction, common-value auction, reserve price, and ad auction all change what a bid means. The same person may bid differently under different rules because the payment rule changes the cost of winning.

Why revenue equivalence is fragile

Revenue equivalence is powerful because it says different auction formats can produce the same expected revenue under a tight set of assumptions. It is also easy to misuse. Change risk attitudes, budgets, entry, reserve prices, private values, common values, or collusion, and the result may no longer hold. The lesson is not that all auctions are the same. The lesson is to find exactly which assumptions are carrying the result.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Computer science

Ad auctions, matching systems, routing rules, and platform marketplaces are mechanisms. The designer writes rules for strategic users and automated agents. The practical question is whether the rule still produces useful outcomes when participants learn how to respond.

Public policy

School choice, spectrum auctions, procurement, and regulation all require rule design. The policy problem is not only what outcome the planner wants. It is whether the people inside the rule have reasons to reveal information and act in ways the rule needs.

Where Topology And Mathematics Enter Honestly

Topology is not a slogan for game theory. It enters through careful existence questions: when does a stable point have to exist inside a space of possible choices?

The plain version of a fixed point

A fixed point is a place where a rule sends you back to the same place. In game theory, the rough idea is this: take a possible description of what everyone might do, ask what each person would want to do in response, and look for a description that survives that response rule. If the response points back to the same description, you have a stable candidate.

Why spaces of choices matter

Some games may not have a stable answer if players can use only pure actions. Rock-paper-scissors is the everyday example: any pure choice can be beaten. Mixed strategies expand the space. Instead of choosing only one action, a player can choose probabilities over actions. That creates a smoother space of possible behavior. Existence theorems use facts about such spaces, such as being closed, bounded, and connected in the right way, to show that a fixed point exists.

What existence does and does not give you

An existence proof can be very important even when it does not tell you which equilibrium people will play or how quickly they will find it. It says the model is not empty. It also warns you to check uniqueness, learning, computation, and interpretation separately. A topological proof can guarantee a stable point under assumptions, but it cannot by itself tell you whether the stable point is good, easy to reach, or relevant to the real setting.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Topology and mathematics

The honest connection is through continuity, compactness, convexity, and fixed points. These words describe the shape of the strategy space and the behavior of response rules. They matter because equilibrium existence often depends on shape, not on a single numerical trick.

Algorithms

Once existence is known, computation becomes the next question. A stable point may exist but be hard to find. This is why computer science asks about algorithms, approximation, learning dynamics, and complexity rather than stopping at the theorem.

Why Any Of This Is Important

The course matters because it teaches a habit: explain outcomes by looking at the structure of choice, not only at intentions, morals, or labels.

It makes hidden pressure visible

Many outcomes look confusing until you ask who can move, what they know, what they want, what they expect, and what happens later. Then the pressure becomes visible. A person may stay silent because speaking is not credible. A firm may keep a high price because rivals can punish cuts. A bidder may shade a bid because the payment rule makes winning expensive. A government may fail to cooperate because future enforcement is weak. The value is not that one model explains everything. The value is that the model forces the important pressure into the open.

It improves criticism

Game theory is useful even when you reject a model. It gives you precise questions for rejection. Which payoff is wrong? Which action was omitted? Which information assumption fails? Which future consequence was ignored? Which rule changes incentives? A vague objection says the model is unrealistic. A first-principles objection says exactly where the model loses the real problem.

It helps design better settings

If behavior responds to incentives, better outcomes often require better settings rather than louder wishes. Rules, timing, information, penalties, verification, repeated contact, and outside options can change what people find worth doing. The practical value of the course is not memorizing names. It is learning how to rebuild the situation so good behavior is easier to sustain.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Research

A researcher can use the course as a checklist for a model: players, actions, information, timing, payoffs, solution concept, evidence, and limits. That checklist makes the argument easier to test.

Work and civic life

The same habit helps outside research. Before blaming individuals, inspect the rules and incentives. Before trusting a policy, ask how people will respond. Before accepting a prediction, ask whether the prediction survives the choices of the people inside it.

How To Turn A Messy Situation Into A Game

A game is a careful simplification. It takes a messy situation and writes down who chooses, what each person can choose, what each person knows, and what each person gets.

Start before the table

A payoff table is not the first step. The first step is the ordinary story. Who is actually making a decision? What can each person really do? Which choices happen at the same time, and which choices happen after observing something? What does each person care about? If those questions are skipped, the table may look clean while hiding the real problem. The course uses strategic-form games because a table makes simultaneous pressure visible, but the table is only useful after the real choices have been named honestly.

Why representation changes the answer

The same situation can look different when represented differently. If a threat is hidden inside a simultaneous table, the reader may miss that the threat would not be carried out later. If a private fact is treated as public, the reader may miss why a signal or belief matters. If the action set is too small, the model may force people into choices they would not actually face. First-principles work means asking whether the representation preserves the pressure that made the situation interesting in the first place.

The practical reading test

When reading any model, translate it back into everyday language. A player is a decision maker. An action is something that person can choose. A payoff is the comparison the model says the person cares about. Information is what the person can observe before choosing. Timing is the order in which choices and observations happen. If you cannot translate those parts back into the real setting, the formal model may be doing more hiding than explaining.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Computer science

A distributed system, platform market, or security protocol can be represented as a game only after the agents, allowed moves, observations, and payoffs are named. A bad representation can make a protocol look robust while ignoring the action an attacker would actually use.

Everyday life

A family disagreement or workplace conflict often changes once the real action set is named. Someone may appear stubborn when the actual options are unsafe, costly, or invisible to the other person.

Removing Bad Choices Without Pretending To Know Everything

Dominance and rationalizability teach a modest habit: remove choices that cannot be defended, but be honest about how much prediction remains afterward.

The simple idea behind dominance

A choice is dominated when another choice does better no matter what the other people do. If an action loses in every relevant comparison, a careful player should not use it. This is not a deep psychological claim. It is a bookkeeping claim about comparisons. The value is that it lets the reader simplify a game without first solving the whole game. You can say, this move is never worth taking, and remove it from the live problem.

Why rationalizability is weaker than prediction

Rationalizability asks which choices can still make sense under some belief about what others might do. That is weaker than saying what will happen. It protects the reader from overclaiming. A move can survive because there is some coherent belief that makes it a best response. If many moves survive, the model has not failed. It has told you that the available assumptions are not enough to force a sharp prediction.

The common wrong shortcut

The wrong shortcut is to delete anything that looks unlikely or unattractive. Dominance is stricter than that. A move is not dominated just because it performs badly against one guess about the opponent. It must be worse across the relevant comparisons. The second wrong shortcut is to treat survival as endorsement. A surviving move is possible under the model, not necessarily wise, likely, fair, or socially good.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Law and institutions

In legal design, some options can be ruled out because they are never worth taking once penalties and benefits are counted. But many behaviors may remain possible, which means the rule has not pinned down behavior by itself.

Research

A model can use dominance as a cleaning step. If the remaining set is large, the honest result is a range of rationalizable behavior, not a single story forced by the math.

Why Random Choice Can Be A Serious Strategy

Mixed strategies are not about being confused. They are about refusing to be predictable when predictability can be exploited.

Why pure choices can fail

In some conflicts, any fixed move invites a response. If a goalie always dives left, the kicker shoots right. If a platform always checks the same transactions, bad actors route around the check. If a player always chooses rock, the opponent chooses paper. A pure action can be too easy to exploit. Random choice becomes useful because it makes the other person's best response less sharp. The point is not that the player loves randomness. The point is that predictability has become a weakness.

What mixing equalizes

A mixed strategy usually makes the other player indifferent among the actions the model expects them to use. That word indifferent means the payoffs are tied, not that the person emotionally does not care. The tie is doing work. If one opponent action paid more, the opponent would lean toward it, and the proposed randomization would not be stable. The math finds probabilities that remove a profitable lean.

Why zero-sum games are special

In a zero-sum game, one side's gain is the other side's loss. That makes security reasoning clean. A player can ask what result they can guarantee against the worst response. But most social and economic settings are not zero-sum. There may be gains from trade, future cooperation, side payments, or rule changes. The security logic is powerful when interests are directly opposed and incomplete when the setting has room for joint improvement.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Cybersecurity

Random audits, rotating defenses, and unpredictable checks use the same logic. A fixed defense pattern can be learned. Randomness can protect a system by making exploitation harder.

Biology

Predator and prey behavior can have this structure when a predictable pattern is costly. Variation may persist because each side is responding to the other side's ability to exploit regularity.

Sequential Games Ask What Will Still Make Sense Later

When choices happen in order, the reader has to test every future plan at the moment when that future choice would actually be made.

Trees show what tables hide

A game tree shows order. It shows who moves first, what the next person observes, and where the decision path can go. This matters because many promises and threats look strong when spoken early and weak when tested later. A table can hide that weakness. A tree forces the reader to ask whether the later person will really choose the announced action after the earlier path has happened. The tree also shows which facts are observed before each decision, so it keeps timing and information tied together.

Backward induction in everyday words

Backward induction means solve the later choice first. Ask what the player at the last decision would actually do. Then move one step earlier and treat that later answer as part of the earlier player's situation. This is not a trick. It is the ordinary logic of planning. If you know a store will be closed when you arrive, that future fact changes whether you leave now.

Why subgame perfection tightens equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium can include a threat that would not be used if the threatened path happened. Subgame perfection removes that kind of weak support. It says the plan must be sensible not only at the start, but after every history where a real decision remains. The result is a cleaner test of credibility. It does not make people honest. It checks whether later incentives support the claimed plan.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Negotiation

A deadline, walk-away option, or final offer changes bargaining because later choices discipline earlier claims. A negotiator should ask what each side will still prefer once the deadline arrives.

Politics

Threats of veto, escalation, or punishment must be tested at the later moment. If carrying out the threat later is too costly, opponents may not believe it now.

Repetition Can Support Cooperation, But Only Under Conditions

Repeated games explain how the future can discipline the present. They do not say that repeated contact automatically creates cooperation.

The future becomes part of today's payoff

If people meet again, today's action can change tomorrow's treatment. A firm that cuts price today may gain customers now and trigger a price war later. A worker who breaks trust today may save effort now and lose future opportunities. The present choice now includes two parts: the immediate payoff and the effect on future behavior. That is the heart of repeated-game reasoning. The reader should always compare the one-time gain from deviation with the stream of future losses that deviation may cause.

Why finite and infinite repetition differ

A known final period removes future discipline at the end. If the final round has no future punishment, cooperation may fail there. Once that is known, the previous round may also lose its reason to cooperate. Infinite or indefinite repetition changes the logic because there may always be a future consequence. The difference is not mystical. It is about whether future rewards and punishments remain available when a player is tempted to deviate.

The folk theorem as a warning

The folk theorem says that many outcomes can be supported when players are patient enough and punishments are available. That is both powerful and dangerous. It means cooperation, collusion, and many other patterns may be possible. It also means the model may predict too much unless the writer explains which equilibrium is selected, how players coordinate on it, what they observe, and why punishments are credible.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Markets

Repeated pricing can support implicit collusion when firms observe each other and care enough about future profits. But the explanation weakens with noisy demand, secret discounts, legal penalties, or firms that cannot punish effectively.

Everyday life

Trust in a long relationship often rests on future consequences. But if the relationship is ending, if behavior is hard to observe, or if punishment is too costly, repetition alone may not sustain trust.

Private Information Changes What Actions Mean

When one person knows something others do not, actions become evidence. Prices, bids, education, messages, and contracts can all change beliefs.

Types are hidden facts that matter

A type is a private fact that changes payoffs or choices. A worker may be high skill or low skill. A buyer may value an object a lot or a little. A firm may have low costs or high costs. Once types exist, a model cannot only ask what action is best. It must ask what each person believes about the hidden type and how observed behavior changes that belief. The same outward action can mean different things depending on which type would want to take it.

Signaling and screening face opposite directions

In signaling, the informed person moves in a way that may reveal their type. Education can signal skill if it is less costly for skilled workers. In screening, the uninformed side designs choices that lead different types to separate themselves. A menu of contracts can screen customers if each type prefers a different option. Both ideas are about sorting hidden types through incentives.

Why belief consistency matters

A model with hidden information needs beliefs that fit the observed path. If an action is supposed to convince others, the reader should ask why that action would not also be attractive to the wrong type. If an off-path event happens, the reader should ask what beliefs are assigned there and whether the conclusion depends heavily on those beliefs. Hidden-information models often turn on these quiet assumptions.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Insurance and health

Adverse selection appears when people know more about their own risk than the insurer or planner. Prices and contract menus can change who participates, which can change the whole market.

Online systems

Badges, reviews, deposits, account age, and verified identity can all act as signals. They matter only if the system makes false signaling costly enough or uses menus that sort users honestly.

Auction Rules Change The Meaning Of A Bid

A bid is not just a number. Its meaning depends on the rule that turns bids into winners and payments.

Why the payment rule matters

In a first-price auction, winning means paying your own bid. That gives the bidder a reason to shade below value. In a second-price auction, winning means paying the next highest bid, so truthful bidding can become attractive under the right assumptions. The same value can produce different bids under different rules. A reader should never interpret a bid until the payment rule is clear. The rule decides whether a bid is mainly an offer to pay, a report of value, or a strategic compromise between winning and paying less.

Private values and common values are different problems

In a private-value auction, each bidder's value is mainly their own. In a common-value auction, the object has a shared value that bidders estimate with different signals. The winner's curse appears when winning is bad news about the estimate: if you won, others may have seen lower signals. That changes bidding because the act of winning reveals information.

Ad auctions add platform design

Ad auctions are not only textbook auctions with internet labels. Platforms care about bids, relevance, user attention, budget constraints, pacing, and repeated participation. The rule may combine bid and quality. The advertiser may care about clicks, conversions, or long-run learning. The platform may care about revenue and user experience. The first-principles move is to identify the objective and rule before borrowing a textbook result. A result about one object sold once may not travel cleanly to repeated automated bidding for attention.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Public policy

Procurement and spectrum auctions use rules to shape bids, entry, and allocation. A rule that raises revenue may not produce the best public use if participation, budgets, or collusion change.

Computer science

Modern ad systems are algorithmic mechanisms. Bidders may be automated agents, budgets may bind over time, and platform rules can change behavior at scale.

Communication Works Only When Incentives And Shared Understanding Support It

Messages, announcements, and shared facts matter because they change beliefs and coordination. They do not work just because words are spoken.

Cheap talk needs aligned incentives

Cheap talk is communication that is costless and nonbinding. A costless message can still matter, but only when the sender has a reason to say something useful and the receiver has a reason to believe it. If every type of sender wants to make the same claim, the message cannot separate types. If the receiver knows that, the words may carry little information. The practical question is not whether a message sounds informative. The question is why this sender would choose that message when lying was also available.

Common knowledge is stronger than shared information

It is not enough that everyone knows a fact. Sometimes everyone must know it, know that everyone knows it, know that everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on. That recursion can matter for coordination. If people are unsure whether others received the same signal, they may fail to coordinate even when the useful action is obvious to each person alone. Common knowledge is therefore not decorative logic. It explains why a public signal can change behavior more than many private signals.

Why this matters for institutions

Public announcements, standards, legal rules, and platform policies can create shared reference points. But the announcement must be understood, credible, and connected to incentives. A rule nobody expects to be enforced may fail. A signal that only some people observe may not coordinate behavior. A message from a biased sender may persuade only if the receiver can explain why the sender would not lie.

Where The Same Reasoning Appears

Politics

Public commitments, polls, endorsements, and official announcements can coordinate expectations. They work best when people believe others saw the same information and expect it to affect behavior.

Networks

Technical standards and platform policies create coordination points. A standard succeeds partly because each adopter expects others to adopt it too.
