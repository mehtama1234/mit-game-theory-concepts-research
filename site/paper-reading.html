Paper Reading Guide

A transfer layer for new papers, models, and applied writeups. Each card starts from the wording a reader may see, then maps it back to the course's ordinary problem, mathematical handle, common misread, and transcript-backed concepts.

Paper reading guide

When a paper hides wants inside an objective function

Paper signal: Look for words like objective, loss, reward, utility, welfare, payoff, preference, cost, risk, surplus, or social value. The paper may sound technical, but first ask whose want is being turned into a number.

Everyday reading: The ordinary problem is that people, firms, platforms, or algorithms cannot compare actions until their wants are put on a common scale. A paper may call that scale a loss function, reward, payoff, or welfare objective. In game theory language the same move is a payoff or utility representation: it converts messy preferences into numbers that can be compared across possible choices.

First-principles test: Ask what would have to be true for the number to mean anything. If a larger number means the actor likes the outcome more, the paper is using the number as a ranking device. If the number also averages across uncertainty, then the paper has moved from ranking finished outcomes to judging risky choices before the world is revealed.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical object is a function, such as u(outcome) or E[u(outcome)]. The function takes a possible world and returns a number; the expectation weights those numbers by how likely the worlds are. The concept behind the math is not decoration: it is the bridge from a story about wanting to a calculation about choosing.

What to check in the model: Check whether the paper explains who owns the objective, whether the scale is ordinal or cardinal, whether uncertainty is averaged honestly, and whether the objective is a proxy for something harder to measure. A mechanism-design paper may choose the rule, but the players still choose using their own payoffs, so keep the designer's objective separate from participant incentives.

Common misread: Do not treat the objective as the truth. It is a modeling commitment. If the payoff omits delay, risk, private information, outside options, or strategic responses, the algebra can be correct while answering the wrong human question.

Course bridge: The opening MIT lectures build this reading habit before games become complicated: preferences become utility, utilities become payoffs in strategic-form games, and expected utility explains choices before uncertainty resolves. Later auction and mechanism lectures reuse the same move when rules turn private values and payments into incentives.

Paper section audit: Read the introduction for the human want being modeled, then read the model section for the exact number that stands in for that want. In the assumptions paragraph, mark whether the paper only needs the order of preferences or whether it needs numerical gaps between outcomes. In the results section, translate each theorem back into the sentence, under this payoff mapping, this actor prefers this choice to that choice.

Claim-to-mechanism check: A payoff-based claim becomes real only when the paper shows how the number changes across actions. If a rule is said to increase welfare, find whose welfare rises, whose falls, and whether transfers are counted as real gains or only moved between people. If a bidder, firm, or platform is said to choose rationally, trace the comparison table that makes one action larger than the alternatives.

Transfer failure warning: This reading move fails when the measured score is only a convenient proxy. A model can maximize click value, revenue, utility, or welfare while leaving out annoyance, fairness, long-run trust, or strategic adaptation. When moving this idea to a new paper, do not ask only whether the formula is solved; ask whether the formula is still a faithful enough stand-in for the thing people actually care about.

Paper reading guide

When a paper says equilibrium, fixed point, stability, or no one wants to move

Paper signal: Look for equilibrium, fixed point, stable outcome, mutual best response, self-consistent behavior, no profitable deviation, stationary behavior, or convergence to a stable rule. Those phrases usually mean the paper is asking whether everyone can be content at once, given everyone else.

Everyday reading: The ordinary problem is that one person's best action depends on the actions of others. A plan that looks good alone may stop being good once everyone reacts. Equilibrium is the discipline of checking whether the proposed outcome survives that circular pressure: each participant looks around, takes the others as given, and still has no better move.

First-principles test: Freeze everyone except one actor. Does that actor want to change? Repeat the same check for every actor. If every local check passes, the outcome is stable in the narrow equilibrium sense. If one actor can improve, the paper has either not found an equilibrium or is using a different notion of stability.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical handle is a best-response condition: a_i is in BR_i(a_-i). In plain language, player i's action is among the best actions against the other actions. Nash equilibrium asks that this be true for all players at the same time. A fixed point appears because each person's chosen action must match what the best-response rule recommends.

What to check in the model: Check whether the paper proves existence, computes a candidate, or merely assumes equilibrium behavior. Then check the information structure, timing, and allowed deviations. A Nash claim in a simultaneous model is weaker than a subgame-perfect claim in a dynamic model, and a mixed equilibrium is not the same thing as people being indifferent about the real world.

Common misread: Equilibrium does not mean fair, efficient, morally good, globally optimal, or likely after any learning process. It means no modeled participant has a unilateral profitable deviation under the model's exact rules.

Course bridge: The MIT sequence moves from dominance and rationalizability into best response and Nash equilibrium, then shows why dynamic and incomplete-information games need stronger versions. This is the same reading move behind subgame perfection, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, and perfect Bayesian equilibrium.

Paper section audit: In a paper, start by finding the candidate outcome before reading the proof. Then inspect the model section for the players, actions, information, and payoff comparisons that define a possible deviation. In the theorem, underline whether the authors prove existence, uniqueness, stability, computation, or comparative statics. Those are different claims: existence says at least one self-consistent outcome is possible, while stability says something about pressure after disturbance.

Claim-to-mechanism check: For each equilibrium claim, force the paper into a deviation checklist. Pick one participant, hold the others fixed, and ask what alternative was available, what payoff it would have produced, and why the stated action survived. If the proof uses a fixed-point theorem, read that as a guarantee that the best-response arrows have at least one place where they point back to the same behavior.

Transfer failure warning: Equilibrium language is easy to over-transfer. A paper may show no one wants to move under the model, but that does not mean the outcome is fair, efficient, learnable, robust, or likely in a lab or market. When applying this card elsewhere, keep the stability notion narrow until the paper separately proves dynamics, welfare, or empirical relevance.

Paper reading guide

When a paper studies threats, promises, timing, or commitment

Paper signal: Look for extensive form, sequential game, commitment, credible threat, credible promise, dynamic incentive, subgame, backward induction, renegotiation, or one-shot deviation. The paper is probably asking whether today's announced plan remains believable when tomorrow arrives.

Everyday reading: The ordinary problem is that time separates words from later incentives. A firm can threaten a price war, a government can promise not to bail someone out, and a negotiator can announce a final offer. The key question is not whether the sentence was spoken. The key question is whether carrying it out will still be the speaker's best move at the later moment.

First-principles test: Jump to the future point where the threat or promise must be executed. At that point, ignore the earlier speech and compare the remaining choices. If the announced action is not best at that future point, earlier players should not treat it as credible unless some commitment device changed the later payoff.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical handle is backward reasoning on a tree. Backward induction solves the last decision first, then passes the resulting value back to earlier nodes. Subgame perfection keeps Nash-style stability but applies it after every possible history, including histories that might not occur if everyone follows the predicted path.

What to check in the model: Check whether the model gives the actor an actual commitment device, changes future payoffs, or simply assumes the threat is believed. In dynamic papers, also check whether off-path histories matter: a claim can look stable along the main story while failing when the model asks what happens after an unexpected move.

Common misread: Do not read every threat as a constraint. A threat only constrains behavior if the future actor would actually want to carry it out, or if some institution, contract, reputation, or technology changes the future choice set.

Course bridge: The MIT lectures on extensive-form games, backward induction, subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium, bargaining, and one-shot deviation all teach the same discipline: future incentives discipline present claims. Repeated games then show how future punishment can make present cooperation credible when the future is valuable enough.

Paper section audit: When the paper contains a game tree, do not read it from left to right only. First list the future decision points where threats, promises, or punishments would actually be carried out. Then read the proof backward from those points. In the model section, look for commitment devices, contracts, reputational payoffs, or irreversible moves; without one of these, an early announcement is just speech.

Claim-to-mechanism check: A credibility claim needs a future-payoff comparison. At the later node, the actor must prefer carrying out the announced action to abandoning it. If the paper says an entrant is deterred, a bargainer concedes, or a platform punishes, locate the later incentive that makes the action believable after the unexpected history has already happened.

Transfer failure warning: The dangerous transfer is to treat timing vocabulary as proof of discipline. Sequential structure alone does not make threats credible, and backward induction can predict collapse of a plan that sounds powerful at the start. In a new paper, any result about commitment should be treated as weak until you find the object that changes later incentives.

Paper reading guide

When cooperation depends on the shadow of future punishment

Paper signal: Look for repeated game, folk theorem, relational contract, reputation, collusion, dynamic enforcement, trigger strategy, patience, discount factor, or long-run incentive. These signals mean today's gain is being compared with tomorrow's consequences.

Everyday reading: The ordinary problem is that many relationships are not one-shot. A supplier can cheat once and lose future business, firms can undercut a cartel today and face price punishment later, and online platforms can reward or exclude future participants. Repetition creates a new kind of discipline: people may give up a short-term gain to preserve a valuable future.

First-principles test: Separate the immediate temptation from the future loss. If cheating today gives a small extra payoff but triggers a long stream of lower payoffs later, cooperation can be stable. If the future is too unlikely, too small, or too far away, the punishment cannot hold the arrangement together.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical handle is discounted value. A repeated payoff stream is read as today plus delta times tomorrow plus delta squared times the next period, and so on. Delta is the weight placed on future payoffs. The inequality compares one-time cheating against the value of preserving the cooperative path.

What to check in the model: Check what the punishment is, whether it is itself credible, how patient the players must be, and whether monitoring is good enough to identify cheating. A paper may claim cooperation, but the claim depends on the future being sufficiently valuable and on the punishment path also passing its own incentive check.

Common misread: Do not say repetition automatically creates cooperation. Repetition only helps when future value, detection, and credible punishment are strong enough. Without those pieces, repeated interaction may simply repeat the one-shot conflict.

Course bridge: The course's repeated-game lectures connect discounting, folk theorem logic, implicit cartels, and one-shot deviation. They show how the same equilibrium idea changes when a current action changes tomorrow's continuation value.

Paper section audit: Read repeated-game papers by separating the stage game from the continuation rule. The stage game tells you what someone is tempted to do today; the continuation rule tells you what tomorrow looks like after cooperation or cheating. In the assumptions, mark how players observe behavior, how long the relationship lasts, and how much the future is discounted. Those three details carry much of the result.

Claim-to-mechanism check: The mechanism is an inequality between temptation and future loss. Write the paper's story as: gain from cheating now versus discounted value lost later. Then check whether the punishment path is itself credible, because a punishment that future players refuse to carry out cannot discipline present behavior. This is where repeated games quietly return to subgame-perfect reasoning.

Transfer failure warning: Do not transfer the folk-theorem intuition as a blanket claim that patience solves conflict. Cooperation can fail when monitoring is noisy, relationships end, people are impatient, punishments hurt the punisher too much, or outside options change. A new paper must show that the future is valuable and observable enough, not merely that the actors meet more than once.

Paper reading guide

When actions are also evidence about hidden information

Paper signal: Look for type, signal, screening, adverse selection, posterior, belief update, private information, asymmetric information, separating, pooling, or perfect Bayesian equilibrium. These terms signal that choices are not just actions; they are clues.

Everyday reading: The ordinary problem is that one side knows something the other side does not. A seller knows product quality, a worker knows effort cost, a bidder knows value, and a sender knows what message serves them. Once information is hidden, behavior has two jobs: it changes payoffs directly and it changes what other people believe.

First-principles test: Ask what each actor knows at the moment of choice, what others can observe, and how the observation should change beliefs. If two hidden types can cheaply imitate the same behavior, the behavior cannot reveal the type. If imitation is costly for one type and cheap for another, the action may become an informative signal.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical handle is a belief over possible hidden types plus Bayes rule for updating that belief after observed behavior. In equilibrium, strategies and beliefs have to fit together: the action must be optimal given beliefs, and the beliefs must be consistent with the strategy wherever the observed path gives enough information.

What to check in the model: Check whether the paper states the type space, prior beliefs, observed signals, and off-path beliefs. In signaling and screening work, inspect the incentive constraints: a proposed separating outcome only works if each type prefers its assigned action to imitating the other type.

Common misread: Do not treat messages or actions as automatically truthful. A signal works because lying or imitation changes payoffs, not because the model assumes honesty. Cheap talk can transmit information only under specific alignment or strategic conditions.

Course bridge: The Bayesian games, types and beliefs, perfect Bayesian equilibrium, signaling, cheap talk, and adverse-selection lectures all teach this reading move. They turn hidden information from background color into an explicit object that affects action, belief, and stability.

Paper section audit: For private-information papers, draw three columns before trusting the math: what each actor knows, what each actor observes, and what each actor believes after observation. In the model section, find the type space and prior. In the equilibrium section, check whether the proposed behavior separates types, pools types together, screens them with menus, or communicates through messages.

Claim-to-mechanism check: A claim about learning from behavior needs an imitation test. Ask whether a low-quality seller, high-cost worker, weak bidder, or strategic sender would want to mimic the other type. If imitation is too cheap, the action cannot carry the claimed information. If the paper uses Bayes rule, track exactly which observation changes the probability of each hidden type.

Transfer failure warning: The common failure is to treat observed behavior as transparent evidence. In strategic settings, people choose signals partly because others will interpret them. When transferring this idea to a paper about markets, platforms, hiring, or communication, do not infer hidden quality from action until the model explains why the wrong type would not take the same action.

Paper reading guide

When the paper changes the rules instead of only predicting play

Paper signal: Look for mechanism, auction format, allocation rule, payment rule, incentive compatibility, truthful bidding, revenue equivalence, market design, reserve price, ranking rule, or platform design. The paper is probably treating rules as the object being engineered.

Everyday reading: The ordinary problem is that a designer cannot directly command private values, effort, or truthfulness. The designer can choose rules: who gets the object, who pays what, what information is requested, and what happens after reports arrive. Good rules make self-interested behavior reveal or produce the outcome the designer needs.

First-principles test: Read the rule from the participant's point of view. If I report truthfully, what do I get and pay? If I shade, hide, or imitate someone else, what changes? A mechanism claim is serious only when the desired behavior is not just requested but made attractive by the rule itself.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical handle is an incentive constraint. A truthful or assigned action must give at least as much payoff as the best misreport or deviation. In auctions, expected payment and allocation probabilities connect private value to bidding behavior; in ad auctions, ranking and payment rules turn platform design into strategic incentives.

What to check in the model: Check whether the paper distinguishes the allocation rule from the payment rule, whether it proves truthful behavior or assumes it, whether participants are risk-neutral or risk-averse, and whether the revenue or welfare claim depends on distributional assumptions. Also check whether the mechanism remains sensible when values are correlated or information is richer than the model allows.

Common misread: Do not treat a well-designed rule as a moral instruction. Mechanisms work by changing consequences, not by asking participants to be good. If the incentive constraint fails, the intended outcome can collapse even when the rule sounds reasonable.

Course bridge: The auction and ad-auction lectures show this shift most directly: instead of taking the game as fixed, the analyst chooses the game form. Earlier payoff, best-response, and Bayesian concepts still matter because the participants solve the designed game using their own incentives and beliefs.

Paper section audit: In mechanism and market-design papers, read the rules before reading the welfare or revenue claim. Identify what participants report, what the rule allocates, what payments or penalties follow, and what information the designer lacks. Then read the proof from a participant's chair: if I lie, shade, wait, split identity, or imitate a different type, what exactly happens to my payoff?

Claim-to-mechanism check: The central check is whether the desired behavior is an incentive, not a wish. For truthfulness, compare truthful reporting with each profitable-looking misreport. For auctions, separate the allocation rule from the payment rule because the first decides who wins and the second shapes how people bid. For platform rules, ask whether ranking, reserve prices, or fees change participant behavior in the claimed direction.

Transfer failure warning: A mechanism can look elegant while depending on thin assumptions about risk, values, independence, entry, or information. Do not transfer a result from a clean auction to a messy marketplace until you ask what happens when bidders are correlated, budgets bind, identities are strategic, or the platform's measured objective misses real user welfare.

Paper reading guide

When everyone knowing is not enough

Paper signal: Look for common knowledge, higher-order belief, public signal, announcement, coordination, p-belief, global games, cheap talk, or information structure. These signals mean the paper cares not only about facts, but about who knows that others know them.

Everyday reading: The ordinary problem is that coordinated action often requires more than private awareness. If I know a bridge is unsafe but do not know whether you know, I may still hesitate. If a public announcement makes the same fact visible to everyone, it can change behavior because everyone can reason about everyone else's awareness.

First-principles test: Ask how many layers of awareness the action requires. Does each person need the fact, or do they also need confidence that others have the fact and will expect them to act on it? If the latter, the paper is about knowledge recursion rather than just information delivery.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical handle is recursive belief: I know X, I know that you know X, I know that you know that I know X, and so on. Common knowledge is the limiting version of that recursion. The point of the math is to represent coordinated certainty that cannot be captured by counting informed individuals one by one.

What to check in the model: Check whether the signal is public or private, whether messages are costless or costly, and whether the equilibrium relies on shared interpretation of the signal. In communication papers, also check whether incentives are aligned enough for words to carry information instead of strategic noise.

Common misread: Do not confuse a true fact with a coordinating fact. A private fact can be true and still fail to move behavior if people cannot rely on others having the same awareness. Publicity changes the strategic environment, not the physical fact.

Course bridge: The common-knowledge and cheap-talk lectures make this point explicit, while rationalizability and Nash equilibrium rely on related layers of reasoning about others. The public-announcement case turns the abstract recursion into a concrete reading test.

Paper section audit: For papers about public information, inspect the communication channel before the result. Ask who receives the signal, whether everyone knows who received it, and whether everyone expects the same interpretation. In the proof, watch for a jump from individual knowledge to coordinated action. That jump is legitimate only if the model supplies the layers of awareness needed for people to reason about one another.

Claim-to-mechanism check: A common-knowledge claim works by changing expectations, not by changing the physical fact alone. Trace how the signal changes what I believe about the world, what I believe you believe, and what I believe you expect me to do. If the model uses cheap talk, add the incentive check: would the speaker still want to send this message when it changes the listener's action?

Transfer failure warning: Do not transfer public-announcement logic to every information release. A dashboard, press release, model explanation, or market signal can fail if people see it at different times, doubt its source, interpret it differently, or suspect strategic messaging. A fact can be true, widely distributed, and still not coordinate behavior unless the higher-order belief structure is strong enough.
