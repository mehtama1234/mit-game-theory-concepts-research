Lecture 1 · 9,945 words · Choice Under Tradeoffs, Strategic Dependence, Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Lecture 1: Introduction to Individual Decision-Making

Builds the individual-choice foundation: preferences, utility, beliefs, lotteries, and the idea that choices need a payoff language before strategy can begin.

Argument Arc

The lecture starts before game theory: a player cannot reason strategically until the model says what that player prefers. It builds from ranked outcomes to utility numbers and then to risky choices, so later payoff tables are not arbitrary decoration.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as representation. A preference ordering becomes a utility function, and uncertain outcomes become expected utility. The point is not to measure happiness; it is to make comparisons precise enough for later strategic checks.

Worked Mini-Example

If someone chooses between a sure payment and a lottery, the model needs more than a list of outcomes. It needs a rule for comparing risk, so the lecture turns probability-weighted consequences into a choice object.

Common Failure

The easy mistake is to treat utility as a psychological substance. In this course, utility is a compact ranking device that lets later game models compute what a player would rather do.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that game theory starts with a language for individual tradeoffs before it talks about strategic conflict. Preferences say what is better than what; utility compresses those rankings into numbers; expected utility adds uncertainty without pretending the numbers are a happiness meter.

Reader Work

While reading this lecture, build a small preference table before writing any utility numbers. Mark which comparisons are rankings, which comparisons involve uncertainty, and which claims would change if the utility scale were stretched. The work is to separate choice order from psychological interpretation.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: explain why two different utility scales can represent the same preference ordering, then give one situation where the spacing between utility numbers would start to matter. If you cannot separate ranking from scale, the later payoff tables will feel more objective than they are.

Handoff

This hands off to representation of games: once each player has a payoff language, the next problem is to describe who moves, what they can do, what they know, and how everyone's choices jointly create outcomes.

Lecture 2 · 12,369 words · Choice Under Tradeoffs, Strategic Dependence, Time, Threats, And Credibility, Private Information And Beliefs

Lecture 2: Representation of Games

Turns social situations into formal objects: players, histories, strategies, strategy profiles, payoffs, and the difference between trees and compact strategic form.

Argument Arc

The lecture asks how a messy interaction becomes an object that can be solved. It separates players, actions, strategies, histories, information, and payoffs, then shows why tables and trees reveal different parts of the same strategic situation.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through the game form. A strategic-form table compresses simultaneous choices; an extensive-form tree exposes order, histories, and information. The representation chooses which constraints the later solution concept can see.

Worked Mini-Example

A threat may look persuasive in a payoff table because the table hides timing. Draw the same situation as a tree and the later player may not want to carry out the threat after the history arrives.

Common Failure

The mistake is to think representation is bookkeeping. It is a modeling claim. If the form hides timing or information, the equilibrium calculation can be exact and still answer the wrong question.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that a game form is an argument about what matters. A strategic-form matrix emphasizes complete plans chosen at once; an extensive-form tree exposes histories and later choices. Neither is neutral bookkeeping.

Reader Work

For each example, redraw the situation twice: once as a compact action table and once as a timeline. Then write what each representation hides. The work is to notice that representation is already an argument about timing, information, and available choices.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: take one example and say what would change if the move order were reversed or if one player could not observe an earlier action. If the answer changes, the representation is doing real modeling work rather than simply drawing the story.

Handoff

This hands off to dominance: once the situation has been represented, the first question is whether any option can be rejected before making detailed beliefs about what everyone else will do.

Lecture 3 · 12,267 words · Strategic Dependence

Lecture 3: Dominance

Introduces pruning: some strategies can be ruled out because another strategy does better across the relevant contingencies.

Argument Arc

The lecture introduces the first disciplined way to simplify a game. Instead of guessing what players believe, it asks whether one strategy is worse than another across the relevant possibilities and can therefore be removed.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as an inequality across contingencies. A strategy is dominated when another strategy gives at least as much payoff in every relevant case and more in some case.

Worked Mini-Example

If one restaurant location earns less than another no matter what a rival does, the worse location can be removed before doing any equilibrium reasoning. The deletion comes from comparison, not intuition.

Common Failure

The mistake is to delete strategies because they seem unlikely. Dominance is stronger: it requires a payoff comparison that survives all opponent actions covered by the model.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that dominance is proof-based simplification. If another strategy always does better, the dominated strategy does not need a story about beliefs; it is removed because the payoff comparison itself rules it out.

Reader Work

Make a column-by-column deletion log. For every proposed dominated action, record the rival contingency, the two payoffs being compared, and whether the inequality survives. The work is to earn the deletion by proof instead of by taste or plausibility.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: find one action you want to delete and prove the deletion without using the word likely. If your explanation depends on what you expect the opponent to do, you are making a belief argument, not a dominance argument.

Handoff

This hands off to rationalizability: many games do not have obvious dominated actions, so the next question is whether an action can be justified by some coherent belief about the other players.

Lecture 4 · 13,209 words · Strategic Dependence, Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency, Communication And Knowledge

Lecture 4: Rationalizability

Adds beliefs to pruning. A strategy can remain plausible if some coherent belief about others makes it a best response.

Argument Arc

The lecture relaxes pure deletion and asks which strategies can be justified by some coherent belief about others. It turns the question from 'is this always bad?' to 'could this be optimal under a defensible belief?'

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through best responses to beliefs. A strategy survives if there is a belief over others' actions that makes it payoff-maximizing for the player.

Worked Mini-Example

A move that is not dominant may still be rationalizable if a player reasonably expects the rival to choose a particular action. The model asks whether some belief can make the move optimal.

Common Failure

The mistake is to confuse rationalizable with predicted. Rationalizability says a move can be justified; equilibrium will later require everyone's justifications to fit together.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that beliefs are part of rational choice. A strategy is rationalizable when there is some belief about others that makes it a best response, so the model separates individual defensibility from collective stability.

Reader Work

Pick one action that looks odd and try to rescue it with a belief about the other player. If no belief makes it optimal, identify the payoff comparison that blocks it. The work is to distinguish individual defensibility from final prediction.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: defend an action that is not your favorite prediction by constructing a belief that makes it optimal. Then state why that defense still falls short of equilibrium. This checks whether you understand survival under belief as weaker than mutual consistency.

Handoff

This hands off to Nash equilibrium: once individual strategies can be justified by beliefs, the next demand is that those justifications close into one consistent best-response loop.

Lecture 5 · 13,551 words · Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency, Time, Threats, And Credibility

Lecture 5: Nash Equilibrium

Closes the best-response loop: every player must be choosing optimally given the others' choices.

Argument Arc

The lecture closes the loop created by best responses. A proposed outcome must survive each player asking, given what the others do, whether changing alone would improve their payoff.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as mutual best response. For every player, the chosen action or strategy must lie in that player's best-response set against the other players' choices.

Worked Mini-Example

Two firms may each like a different price in isolation. A Nash check asks whether either firm would cut or raise price after seeing the rival's chosen price held fixed.

Common Failure

The mistake is to read equilibrium as good, fair, or socially desirable. Nash only says no single player has a profitable one-person deviation.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that Nash equilibrium is a consistency check. Each player's action must be a best response to the others, so the prediction is not a wish; it is a point where unilateral regret has disappeared.

Reader Work

Take every proposed outcome and run a one-player deviation audit. Freeze everyone else, test one player, then move to the next player. The work is to stop asking whether the outcome feels good and ask whether unilateral regret remains.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: reject one tempting outcome by identifying exactly one profitable unilateral deviation. Then find one outcome where that same one-player switch test fails for every player. This separates Nash stability from efficiency or moral appeal.

Handoff

This hands off to imperfect competition: the best-response loop becomes concrete when firms choose prices or quantities and each firm's profit depends on what rivals do.

Lecture 6 · 13,639 words · Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Lecture 6: Imperfect Competition

Applies strategic reasoning to firm competition, where prices or quantities interact through demand and payoff consequences.

Argument Arc

The lecture turns markets into games. It starts from demand, monopoly markup, and market power, then asks what changes when more than one firm can affect price, quantity, and profit.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through firm profit functions and best-response curves. A firm's profit depends on its own choice, rival choices, market demand, and cost, so optimization becomes strategic.

Worked Mini-Example

Two sellers of bottled water choose quantities. If one produces more, total quantity rises and price may fall. The other seller's best quantity is therefore tied to the first seller's choice.

Common Failure

The mistake is to treat imperfect competition as monopoly with extra names. The core idea is interaction: each firm has some power, but rivals make that power strategically constrained.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that market power can be relational. A firm's best move depends on how rivals respond, and the equilibrium captures the mutual adjustment between individual profit motives and market-level consequences.

Reader Work

Write each firm's profit as depending on its own choice and the rival's choice before solving anything. Then derive a best response against a named rival action. The work is to see market power as relational rather than as isolated monopoly logic.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: write a sentence that begins, 'my firm's best output depends on your output because...' and finish it with demand, price, or cost. If the rival disappears from the sentence, you have slipped back into single-agent optimization.

Handoff

This hands off to zero-sum games by sharpening a boundary: some competition is exact opposition, but many economic settings mix rivalry with shared surplus and market structure.

Lecture 7 · 12,829 words · Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency

Lecture 7: Zero-Sum Games

Studies exact conflict, where one player's gain is the other's loss and minimax/security reasoning becomes natural.

Argument Arc

The lecture studies exact conflict, where one player's gain is the other's loss. This special structure makes security reasoning natural: each player asks how to protect against the opponent's best attack.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through maxmin, minimax, and mixed strategies. The player compares worst-case payoffs and may randomize to avoid being exploited by a predictable action.

Worked Mini-Example

In matching pennies, choosing heads every time can be exploited. Randomizing evenly makes the opponent unable to target a fixed pattern, which gives the player a security value.

Common Failure

The mistake is to apply zero-sum intuition everywhere. Many economic games mix conflict with shared surplus, future cooperation, or side effects, so exact-opposition logic may overstate rivalry.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that zero-sum structure makes caution mathematically precise. A player can ask what payoff is guaranteed against the opponent's best attack, and randomization can protect against being too predictable.

Reader Work

Add the players' payoffs in each cell before using zero-sum language. If the total is fixed, compute what each action protects against; if not, name the shared gains or losses. The work is to keep security reasoning inside its proper boundary.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: show why randomizing can protect a player from being targeted. Then name one economic setting from the course that is competitive but not zero-sum. This checks both the power and the boundary of minimax-style reasoning.

Handoff

This hands off to backward induction: after studying simultaneous exact conflict, the course turns to time, where future choices can make present promises or threats credible or empty.

Lecture 8 · 12,791 words · Time, Threats, And Credibility, Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 8: Backward Induction

Makes credibility calculable by solving later choices first and folding their values back into earlier decisions.

Argument Arc

The lecture reverses the natural storytelling order. Instead of starting at the first move, it solves the last decision first and uses that continuation value to judge earlier choices.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as recursive value assignment. At each decision node, the player chooses the branch with the best continuation payoff, and that value is passed backward through the tree.

Worked Mini-Example

If a seller threatens to reject a future offer but would accept once that future node arrives, the threat should not shape earlier play. Solving backward exposes that problem.

Common Failure

The mistake is to believe every announced future action. Backward induction asks whether the action will still be optimal when the future decision actually has to be made.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that time turns prediction into a recursion. Later optimal choices become continuation values, and those values are folded backward until earlier players can see the real consequences of moving now.

Reader Work

Start from the last decision nodes and write the action the future player will actually want there. Replace those nodes with values before moving earlier. The work is to make present threats answer to future incentives instead of narrative force.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: identify a threat in the lecture and solve the node where that threat would be executed. If the threatened action is not optimal there, explain how the earlier story changes. This is the core credibility move.

Handoff

This hands off to negotiation: bargaining is a dynamic problem where offers, refusals, patience, and outside options all depend on what happens after today's choice.

Lecture 9 · 11,475 words · Time, Threats, And Credibility, Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 9: Negotiation

Treats bargaining as strategic surplus division shaped by offers, rejection, timing, patience, and outside options.

Argument Arc

The lecture treats bargaining as surplus division under timing pressure. Offers, rejection, patience, and outside options are not personality traits; they become payoff consequences inside a dynamic game.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through continuation values and discounting. Waiting can shrink payoffs, so an offer is judged against what each side expects from rejecting and continuing.

Worked Mini-Example

A buyer and seller can split value today or wait for another offer tomorrow. If waiting is costly, the side that can credibly wait longer may receive a larger share.

Common Failure

The mistake is to explain bargaining power as confidence or stubbornness. The model asks what each side can get by refusing now and moving to the next bargaining stage.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that bargaining is a dynamic comparison of continuation values. An offer is attractive or not because of what each side expects from delay, rejection, and future bargaining positions.

Reader Work

Separate total surplus, each side's outside option, and the cost of waiting. Then change one of those three inputs and trace how the bargaining position changes. The work is to translate toughness into continuation values rather than personality.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: change only one outside option or waiting cost and predict how the split should move. If your answer uses toughness without changing a continuation payoff, you have left the model and returned to informal personality language. Then state which side's threat point actually improved.

Handoff

This hands off to subgame perfection: if a bargaining path uses future refusals or punishments, those continuation actions must be rational when the later subgame is reached.

Lecture 10 · 12,012 words · Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency, Time, Threats, And Credibility

Lecture 10: Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

Repairs Nash for dynamic games by requiring continuation behavior to remain rational inside every subgame.

Argument Arc

The lecture repairs Nash for dynamic settings. It requires strategies to remain equilibrium behavior after every proper subgame, so threats and promises must be credible at the histories where they matter.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters by applying Nash equilibrium inside each subgame. A full strategy profile must pass the no-deviation test not only at the start but after relevant histories.

Worked Mini-Example

A firm may threaten a price war after entry. Subgame perfection asks whether the price war would actually be profitable once entry has happened, not whether it scares entrants beforehand.

Common Failure

The mistake is to check only the opening node. A dynamic prediction can look stable initially while relying on later behavior no player would want to carry out.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that credibility is local. A strategy must remain a Nash equilibrium inside every relevant subgame, so the solution concept rejects plans that survive only because impossible-looking histories were ignored.

Reader Work

For every threat or promise, jump to the history where it would be carried out and solve from there. The work is to test credibility locally, not just at the initial node where the threat changes behavior.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: name a Nash-looking strategy profile that relies on a later threat. Then test the threat inside the subgame after the relevant history. If it fails there, explain why Nash at the start was too weak.

Handoff

This hands off to the one-shot deviation principle: once dynamic credibility is the standard, the next problem is finding a practical way to check many possible deviations.

Lecture 11 · 12,985 words · Time, Threats, And Credibility, Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Lecture 11: One-Shot Deviation Principle and Bargaining

Adds a practical dynamic-equilibrium check and returns to bargaining with sharper tools for deviations over time.

Argument Arc

The lecture gives a practical way to check dynamic optimality and then applies it to bargaining. Instead of comparing every possible strategy change, it asks whether a single local deviation can improve the continuation payoff.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as a local deviation test under the theorem's conditions. If no profitable one-shot deviation exists at any history, the strategy can pass the dynamic optimality check.

Worked Mini-Example

In an alternating-offer bargain, ask whether a player would gain by rejecting just this offer or changing just this proposal while leaving future behavior fixed.

Common Failure

The mistake is to use the one-shot test without checking the environment. The shortcut is powerful because theorem conditions make local deviations informative, not because all strategy changes are always local.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that theorems buy convenience by adding assumptions. When the conditions hold, dynamic optimality can be audited history by history with a manageable local test.

Reader Work

Choose a history and change exactly one current action while keeping the continuation plan fixed. Record the immediate gain and future loss. The work is to understand why a local deviation test is powerful only under the theorem's conditions.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: choose one history and compare the planned action with exactly one current deviation. Then say which theorem condition makes this local check informative. If you cannot name the condition, the shortcut is being used mechanically.

Handoff

This hands off to finitely repeated games: the same dynamic tools now face a setting with a known last period, where backward reasoning can unravel cooperation.

Lecture 12 · 11,423 words · Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 12: Finitely Repeated Games

Shows why a known last round can unravel cooperation even when the game is played many times.

Argument Arc

The lecture shows how a known endpoint can undermine cooperation. Even many repeated rounds may unravel if everyone knows the last round has no future punishment left.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through backward induction over periods. The last stage is solved first; if cooperation fails there, the same logic can roll backward into earlier stages.

Worked Mini-Example

Two firms might want to keep prices high for ten rounds, but in round ten there is no later punishment. If round ten unravels, round nine loses its punishment threat too.

Common Failure

The mistake is to think repetition alone creates cooperation. The future must still have disciplinary force, and a known last round can remove that force.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that repetition only matters when the future can discipline the present. A finite horizon with a commonly known endpoint can make future punishment vanish exactly when it is needed.

Reader Work

Solve the final period first and write what future punishment is unavailable there. Then move one period earlier and repeat. The work is to see how a known endpoint can travel backward through incentives. If cooperation survives in your notes, name the extra force stopping the unraveling.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: write the final-round incentive first, then explain how that final answer affects the previous round. If you still predict cooperation, name the extra ingredient that blocks backward unraveling, such as reputation outside the modeled game.

Handoff

This hands off to infinitely repeated games: removing the known endpoint allows future punishment and reward to become a real enforcement device again.

Lecture 13 · 12,974 words · Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 13: Infinitely Repeated Games

Removes the known last round and turns future punishment into a possible enforcement mechanism, where ongoing relationships can change one-shot incentives.

Argument Arc

The lecture removes the known final period and makes the future a possible enforcement device. Cooperation can survive when the discounted loss from punishment outweighs today's temptation.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through discounted payoff sums and incentive constraints. A player compares the one-period deviation gain against the present value of future punishment.

Worked Mini-Example

A firm can cut price today and steal customers, but rivals may punish with low prices afterward. Cooperation works only if the firm values future profit enough.

Common Failure

The mistake is to say indefinite repetition creates trust by itself. The lecture's claim is an inequality: patience and punishment must make deviation unattractive.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that patience can create self-enforcement. Players may cooperate without external contracts when future consequences are valuable enough and punishments are credible enough.

Reader Work

Write the cooperate path and the cheat-then-punish path side by side with discount factors attached. The work is to compare today's temptation with the present value of future consequences, not simply to say repetition creates trust.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: write the deviation gain on one side and the discounted punishment value on the other. Then lower the discount factor and say which side becomes harder to satisfy. This turns 'long relationship' into an incentive inequality.

Handoff

This hands off to the folk theorem: once future incentives can sustain cooperation, the next question is how many payoff patterns can be supported by suitable reward and punishment schemes.

Lecture 14 · 11,322 words · Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 14: Folk Theorem

Explains how patient repeated games can sustain many feasible and individually rational payoff patterns.

Argument Arc

The lecture expands the set of sustainable outcomes in patient repeated games. It teaches possibility and multiplicity: many payoff patterns can be stable when punishments and patience are strong enough.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through feasible payoffs, individual rationality, discount factors, and punishment constructions. The theorem characterizes a set rather than one unique prediction.

Worked Mini-Example

If players can reward and punish across a long relationship, they may sustain many different divisions of surplus, not just the one-shot Nash outcome.

Common Failure

The mistake is to treat the folk theorem as a prediction that cooperation happens. It often says many things can happen, which raises a new selection problem.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that possibility is not the same as prediction. Repeated-game incentives can support many arrangements, which is powerful for explanation but creates a selection problem for forecasting. The theorem teaches how enforcement works, while also warning that enforcement alone may not identify which convention players will coordinate on.

Reader Work

Pick one candidate long-run payoff and check three gates: feasibility, individual rationality, and punishment credibility. The work is to treat the theorem as a supportability test, then separately ask what selects one supported outcome.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: pick a payoff vector and classify it as infeasible, feasible but not individually rational, or potentially supportable. Then explain why supportable does not mean selected. This checks the theorem's central possibility-versus-prediction distinction. Add the punishment story that would make the payoff self-enforcing.

Handoff

This hands off to implicit cartels: the course applies repeated-game enforcement to firms and asks when high prices can be self-enforcing without an explicit contract.

Lecture 15 · 13,451 words · Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 15: Implicit Cartels

Applies repeated-game enforcement to firms that may sustain collusion without an explicit enforceable contract.

Argument Arc

The lecture applies repeated-game logic to firms that may keep prices high without an explicit enforceable contract. The strategic question is whether future punishment can discipline today's undercutting.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as a cartel incentive constraint. Collusive profit today plus future cooperation is compared with the one-time cheating gain plus future punishment profit.

Worked Mini-Example

A gas station could cut price and win customers today. If nearby stations respond with a price war, the discounted future loss may make quiet high pricing self-enforcing.

Common Failure

The mistake is to infer explicit agreement from high prices alone. The model explains an incentive mechanism; real antitrust claims require evidence about communication, monitoring, and market conditions.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that self-enforcement has two sides. Cheating must be unattractive for the potential cheater, and punishment must also be worthwhile for the firms expected to punish.

Reader Work

Name the cheating gain, the monitoring signal, and the punishment that rivals are supposed to impose. Then ask whether punishment is itself profitable. The work is to separate a repeated-game mechanism from legal evidence of explicit agreement. Also mark whether demand shocks could be mistaken for cheating, because monitoring quality is part of the incentive story.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: describe a high-price pattern twice: once as explicit agreement and once as tacit repeated-game discipline. Then list what evidence would distinguish the two. This prevents the model from being mistaken for a legal conclusion.

Handoff

This hands off to Bayesian games: after studying repeated behavior under known payoffs, the course adds hidden information about values, costs, and types.

Lecture 16 · 13,970 words · Choice Under Tradeoffs, Private Information And Beliefs

Lecture 16: Bayesian Games

Introduces hidden types and priors so strategy can be modeled when players do not share the same information.

Argument Arc

The lecture begins the incomplete-information part of the course. It introduces types and priors so hidden values, costs, beliefs, or payoff states become part of the game rather than vague uncertainty.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through type spaces and probability distributions. A strategy can become a rule from types to actions, and payoffs are evaluated in expectation over unknown types.

Worked Mini-Example

A bidder knows their own value but not a rival's value. The model represents the bidder's value as a type and the rival's unknown value through a prior distribution.

Common Failure

The mistake is to say players are uncertain and leave it there. Bayesian games force the model to name what is hidden, who knows it, and what probabilities others assign.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that private information changes the object of strategy. A player no longer chooses just an action; the model often needs a rule mapping each type to an action.

Reader Work

Before solving, list the hidden types, who observes each type, and the prior probabilities. Then write a strategy as a rule for every type. The work is to stop using uncertainty as a vague excuse and put it into the model.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: rewrite a complete-information strategy as a type-contingent rule. Then identify the prior belief that lets other players reason about hidden types. If there is no type rule, the private-information structure is not yet modeled.

Handoff

Argument Arc

The lecture uses incomplete-information representation to solve applied games. Equilibrium is now a plan for each type, and each type must be optimizing against beliefs about other types.

The math enters through expected-payoff best responses for type-contingent strategies. Each type asks whether its assigned action maximizes expected utility given the strategy rules of others.

Worked Mini-Example

In a first-price auction, a low-value bidder and high-value bidder follow different bidding rules. Bayesian Nash checks the incentive of each value type, not only the average bidder.

Common Failure

The mistake is to check one action per player. With private information, the strategy is a function from types to actions, and each type needs its own deviation check.

Durable Lesson

Reader Work

Check incentives type by type. Hold the other players' type-contingent rules fixed and ask whether each type wants to deviate. Self-test: choose two types and check their deviations separately. If one type wants to deviate while another does not, explain why an average-player check would miss the failure. This is the applied BNE audit.

Lecture 21: Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium

Combines dynamic credibility with belief updating in games where histories can reveal private information and observed actions change what players infer.

The math enters as a strategy profile plus a belief system. At each information set, actions must be optimal given beliefs, and beliefs must follow Bayes' rule on path.

Worked Mini-Example

After observing a price or signal, a buyer forms a belief about seller type and chooses whether to buy. PBE checks both the seller's signal and the buyer's belief-driven response.

Common Failure

The mistake is to solve only strategies and treat beliefs as commentary. In PBE, beliefs are part of the equilibrium object.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that equilibrium now has two coupled parts: strategies must be optimal given beliefs, and beliefs must be updated consistently where the path of play gives evidence.

Reader Work

Write strategies and beliefs in pairs. After every observed action, ask whether Bayes rule pins down the belief and whether the action is optimal under that belief. The work is to keep off-path assumptions visible.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: after an observed action, write both the receiver's belief and the receiver's best response. Then mark whether the belief was pinned down by Bayes rule or chosen off path. This keeps belief discipline visible.

Lecture 22: Signaling

Studies how privately informed senders can influence receivers through observable actions that may reveal type.

The math enters through sender types, signal choices, receiver beliefs, receiver actions, and incentive constraints that stop one type from profitably mimicking another.

Worked Mini-Example

Education can signal ability if high-ability workers find education less costly or more worthwhile. If low-ability workers would copy the same education level, the signal pools rather than separates.

Common Failure

The mistake is to think costly automatically means informative. A signal works only if the wrong type does not want to imitate it under the receiver's response.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that information can be carried by incentive differences. A signal works when the cost-benefit tradeoff differs across types so the receiver can rationally update after seeing it.

Reader Work

For each type, compare the payoff from sending the signal with the payoff from imitating another type's signal. Then update the receiver's belief from the behavior that survives. Self-test: test whether the low type wants to imitate the high type's signal and whether the high type wants to separate. If both types prefer the same visible action, explain why the receiver should not infer separation.

An insurer posts a price. The buyers who accept may be the people with the highest expected costs, so raising the price can worsen the pool rather than simply increase revenue per buyer.

An advisor can recommend an action. If the advisor and decision maker want similar outcomes, the message may guide action; if interests conflict sharply, every type may send the same persuasive message.

The mistake is to say cheap talk is always useless or always informative. The lecture's answer is conditional: speech works when incentives make the receiver's interpretation defensible.

Lecture 25: Common Knowledge

Climbs from private knowledge to shared higher-order knowledge, where everyone knows and knows that others know.

Argument Arc

The lecture climbs from private knowledge to shared higher-order knowledge. Coordination may require not only that everyone knows a fact, but that everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through recursive knowledge operators. Starting with an event, the model repeatedly applies the idea that everyone knows the event and everyone knows that others know it.

Worked Mini-Example

If every firm privately hears a rule change, each may still wonder whether others heard it. A public announcement can change behavior because it also tells everyone that others heard it.

Common Failure

The mistake is to treat true, known, and common knowledge as identical. Strategic coordination can depend on the publicness and higher-order structure of knowledge.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that publicness is a strategic resource. Common knowledge turns a fact into a coordination object by making higher-order beliefs line up across players.

Reader Work

For every fact in the example, label whether it is private, mutual, public, or common knowledge. Then name the coordinated action that needs higher-order confidence. The work is to distinguish shared information from shared awareness.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: construct a case where everyone privately knows the fact but no one knows whether everyone else knows. Then add a public announcement and explain which belief levels change. This tests the difference between truth and publicness.
