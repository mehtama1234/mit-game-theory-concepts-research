Problem Drills

Small transfer problems for checking whether the reader can choose the right concept, name the math move, avoid the common wrong turn, and return to transcript evidence.

Problem drill

Separate wants from strategy before solving

Scenario: A city is deciding whether to subsidize two transit options. A rider likes shorter travel time, lower price, and fewer transfers, but no other strategic player has entered the story yet.

Reader task: Decide whether this is already a game-theory problem or whether the first job is to represent one person's ranking of outcomes.

Setup pressure: The pressure is that a reader may see a policy choice and immediately look for strategic interaction. But before another strategic player matters, the model has to know what the rider is trying to get. Otherwise later payoff numbers are unexplained labels.

First-principles answer: The first job is not equilibrium. It is preference and utility representation. Before strategic interaction can be modeled, the reader needs a disciplined way to say which outcomes the rider ranks above others and when a numerical payoff is only preserving that ranking.

Math move: Use a preference ordering or utility function. The mathematical move is mapping outcomes to an ordered scale, not yet computing a best response. If uncertainty about travel time or price enters, expected utility weights possible outcomes before the rider chooses.

Worked resolution: List the outcomes first: cheap but slow, fast but expensive, fewer transfers but longer walk, and so on. Then ask which outcomes the rider ranks above which. Only after the ranking is represented should the analyst decide whether other players, such as agencies or competing riders, enter the model.

Assumption check: Check whether the utility numbers are being used only to preserve order or whether the argument needs stronger claims about money, welfare, risk, or comparisons across people. If the policy claim compares riders against taxpayers, the model needs more than one rider's preference ranking.

Common wrong turn: Jumping straight to incentives or equilibrium hides the simpler missing object: what the chooser is trying to get. It also makes later strategic conclusions look objective even though the payoff construction was never defended.

Transfer prompt: When reading a new model, find the first payoff table and ask: what preference ranking created these numbers, and what real-world comparison would fail if the ranking were wrong?

Evidence checkpoint: The early decision-making evidence anchors the idea that the course builds individual choice language before representing strategic games.

Solve from scratch: Solve this by writing the outcome list before writing any game table. Put the rider in the center, list the concrete outcomes, then mark which comparisons are about time, price, transfers, risk, or comfort. Only after the ranking is visible should you introduce another actor. If no other actor changes the ranking, the drill ends with preferences and utility rather than equilibrium.

Answer breaks if: The answer breaks if the city, transit agencies, taxpayers, or rival riders directly change the rider's payoff through their own choices. Then the preference map is only the first layer. The problem must be expanded into a strategic interaction because the rider's best option now depends on how someone else acts.

Problem drill

Choose the representation before choosing the solution

Scenario: A startup may enter a market. The incumbent announces that it will cut prices if entry occurs. The entrant must decide whether the threat should change its entry decision.

Reader task: Decide whether a flat payoff table is enough, or whether the situation needs a tree with later decision points.

Setup pressure: The pressure is timing. The threat is spoken before entry, but the costly price cut would be chosen after entry. A flat table can hide that later moment and make the announcement look like a fixed fact instead of a future choice.

First-principles answer: The story is about timing and credibility, so a tree is doing real work. The entrant should not merely record the threat; it should ask what the incumbent would want after entry has already happened.

Math move: Use extensive-form representation and backward induction. The mathematical move is solving later continuation choices before assigning meaning to earlier threats. A threat matters only if it survives the later best-response comparison.

Worked resolution: Draw entrant first: enter or stay out. If entry happens, draw incumbent second: fight or accommodate. Compare the incumbent's payoffs at that later node. If accommodation is better after entry is sunk, the entrant should evaluate entry against accommodation, not against the earlier announced fight.

Assumption check: Check whether the incumbent really gets to choose later, whether fighting has extra reputation value outside this market, and whether the entrant understands those payoffs. If an external commitment device exists, the simple tree must be amended.

Common wrong turn: Treating the threat as a fixed payoff-table entry makes an empty threat look like a real constraint. The wrong representation lets early speech do work that later incentives would not support.

Transfer prompt: For any threat in a paper, mark the date when it is spoken and the date when it must be carried out. If those dates differ, test the later node before believing the earlier speech.

Evidence checkpoint: The representation and backward-induction evidence anchors support the course's shift from games as tables to games as time-ordered decision problems.

Solve from scratch: Solve this by drawing time before payoffs. First mark the entrant's choice. Then mark the incumbent's later choice after entry is observed. At the later node, compare fight with accommodate using only payoffs that matter after entry has happened. Carry that later answer backward to the entrant's original decision, so the threat is judged by future incentives rather than by its announcement.

Answer breaks if: The answer breaks if the incumbent has a real commitment device, a reputation payoff in other markets, or a contract that changes the later comparison. In that case fight may become credible for reasons outside the simple tree, and the representation has to include those extra payoffs before backward induction is trusted.

Problem drill

Delete only after every comparison passes

Scenario: A firm has two pricing actions. High price beats low price when the rival is calm, but low price beats high price when the rival is aggressive.

Reader task: Decide whether low price can be deleted as dominated, and name exactly which rival cases must be checked before deletion is legitimate.

Setup pressure: The pressure is the lure of a favorite story. If the rival is probably calm, high price looks attractive. But dominance is not about the most plausible rival action; it is about whether one action loses across the whole action set.

First-principles answer: Low price cannot be deleted by dominance. A dominated action must lose against every relevant rival action. Here low price has a case where it performs better, so deleting it would smuggle in a belief about the rival rather than using dominance.

Math move: Use the dominance inequality as a universal comparison across rival actions. The important word is every, not likely. A belief-weighted expected payoff comparison may come later, but it is a different mathematical move.

Worked resolution: Make two columns: rival calm and rival aggressive. Compare high price versus low price in each column. High price wins in the calm column, but low price wins in the aggressive column. Since each action has a column where it can be defended, neither action is removed by dominance.

Assumption check: Check whether the rival's listed actions are complete and whether the payoff entries use the right objective. A missing rival action can make deletion look valid, and a wrong payoff mapping can prune the action that would matter in the real situation.

Common wrong turn: Deleting a move because it loses in the most salient case confuses dominance with prediction. That shortcut turns a model-free deletion rule into an unstated belief assumption.

Transfer prompt: When reading a proof that deletes strategies, underline the comparison set. If the proof does not compare against every relevant opponent action, keep the strategy alive and ask what belief or equilibrium argument replaces dominance.

Evidence checkpoint: The dominance and rationalizability evidence anchors support the distinction between ruling out impossible-to-justify moves and reasoning from beliefs.

Solve from scratch: Solve this as a grid checklist. Write each rival action as a separate column and compare the two pricing actions inside every column. Do not average columns, guess which column is likely, or use a story about the rival's mood. Dominance asks whether one action survives all columns. If each price wins somewhere, both remain live for later reasoning.

Answer breaks if: The answer breaks if the rival action list is incomplete or if the payoff numbers already include an unstated belief about the rival. A dominance conclusion also breaks if the author switches from strict to weak dominance without warning, because deletion may then depend on order or on what remains after previous pruning.

Problem drill

Check stability without confusing it with goodness

Scenario: Two firms both choose an aggressive price. Both would earn more if both chose a softer price, but either firm would gain by undercutting if the other stayed soft.

Reader task: Explain why the aggressive outcome might be a Nash equilibrium even though both firms dislike it relative to coordinated softness.

Setup pressure: The pressure is that the cooperative outcome sounds better for everyone, so it feels like it should be the prediction. Game theory separates that welfare comparison from the stability question: what can one player gain by moving alone?

First-principles answer: Nash equilibrium is a unilateral deviation test, not a test of collective welfare. If each firm is doing the best it can given the other's aggressive price, the outcome can be stable even when the firms would jointly prefer another outcome.

Math move: Use best responses and mutual consistency. Hold the other firm fixed, check one player's profitable deviations, then repeat for the other player. The proposed outcome passes only if no single-player change improves payoff.

Worked resolution: At aggressive-aggressive, freeze firm B at aggressive and ask whether firm A would rather switch. Then freeze firm A at aggressive and ask the same for B. If neither switch helps, the outcome is Nash. The fact that both would prefer soft-soft is a separate collective comparison.

Assumption check: Check whether firms can coordinate, whether side agreements are allowed, whether actions are simultaneous, and whether repeated punishment exists. A one-shot Nash check deliberately ignores joint deviations and future discipline unless the model adds them.

Common wrong turn: Rejecting an equilibrium because it is inefficient mistakes a prediction test for a social-desirability test. The model can predict an outcome precisely because every individual escape route is blocked.

Transfer prompt: In a new model, write two headings: unilateral stability and welfare. Do not let a result under one heading answer the other unless the paper explicitly proves the connection.

Evidence checkpoint: The Nash and best-response evidence anchors support the course's distinction between stable prediction and desirable outcome.

Solve from scratch: Solve this by running two separate tests. First, run the stability test: hold firm B fixed at aggressive and ask whether firm A improves by moving alone, then reverse the roles. Second, run the welfare comparison: compare aggressive-aggressive with soft-soft for both firms together. Keeping those tests separate is the point of the drill.

Answer breaks if: The answer breaks if firms can make binding agreements, move sequentially, punish later, or coordinate through an institution that changes unilateral incentives. Then the one-shot Nash diagnosis is incomplete. It may still be a useful baseline, but it no longer proves what happens in the richer setting. The diagnostic repair is to name the new escape route and choose the concept that tests it.

Problem drill

Compare today's temptation with tomorrow's punishment

Scenario: Two sellers have quietly kept prices high for months. One seller can gain today by secretly cutting price, but the other seller would retaliate with low prices afterward.

Reader task: Decide what calculation determines whether cooperation can survive, and identify the one-time temptation and future punishment terms.

Setup pressure: The pressure is that cheating is immediate while punishment is delayed. Repeated interaction only changes present behavior if the future loss is large enough after discounting and if the punishment can actually be triggered.

First-principles answer: The question is whether the future loss from punishment is large enough to outweigh the one-time gain from cheating. Repeated interaction does not automatically create discipline; patience and the punishment path matter.

Math move: Use a discounted payoff comparison. Current gains count fully, future gains or losses are multiplied by powers of the discount factor. The cooperation condition is an incentive constraint: stay payoff must beat cheat-now payoff plus future punishment.

Worked resolution: Name the pieces. Cheating gives an extra gain today. Punishment lowers later payoffs. If the seller values future periods heavily, the stream of lost future profit can outweigh today's gain. If the seller is impatient or expects the relationship to end soon, cheating can still win.

Assumption check: Check whether cheating is observed, whether retaliation is credible, whether the game has a known final period, and whether the discount factor is high enough. Without those assumptions, future discipline may be an attractive story but not a solved equilibrium.

Common wrong turn: Saying cooperation is possible because the game repeats skips the actual incentive comparison. Repetition supplies possible future consequences; it does not automatically make those consequences strong enough.

Transfer prompt: When a paper invokes future discipline, locate the deviation payoff, cooperative continuation payoff, punishment continuation payoff, monitoring rule, and discount factor. The mechanism lives in that comparison.

Evidence checkpoint: The repeated-games and cartel evidence anchors support the course's focus on future incentives as a mechanism, not a slogan.

Solve from scratch: Solve this by writing the incentive inequality in words first. Left side: value from staying cooperative today and keeping the future relationship. Right side: one-time cheating gain today plus the discounted value after punishment begins. Then inspect monitoring and credibility. The future only disciplines the present if cheating is detected and the punishment path is itself rational to carry out. Label the exact period where cheating is observed, because delayed detection weakens the punishment.

Answer breaks if: The answer breaks if cheating cannot be observed, if the punishment hurts the punisher too much to be credible, if the relationship has a known final period, or if the discount factor is too low. In any of those cases, repeated contact may not create enough continuation value to hold cooperation together.

Problem drill

Treat behavior as both choice and evidence

Scenario: A used-car seller chooses whether to offer a warranty. Buyers know that reliable sellers find warranties cheaper than unreliable sellers.

Reader task: Explain why the warranty decision can change buyer beliefs and why not every message would work the same way.

Setup pressure: The pressure is hidden type. Buyers cannot inspect quality directly, so they try to learn from behavior. But behavior is chosen strategically, which means the same action is informative only if different types have different incentives to take it.

First-principles answer: The seller's action is informative because different hidden types face different costs. A signal works when the types do not all want to mimic each other in the same way.

Math move: Use beliefs over types plus incentive compatibility. The reader should ask how likely each type is to choose the observed action and whether imitation is profitable. Belief updating and incentive checks have to agree.

Worked resolution: Suppose reliable sellers expect warranty costs of 1 and unreliable sellers expect warranty costs of 8. If the warranty raises price by 4, reliable sellers want it and unreliable sellers do not. Buyers can then treat the warranty as evidence of reliability because imitation is too costly for the bad type.

Assumption check: Check whether buyers observe the warranty, whether repair costs really differ by type, whether the price premium is large enough, and whether low-quality sellers can cheaply mimic. If imitation is cheap, the signal loses force.

Common wrong turn: Treating the warranty as a direct statement of quality ignores the incentive problem: low-quality sellers may want to imitate unless the cost structure stops them.

Transfer prompt: When a paper calls an action a signal, ask which type finds the action cheaper or more valuable, and write the imitation check before accepting the interpretation.

Evidence checkpoint: The Bayesian, type, and signaling evidence anchors support the course's treatment of hidden information as a joint problem of choice and interpretation.

Solve from scratch: Solve this by making a type-by-action table. Put reliable and unreliable sellers in separate rows, warranty and no warranty in columns, and write the payoff comparison for each type. Then ask what buyers should believe after seeing warranty. The signal works only if the action is chosen differently by different types for payoff reasons. After the table, write the posterior in words: warranty makes reliability more likely only because mimicry is too costly.

Answer breaks if: The answer breaks if unreliable sellers can mimic cheaply, if buyers do not observe the warranty, if repair costs do not differ by type, or if the price premium makes warranty attractive for everyone. Then the same action no longer separates types, so belief updating cannot carry the claimed information. The repair is to write the mimic payoff explicitly before accepting the signal.

Problem drill

Read auction rules as incentive machines

Scenario: A platform changes from a second-price ad auction to a rule that weights bids by predicted click quality. Advertisers respond by changing bids and ad designs.

Reader task: Identify why this is not only a pricing change but a mechanism-design problem.

Setup pressure: The pressure is that a rule changes behavior. Advertisers respond not only to prices but to ranking, payment, click probability, quality score, and the chance that a different action changes allocation.

First-principles answer: The rule changes how private values, bids, quality, allocation, and payment interact. The central issue is whether each advertiser's self-interested response produces the platform's intended allocation and revenue properties.

Math move: Use expected payment and incentive compatibility. Separate the event of winning, the price paid if winning, and how the rule changes the probability of winning. Then ask whether truthful or shaded behavior is privately sensible.

Worked resolution: Break the rule into allocation and payment. If a higher bid or better ad quality raises the chance of winning, compute the extra value from that higher chance. Then compare it with the extra expected payment. The platform's design goal is achieved only if those private calculations induce the desired ranking.

Assumption check: Check whether bidders know their values, whether quality scores are fixed or manipulable, whether payment is first-price or second-price-like, and whether the revenue-equivalence assumptions actually hold. Auction labels are not enough.

Common wrong turn: Looking only at the winning bid misses the strategic response created by the allocation rule. The mechanism is the whole mapping from bids and qualities to slots and payments.

Transfer prompt: For any auction-like system, write the allocation rule and payment rule in plain language before solving. Then ask what action each participant can change to improve expected payoff.

Evidence checkpoint: The auction, revenue-equivalence, and ad-auction evidence anchors support the course's treatment of rules as incentive-shaping objects.

Solve from scratch: Solve this by separating the rule into inputs and outputs. Inputs are bid, estimated click quality, and advertiser value. Outputs are slot allocation, probability of winning, payment, and expected surplus. Then ask how changing one input changes expected payoff. A mechanism-design answer has to show how private calculations under the rule create the platform's intended ranking. Read quality weighting as a price-like incentive, because it rewards some ad designs and penalizes others.

Answer breaks if: The answer breaks if quality scores are manipulable in an unmodeled way, if advertisers have budget constraints, if the payment rule is not what the paper assumes, or if revenue-equivalence assumptions fail. Auction vocabulary alone is not proof that bidders have the intended incentives. The repair is to recompute expected payoff under the actual allocation and payment rule, not under the auction label.

Problem drill

Distinguish private knowledge from common knowledge

Scenario: Every trader has quietly heard that a policy change is coming, but nobody knows whether the others have heard it. Later, the regulator announces the same fact publicly.

Reader task: Explain why the public announcement can change behavior even if the factual content was already privately known.

Setup pressure: The pressure is coordination under uncertainty about other people's knowledge. A trader's best action may depend less on the fact itself than on whether others know the fact and know that others know it.

First-principles answer: The announcement changes higher-order knowledge. After the public event, each trader knows the others heard it and can reason about the others reasoning from it.

Math move: Use knowledge recursion: the fact, everyone knows the fact, everyone knows everyone knows it, and so on. The mathematical object exists because private information and public information can support different strategic behavior.

Worked resolution: Before the announcement, selling may be risky because a trader is unsure whether others will also react. After the announcement, the trader can expect others to have the same public starting point. The first-order fact did not change, but the belief hierarchy did.

Assumption check: Check whether the announcement is public, whether everyone interprets it similarly, and whether coordinated action is payoff-relevant. If the message is private, ambiguous, or not trusted, common-knowledge reasoning is too strong.

Common wrong turn: Saying the announcement adds no information because the fact was already true ignores the publicness needed for coordination. The relevant object is not just the fact; it is shared awareness of the fact.

Transfer prompt: When a model uses a public signal, ask whether it changes first-order belief, higher-order belief, credibility, or all three. Do not collapse private knowledge and common knowledge.

Evidence checkpoint: The common-knowledge evidence anchor supports the course's distinction between knowing a fact and sharing the recursive awareness needed for strategic coordination.

Solve from scratch: Solve this by listing belief layers. Before the announcement, write: each trader may know the policy fact, but may not know whether others know it. After the public announcement, write: each trader knows others heard the same message and can expect them to reason from it. The action changes because the shared reasoning base changes. Keep the physical fact and the public event on separate lines.

Answer breaks if: The answer breaks if the announcement is not actually public, if traders interpret it differently, if the source is not trusted, or if the payoff does not depend on coordinated expectations. In those cases, the fact may spread without becoming the common starting point needed for strategic coordination. The repair is to model the message as private, noisy, or noncredible instead of common knowledge.
