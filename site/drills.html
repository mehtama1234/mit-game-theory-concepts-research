Problem Drills

Small transfer problems for checking whether the reader can choose the right concept, name the math move, avoid the common wrong turn, and return to transcript evidence.

Problem drill

Separate wants from strategy before solving

Scenario: A city is deciding whether to subsidize two transit options. A rider likes shorter travel time, lower price, and fewer transfers, but no other strategic player has entered the story yet.

Reader task: Decide whether this is already a game-theory problem or whether the first job is to represent one person's ranking of outcomes.

First-principles answer: The first job is not equilibrium. It is preference and utility representation. Before strategic interaction can be modeled, the reader needs a disciplined way to say which outcomes the rider ranks above others and when a numerical payoff is only preserving that ranking.

Math move: Use a preference ordering or utility function. The mathematical move is mapping outcomes to an ordered scale, not yet computing a best response.

Common wrong turn: Jumping straight to incentives or equilibrium hides the simpler missing object: what the chooser is trying to get.

Evidence checkpoint: The early decision-making evidence anchors the idea that the course builds individual choice language before representing strategic games.

Problem drill

Choose the representation before choosing the solution

Scenario: A startup may enter a market. The incumbent announces that it will cut prices if entry occurs. The entrant must decide whether the threat should change its entry decision.

Reader task: Decide whether a flat payoff table is enough, or whether the situation needs a tree with later decision points.

First-principles answer: The story is about timing and credibility, so a tree is doing real work. The entrant should not merely record the threat; it should ask what the incumbent would want after entry has already happened.

Math move: Use extensive-form representation and backward induction. The mathematical move is solving later continuation choices before assigning meaning to earlier threats.

Common wrong turn: Treating the threat as a fixed payoff-table entry makes an empty threat look like a real constraint.

Evidence checkpoint: The representation and backward-induction evidence anchors support the course's shift from games as tables to games as time-ordered decision problems.

Problem drill

Delete only after every comparison passes

Scenario: A firm has two pricing actions. High price beats low price when the rival is calm, but low price beats high price when the rival is aggressive.

Reader task: Decide whether low price can be deleted as dominated, and name exactly which rival cases must be checked before deletion is legitimate.

First-principles answer: Low price cannot be deleted by dominance. A dominated action must lose against every relevant rival action. Here low price has a case where it performs better, so deleting it would smuggle in a belief about the rival rather than using dominance.

Math move: Use the dominance inequality as a universal comparison across rival actions. The important word is every, not likely.

Common wrong turn: Deleting a move because it loses in the most salient case confuses dominance with prediction.

Evidence checkpoint: The dominance and rationalizability evidence anchors support the distinction between ruling out impossible-to-justify moves and reasoning from beliefs.

Problem drill

Check stability without confusing it with goodness

Scenario: Two firms both choose an aggressive price. Both would earn more if both chose a softer price, but either firm would gain by undercutting if the other stayed soft.

Reader task: Explain why the aggressive outcome might be a Nash equilibrium even though both firms dislike it relative to coordinated softness.

First-principles answer: Nash equilibrium is a unilateral deviation test, not a test of collective welfare. If each firm is doing the best it can given the other's aggressive price, the outcome can be stable even when the firms would jointly prefer another outcome.

Math move: Use best responses and mutual consistency. Hold the other firm fixed, check one player's profitable deviations, then repeat for the other player.

Common wrong turn: Rejecting an equilibrium because it is inefficient mistakes a prediction test for a social-desirability test.

Evidence checkpoint: The Nash and best-response evidence anchors support the course's distinction between stable prediction and desirable outcome.

Problem drill

Compare today's temptation with tomorrow's punishment

Scenario: Two sellers have quietly kept prices high for months. One seller can gain today by secretly cutting price, but the other seller would retaliate with low prices afterward.

Reader task: Decide what calculation determines whether cooperation can survive, and identify the one-time temptation and future punishment terms.

First-principles answer: The question is whether the future loss from punishment is large enough to outweigh the one-time gain from cheating. Repeated interaction does not automatically create discipline; patience and the punishment path matter.

Math move: Use a discounted payoff comparison. Current gains count fully, future gains or losses are multiplied by powers of the discount factor.

Common wrong turn: Saying cooperation is possible because the game repeats skips the actual incentive comparison.

Evidence checkpoint: The repeated-games and cartel evidence anchors support the course's focus on future incentives as a mechanism, not a slogan.

Problem drill

Treat behavior as both choice and evidence

Scenario: A used-car seller chooses whether to offer a warranty. Buyers know that reliable sellers find warranties cheaper than unreliable sellers.

Reader task: Explain why the warranty decision can change buyer beliefs and why not every message would work the same way.

First-principles answer: The seller's action is informative because different hidden types face different costs. A signal works when the types do not all want to mimic each other in the same way.

Math move: Use beliefs over types plus incentive compatibility. The reader should ask how likely each type is to choose the observed action and whether imitation is profitable.

Common wrong turn: Treating the warranty as a direct statement of quality ignores the incentive problem: low-quality sellers may want to imitate unless the cost structure stops them.

Evidence checkpoint: The Bayesian, type, and signaling evidence anchors support the course's treatment of hidden information as a joint problem of choice and interpretation.

Problem drill

Read auction rules as incentive machines

Scenario: A platform changes from a second-price ad auction to a rule that weights bids by predicted click quality. Advertisers respond by changing bids and ad designs.

Reader task: Identify why this is not only a pricing change but a mechanism-design problem.

First-principles answer: The rule changes how private values, bids, quality, allocation, and payment interact. The central issue is whether each advertiser's self-interested response produces the platform's intended allocation and revenue properties.

Math move: Use expected payment and incentive compatibility. Separate the event of winning, the price paid if winning, and how the rule changes the probability of winning.

Common wrong turn: Looking only at the winning bid misses the strategic response created by the allocation rule.

Evidence checkpoint: The auction, revenue-equivalence, and ad-auction evidence anchors support the course's treatment of rules as incentive-shaping objects.

Problem drill

Distinguish private knowledge from common knowledge

Scenario: Every trader has quietly heard that a policy change is coming, but nobody knows whether the others have heard it. Later, the regulator announces the same fact publicly.

Reader task: Explain why the public announcement can change behavior even if the factual content was already privately known.

First-principles answer: The announcement changes higher-order knowledge. After the public event, each trader knows the others heard it and can reason about the others reasoning from it.

Math move: Use knowledge recursion: the fact, everyone knows the fact, everyone knows everyone knows it, and so on.

Common wrong turn: Saying the announcement adds no information because the fact was already true ignores the publicness needed for coordination.

Evidence checkpoint: The common-knowledge evidence anchor supports the course's distinction between knowing a fact and sharing the recursive awareness needed for strategic coordination.
