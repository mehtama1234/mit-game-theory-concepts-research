Method Families

Equilibrium Family

Equilibrium methods turn strategic dependence into a stability test.

Family Problem

The family exists because strategic predictions are circular: what I should do depends on what you do, while what you should do depends on what I do. The method family looks for points where those expectations close on themselves.

First-Principles Pattern

Freeze everyone else, find one player’s best response, then require the same kind of check for every player or every relevant history/type. The family grows by repairing the basic check when time, randomization, or private information makes the simple version too weak.

Family Walkthrough

Start with a proposed outcome. Audit one player at a time. If someone can gain by changing while others stay fixed, the proposal is not stable. If the game has time, repeat the audit after every history. If the game has hidden information, repeat it for every type under the relevant beliefs.

Mathematical Signature

Best-response correspondences, fixed-point logic, deviation inequalities, mixed probabilities that make opponents indifferent, and refinements that require optimality in subgames or for every type.

Why This Family Matters

This is the central prediction engine of the course. It teaches that a model must survive the question each participant asks alone: given what I believe others will do, do I want to change?

Where The Analogy Breaks

Nash equilibrium is too coarse for dynamic threats and too silent about belief updating in private-information histories. The family is unified by deviation checks, but each member adds conditions for a different modeling pressure.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Nash evidence anchors mutual best response; mixed-strategy evidence shows why randomization can be necessary; subgame-perfection and PBE evidence show why later histories and beliefs demand stronger tests.

How To Read Papers In This Family

When reading a paper in this family, ask what counts as a deviation, what information the deviator has, and which histories or types must pass the test. The solution concept is usually the paper’s answer to those questions.

Naive Failure Case

The naive reading says, here is an outcome that sounds reasonable, so call it the prediction. That fails because every participant gets a private veto: if the outcome gives even one person a better unilateral move, the story unravels. A second naive reading says one equilibrium concept should cover every setting. That also fails. A simultaneous matrix, a sequential bargaining tree, and a signaling game ask the same stability question under different information and timing conditions.

Worked Model Pattern

To build a model in this family from scratch, first write the proposed behavior in ordinary language. Then list each player's possible one-person deviations. In a static game, compare payoffs while holding others fixed. In a dynamic game, repeat the check after later histories. In a private-information game, repeat it for each type and belief. The mathematical object is just bookkeeping for this audit: best responses, fixed points, mixed indifference, or sequential rationality.

Paper Diagnostic

A strong paper in this family tells you exactly which deviations are allowed and why the chosen solution concept is strong enough. Look for sentences that define the strategy space, information available at deviation time, and the equilibrium refinement. If the paper jumps straight to a claimed equilibrium, reconstruct the missing deviation checklist yourself before trusting the comparative statics or policy lesson.

Transfer Boundary

Do not transfer an equilibrium result as if stability meant desirability. The method proves that no modeled player wants to move alone under modeled beliefs. It does not prove the outcome is efficient, fair, robust to trembles, immune to coalitions, or credible under omitted histories. The transfer is strongest when the paper's institutional details match the deviation test; it is weakest when real players can communicate, renegotiate, learn, or coordinate outside the model.

Information And Belief Family

Information methods explain how private facts, beliefs, and messages affect strategy.

Family Problem

Information and belief methods are needed because players do not always share the same facts. A model has to track hidden types, private signals, messages, and the beliefs formed from them.

First-Principles Pattern

List the possible worlds, assign beliefs over them, let each type choose, then ask whether observed actions or messages should change beliefs. Communication concepts enter when actions are informative because different types have different incentives.

Family Walkthrough

Begin by asking what each participant knows that others do not. Translate that hidden fact into a type or state. Then ask what each type wants the receiver or opponent to believe, and whether the action observed is consistent with those incentives.

Mathematical Signature

Probability distributions over types, expected payoff sums, Bayes-rule updates on observed paths, separating and pooling conditions, and higher-order knowledge statements when public awareness matters.

Why This Family Matters

It keeps the course honest about uncertainty. Without this family, auctions, signaling, cheap talk, and PBE would pretend that everyone knows the same values and motives.

Where The Analogy Breaks

Belief-based models can become too flexible if off-path beliefs or priors are chosen casually. A signaling result also does not transfer to cheap talk unless the credibility mechanism is rechecked.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Bayesian-game and type evidence support hidden information; Bayesian-Nash evidence supports type-contingent best response; signaling, cheap-talk, and common-knowledge evidence support the communication and public-awareness extensions.

How To Read Papers In This Family

For papers in this family, identify the hidden variable first. Then inspect how beliefs are formed, whether messages are costly or cheap, and which incentive constraints prevent misleading behavior.

Naive Failure Case

The naive reading treats uncertainty as an inconvenience that can be averaged away. That misses the central issue: different people may know different facts, and actions can be attempts to change what others believe. Another naive reading treats every message as evidence. In this family, a message is informative only when the sender's incentives make imitation, lying, or pooling costly or unattractive enough.

Worked Model Pattern

To build a model in this family, start by naming the hidden fact in plain language: value, quality, ability, cost, intent, or signal. Turn that fact into types or states, then write what each type knows and what others initially believe. Next ask what action or message each type chooses. Only after that should Bayes' rule, posterior beliefs, separating behavior, pooling behavior, or common knowledge enter. The math tracks possible worlds and belief movement.

Paper Diagnostic

When reading a paper in this family, make a belief ledger. What is known before the action, what is observed, what belief changes afterward, and which incentive stops a low type from pretending to be a high type? If the result depends on off-path beliefs, mark that explicitly. If a cheap-talk result works, identify the payoff alignment that makes talk useful despite being costless.

Transfer Boundary

Information models can look more precise than their evidence allows. Priors, type spaces, and off-path beliefs may be modeling choices rather than measured facts. A signaling argument does not automatically transfer to cheap talk; a public announcement does not automatically create common knowledge; a Bayesian update does not help if the observed action has many possible causes. Transfer only after checking the credibility mechanism and the information channel.

Mechanism And Auction Family

Mechanism methods treat game theory as design for markets and institutions.

Family Problem

Mechanism and auction methods are needed because rules are not neutral containers. Participants react to the rule, and their reaction can either make the institution work or break it.

First-Principles Pattern

Specify the allocation rule, payment rule, information each participant holds, and the action space. Then check whether each participant’s best response under the rule produces the intended outcome.

Family Walkthrough

Write down what the designer controls and what participants privately know. For each possible report or bid, compute what the participant gets. A rule is credible only if the desired report or bid is at least as attractive as manipulation.

Mathematical Signature

Allocation functions, payment functions, expected revenue expressions, incentive-compatibility inequalities, bidding strategies, reserve or position rules, and assumptions about values or distributions.

Why This Family Matters

It shows game theory as engineering. Auctions and platforms are designed environments, so the central question is not only what equilibrium occurs but what rule creates useful incentives.

Where The Analogy Breaks

Revenue equivalence and auction prescriptions are assumption-sensitive. Risk aversion, affiliated values, budgets, entry, platform quality scores, repeated interaction, or collusion can change the predicted behavior.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Auction records support private-value bidding; revenue-equivalence records support the theorem-level comparison of rules; ad-auction records support platform-specific mechanism design; cartel records show informal rule enforcement through repeated interaction.

How To Read Papers In This Family

When reading mechanism papers, separate the designer’s objective from participants’ incentives. Then audit the assumptions that make truthful reporting, efficient allocation, or revenue claims go through.

Naive Failure Case

The naive reading treats a rule as if people will use it the way the designer intended. That fails because participants respond to rules strategically. A bidder shades, a seller sets a reserve, a platform weights quality, and a cartel uses future punishment. The rule does not merely organize behavior; it creates the payoffs that make manipulation, honesty, entry, or exit attractive.

Worked Model Pattern

To build a mechanism model, split the institution into pieces: who can participate, what private information they hold, what action or report they can send, how the object is allocated, and how payments are calculated. Then stand inside one participant's head and compare truthful behavior with manipulation. Incentive compatibility means the intended action wins that comparison. Revenue, efficiency, and welfare claims come only after that behavioral check is passed.

Paper Diagnostic

A strong mechanism paper exposes the allocation rule, payment rule, information assumptions, and participation assumptions before stating the theorem. Read every revenue or efficiency result by asking which assumption does the heavy lifting: independent values, risk neutrality, symmetric bidders, no budgets, no collusion, truthful reports, or platform quality estimates. If the paper changes the rule, ask which incentive constraint changed with it.

Transfer Boundary

Mechanism results are brittle when moved into real institutions. Budgets, risk aversion, correlated values, reserve prices, entry costs, repeated interaction, search frictions, quality scores, and measurement error can all change bidding behavior. The family transfers best when the implementation details are part of the model. It transfers worst when a theorem about clean private values is used to justify a messy platform rule without auditing manipulation channels.
