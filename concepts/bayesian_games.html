Bayesian Games

A game where some players know things the others do not. You might know your own cost or value, but the other player only guesses at it using probabilities—and your move depends on what you think they believe you will do.

What real-world problem is this about?

When you bid in an auction, you might know what an item is worth to you, but you do not know how much it is worth to your rivals. You must decide how much to bid, facing uncertainty about what they value. This is the core: strategic choice when you lack full information about who you are playing and what the other side cares about.

Why does this problem exist?

Private information reshapes what your best move is. If you must choose your bid without knowing your rival's value, you cannot just counter one known type—you have to make your move work against a range of possible types, weighted by how likely each one is. That is why games with hidden information demand a separate model.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is your best guess, expressed as a probability distribution, over what the hidden facts are. The formula is expected utility: `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).` Breaking it down: theta represents the secret type or state you do not observe; p(theta) is the probability you assign to it; and the sum gives your average payoff across all the possibilities you face. You pick the action that maximizes this average.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture starts by changing the game form. Instead of a single payoff table, you now have type-dependent payoff tables—one for each possible type. A player might know their own type (their own cost or value) while others hold only a belief (a probability) over what that type is. Next, strategies change. A strategy is no longer 'bid 50'; it is 'if I am the high-value type, bid 60; if low-value, bid 30.' This is type-contingency. The payoff for each player is an average—expected utility over the types they do not observe, weighted by the probabilities they assign. The lecture unpacks this before equilibrium because the private information itself creates the strategic environment. You cannot solve an equilibrium until you have set up what each player knows, what they believe, and what depends on type.

Mathematical Intuition

The key intuition is expected utility over hidden types. When you choose an action, you do not know which type you are facing, so you average your payoff across all possibilities. Priors tell you the weight—60% for one type, 40% for another. You pick the action that makes this average as high as possible. Everyone is doing the same: choosing to maximize their average payoff given their beliefs about the opponent's hidden information.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical machinery exists because private information changes what the decision problem is. If payoffs depend on a hidden type, you cannot solve the game by looking at one payoff table. You need types to name what is hidden, priors to say how likely each type is, and type-contingent strategies to specify what each type does. Without these objects, private values and costs would be invisible. The equilibrium calculation would be meaningless.

Why is this concept important?

Hidden information changes what a strategy has to do. A strategy is no longer just 'always bid 50'—it becomes 'if you know the item is worth 30 to you, bid 20; if it is worth 80, bid 60.' Each player has a private type (their secret value), and their strategy must tell them what to do depending on their type. Everyone else chooses their actions using probabilities over what those hidden types are.

What breaks without it?

If you model every game as if everyone knew everything, auctions would collapse. You would treat a bidder who knows the item is worth 50 to them the same as a bidder unsure whether it is worth 20 or 80—and the model would not make sense. Signaling (like a job applicant proving they are skilled), bargaining when both sides hide their reservation prices, and most real-world contracts would all disappear from the model. The course would be describing a world that does not exist.

Worked Mini-Example

In an auction, you know your own budget limit, but you do not know whether your rival can afford to bid high or will run out of money fast. Your bid must be set to make sense if the rival is the high-budget type (and bids aggressively) or the low-budget type (and bids cautiously). You bid something that does reasonably well no matter which one they are, given the odds of each.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students get stuck by thinking uncertainty is external—something that happens to the model. In a Bayesian game, uncertainty is internal and has structure. The opponent has a type. That type affects their payoff. You hold a prior—a probability over that type. Your strategy responds to your own type. Everyone is doing this at the same time. The model is not incomplete because of uncertainty; the uncertainty itself is part of what defines the model.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is where you define the type space. If types, priors, or payoff dependence are wrong, the Bayesian equilibrium will still be mathematically consistent—but it will not describe the real game. The lecture explains that the type space is a modeling choice. Too few types means you miss important variation in what players care about. Too many types means the model becomes flexible but loses meaning. The useful type structure is the one that captures the asymmetric information actually driving behavior—the gap between what one player knows and what others believe.

Common Misunderstanding

Students often treat hidden information as random static that floats outside the model. In truth, it is embedded in the game. The rival's type—low value or high value—is part of the strategic problem. It is not noise; it is why your strategy has to be type-contingent (meaning it depends on what type you are).

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you see a model or situation with hidden types, secret values, private costs, private information, asymmetric information, signals, or one player knowing something the others must guess at—that is Bayesian-game territory.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: hidden information must be put into the game, not waved at verbally. Name the types, priors, who observes what, and the strategy rule for each type. Otherwise uncertainty becomes an excuse rather than a model. The strategic object is a rule across possible private facts. Average-player reasoning is too coarse.

Connected Concepts

Bayesian games are the foundation for Bayesian Nash equilibrium (how rational players choose when information is hidden), auctions (bidders do not know each other's values), signaling (proving you are the type you claim to be), and perfect Bayesian equilibrium (beliefs and strategies must fit together across all information sets). They show up in the final third of the course because almost every real negotiation, job market, and competitive situation involves hidden information.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A bidder knows their own value but not the rival's value.

Walkthrough: The bidder represents the rival's value as a type drawn from a probability distribution. A bid is evaluated by averaging over those possible rival types. The strategy can depend on the bidder's own type because different values create different incentives.

Lesson: Bayesian games keep hidden information inside the strategic model.

Trap: Do not replace uncertainty with a single average opponent if the action should vary by type.

Model move: The modeling move is to index strategy by private information. A bidder's rule says what to do for each own value while averaging over rival values that remain hidden.

Reader action: Name each type, who observes it, and the prior probability over it before writing any expected payoff. Then make the strategy a rule for every type, not one action for an average player. Check whether the action itself reveals information that should change later beliefs.

Numbers / table check: Make rows for low value and high value. Add columns for who observes the type, prior probability, and prescribed action. If the strategy column has only one action for both rows, ask whether the model has accidentally erased private information.

Boundary check: The example is too simple if values are correlated, bidders learn from signals, or the distribution over types is assumed without evidence.

Equation Walkthroughs

Expected Utility and Bayes Rule are the two math moves behind Bayesian games. Expected utility lets a player choose while rival types are still hidden. Bayes rule explains how beliefs should move when signals or actions are observed. The important modeling step is to make strategies type-contingent: each private type needs its own action rule. Replacing hidden information with one average opponent usually erases the strategic problem.

Bayesian Games As Strategy With Hidden Facts

Start From Not Knowing Who You Face

A Bayesian game begins when a player must choose without knowing some fact that matters. A bidder may not know other bidders' values. A firm may not know a rival's cost. A negotiator may not know whether the other side is patient or desperate. The model calls these hidden facts types. A type is not a personality label. It is the private information that changes payoffs, choices, or beliefs.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Complete-information games pretend that everyone knows the relevant payoff facts. Many important settings do not look like that. If hidden information changes what a player wants, the strategy must say what each possible type would do. Other players then choose using beliefs about which type they may be facing. Bayesian games exist because uncertainty about people is not a footnote; it changes the action that is best.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to average people together too quickly. If half the sellers have high quality and half have low quality, the buyer does not face one average seller in a simple way. The buyer faces uncertainty, and the seller's action may itself give information. A Bayesian model keeps the hidden states separate long enough to ask how each type acts and how others reason from those possible actions.

Why It Matters Beyond Auctions

Bayesian games matter in insurance, hiring, medical testing, cybersecurity, diplomacy, and any setting where one side knows something the other side must infer. They also matter for topology-based existence work because beliefs and type-contingent strategies enlarge the space in which fixed-point reasoning may be applied. The math can prove a self-consistent plan exists only after the model has clearly named the hidden facts and the belief structure. If those hidden facts are chosen carelessly, the proof may be clean while the lesson is pointed at the wrong uncertainty.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Hiring

An employer does not observe worker fit directly. Schooling, references, interviews, and trial tasks become actions interpreted through beliefs about hidden ability.

Cybersecurity

A defender may not know the attacker's type, patience, or target. Defensive choices depend on beliefs about hidden motives and costs.
