Bayesian Games

A game where players may not know each other's type, value, or payoff information.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is strategic choice when players do not know exactly who they are facing or what payoffs the other side has.

Why does this problem exist?

Bayesian games exist because private information changes best responses. A player must choose against a distribution of possible types, not a single known opponent.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a probability distribution over states, types, or histories. The useful relation is `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).`. In plain language: theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version changes the game form by adding hidden types. A player may know their own value, cost, or payoff parameter while others only hold beliefs. Strategies must now specify actions for each type, because a high-value bidder and low-value bidder may rationally behave differently. The course uses this representation before solving equilibrium because the private information itself is part of the strategic environment.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is expected utility over types. A player chooses an action or plan while averaging over the possible hidden states they do not observe. Priors define the probability weights before actions reveal anything.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because hidden information changes the identity of the decision problem. Types and priors let the model represent what each player knows privately and what others believe. Without them, private values and costs would be invisible to the equilibrium calculation.

Why is this concept important?

Bayesian games matter because private information changes what strategy even means. A strategy must often specify what each possible type would do, while opponents choose using beliefs over those types.

What breaks without it?

Without Bayesian games, the course would treat hidden values, costs, and motives as if everyone knew them. Auctions, signaling, and incomplete-information bargaining would collapse into misleading complete-information models.

Worked Mini-Example

A bidder may not know whether rivals value the object highly or weakly. Their bid must respond to that uncertainty, not to one known payoff table.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by treating uncertainty as noise outside the model. In a Bayesian game, uncertainty is inside the model and has named objects: types, beliefs, strategies, and payoffs.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the type model. If the type space, priors, or payoff dependence are wrong, Bayesian equilibrium can be internally consistent but externally misleading. The lecture boundary also explains why the type space is a modeling choice. Too few types can erase important private information; too many can make the model flexible without adding insight. The useful type structure is the one that carries the strategic uncertainty actually driving behavior. The model’s usefulness depends on naming that hidden structure carefully.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to treat hidden information as random noise outside the game. In Bayesian games, the uncertainty is part of the strategic structure.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for models with types, priors, private values, costs, signals, or asymmetric information.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: hidden information must be put into the game, not waved at verbally. Name the types, priors, who observes what, and the strategy rule for each type. Otherwise uncertainty becomes an excuse rather than a model. The strategic object is a rule across possible private facts. Average-player reasoning is too coarse.

Connected Concepts

Bayesian games connect expected utility to Bayesian Nash, auctions, signaling, and PBE. They are the private-information foundation for the final third of the course.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A bidder knows their own value but not the rival's value.

Walkthrough: The bidder represents the rival's value as a type drawn from a probability distribution. A bid is evaluated by averaging over those possible rival types. The strategy can depend on the bidder's own type because different values create different incentives.

Lesson: Bayesian games keep hidden information inside the strategic model.

Trap: Do not replace uncertainty with a single average opponent if the action should vary by type.

Model move: The modeling move is to index strategy by private information. A bidder's rule says what to do for each own value while averaging over rival values that remain hidden.

Reader action: Name each type, who observes it, and the prior probability over it before writing any expected payoff. Then make the strategy a rule for every type, not one action for an average player. Check whether the action itself reveals information that should change later beliefs.

Numbers / table check: Make rows for low value and high value. Add columns for who observes the type, prior probability, and prescribed action. If the strategy column has only one action for both rows, ask whether the model has accidentally erased private information.

Boundary check: The example is too simple if values are correlated, bidders learn from signals, or the distribution over types is assumed without evidence.

Equation Walkthroughs

Expected Utility and Bayes Rule are the two math moves behind Bayesian games. Expected utility lets a player choose while rival types are still hidden. Bayes rule explains how beliefs should move when signals or actions are observed. The important modeling step is to make strategies type-contingent: each private type needs its own action rule. Replacing hidden information with one average opponent usually erases the strategic problem.
