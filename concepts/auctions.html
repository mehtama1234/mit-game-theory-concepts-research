Auctions

Rules for allocating an object when bidders have private values.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is allocating an object when people know their own values but the seller and rival bidders may not.

Why does this problem exist?

Auctions exist because rules can make private information compete in public. Bids are actions shaped by values, beliefs, payment rules, and competition.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Truthful behavior must give at least as much payoff as each profitable lie. The useful relation is `u_i(truth) >= u_i(report) for every alternative report.`. In plain language: The inequality says a person should not gain by replacing the truthful action with another report.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version treats an auction as a rule system that turns hidden values into public actions. A seller wants to allocate an object and collect payment, but bidders know more about their own willingness to pay than the seller does. The auction format tells bidders how bids map to allocation and payment. That mapping changes behavior. First-price rules make bidders care directly about the price they name; second-price rules separate the winning bid from the payment in a way that can make truthful bidding attractive under standard assumptions. The lecture evidence about bid deviations matters because auctions are evaluated by the deviations participants want to make, not by the designer desired story.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is expected payoff from bidding. A bid affects the probability of winning and the payment if winning occurs. The bidder chooses the bid that balances those two forces against beliefs about rival bids or values.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because allocation and payment cannot be analyzed separately. A high bid changes the probability of winning and may also change the amount paid. A bidder compares expected surplus, not just the thrill of winning. The model therefore needs values, bid spaces, allocation rules, payment rules, beliefs about competitors, and payoff comparisons. Without that combined object, the analyst cannot tell whether a bid reports value, shades value, or strategically distorts information.

Why is this concept important?

Auctions matter because they show how rules translate private values into strategic bids. The course uses them to move from predicting behavior under rules to designing rules that produce allocation, revenue, or information goals.

What breaks without it?

Without auction theory, the course would miss one of the clearest economic applications of incomplete-information games. It could not explain bid shading, truthful bidding in special formats, or why payment rules change behavior.

Worked Mini-Example

In a first-price auction, a bidder who values the object at 100 faces two forces. Bidding closer to 100 makes winning more likely, but if they win at 100 they keep no surplus. Bidding 70 leaves surplus if they win, but risks losing to someone they might have beaten. The bid is therefore not a direct confession of value. It is a strategic choice shaped by the payment rule and beliefs about rival bids. In a second-price auction the same value can create different incentives because the winner pays someone else bid.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by treating bids as direct value reports. In many auctions, bids are strategic actions distorted by payment rules, risk, competition, and beliefs about rivals.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the value and information environment. Private values, common values, risk aversion, budget limits, reserve prices, entry costs, collusion, and correlated signals can all change bidding logic. A revenue or truthfulness claim that is correct in one auction model may fail after one of those assumptions changes. The practical reading rule is: never interpret a bid until you know the payment rule and what bidders know.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to think the highest-value bidder automatically bids their value in every auction format.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for allocation and payment rules that transform private values into strategic bids.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: a bid has two effects at once: it changes the chance of winning and the surplus if winning. Read the payment rule before drawing conclusions, because first-price, second-price, and platform formats change incentives. A bid is an action under rules, not a value confession.

Connected Concepts

Auctions connect Bayesian games, expected utility, incentive compatibility, and revenue equivalence. Ad auctions extend the same logic when platforms allocate ranked positions rather than a single object.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A bidder in a first-price auction values the object at 100.

Walkthrough: Bidding 100 may win often but leaves no surplus when it wins. Bidding lower wins less often but leaves positive surplus when it does win. The bidder compares both the probability of winning and the payoff conditional on winning.

Lesson: Auction strategy is a tradeoff between chance of winning and surplus if winning.

Trap: Do not analyze the bid without the payment rule; first-price and second-price formats change incentives.

Model move: The modeling move is to split a bid into two effects: changing the chance of winning and changing the payoff conditional on winning. Auction format determines that tradeoff.

Reader action: For a small bid increase, write what happens to the chance of winning and what happens to surplus if winning. If you cannot name both effects, you are not yet analyzing the auction rule. Repeat the exercise after changing the payment rule to see why formats matter.

Numbers / table check: Compare bid 70 and bid 80 for value 100. If bid 70 wins with chance 0.4, surplus if winning is 30. If bid 80 wins with chance 0.6, surplus is 20. The bid comparison needs both probability and surplus columns.

Boundary check: The example is too clean if bidders are budget constrained, risk averse, asymmetric, colluding, or uncertain about whether the object has common value.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Auction Expected Payment card separates three pieces that are easy to blur: bid, chance of winning, and payment if winning. Expected Utility then adds the bidder's value from winning, while Bayes Rule explains how beliefs over rival values enter the chance of winning. A higher bid can help and hurt at the same time. Always read the payment rule before importing intuition from another auction format.
