Rationalizability

A strategy is defensible if there exists some reasonable belief about the other players that would make this strategy give you the highest possible payoff — the score or outcome you care about. Your move doesn't have to be what experts predict. It just has to be what some rational expectation could justify.

What real-world problem is this about?

Deciding which strategies make sense to consider without assuming everyone has reached one locked-in agreement about what will happen. You need a test that catches strategies people might reasonably choose before you check whether all their choices can fit together perfectly.

Why does this problem exist?

A strategy can be rational for someone who holds one particular belief about others, even when that strategy does not appear in the final prediction of what everyone will do. The course uses this idea because Nash equilibrium is only one possible anchor for what strategies to take seriously. You need a wider net first.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a probability assignment—a guess about how likely each possible situation or type is. The key formula is E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta). In plain words: 'a' is your action; 'theta' is the unknown situation; 'p(theta)' is your guess about how likely that situation is; 'u(a, theta)' is the score you get from action 'a' when situation 'theta' occurs. The expected value adds up: multiply each payoff by its probability, then sum. This tells you the average score you expect.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

Rationalizability sits between dominance and Nash equilibrium in the progression. Dominance asks: is there any state of the world where this action is worth taking? If not, delete it. Nash asks: is there one mutually consistent agreement where each player does what is optimal given the actual choices of others? Rationalizability occupies the middle: keep a strategy if it could be optimal under some belief about what the other players will choose. The lecture builds this by first recalling what a belief is—a probability guess. Then it shows that the belief is not decoration. It is the structural reason the strategy survives. But belief is not enough on its own; the strategy still must maximize expected payoff given that belief. Best response is still the test. The strategy has to be someone's optimal choice under that particular belief, not just a move a player might randomly try.

Mathematical Intuition

The intuition is that coherence of belief supports the strategy. Rather than checking whether all players' strategies and beliefs agree in one final picture, you ask whether a single strategy can be justified by at least one belief. That belief is a probability distribution over what others do. The strategy is rationalizable if there exists such a distribution where it maximizes expected payoff. Existence—not uniqueness, not everyone else agreeing—is what matters.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

Because plausibility depends on belief. A move can look odd from outside and still be rational for someone who expects opponents to behave differently. Normal language tends to lump surprising, wrong, irrational, and off-the-equilibrium-path into one bucket. Rationalizability separates them. The math must exist because the question—does there exist a probability distribution over surviving opponent strategies such that this action maximizes expected payoff—requires checking an infinite space of possible beliefs. Ordinary reasoning cannot track this precisely. The math asks for existence: there must be at least one coherent belief that supports the move. The belief does not have to be the analyst's best guess or shared by everyone. It just has to be coherent enough to make the action optimal.

Why is this concept important?

Because not every strategy that makes sense under some belief has to show up in Nash equilibrium. You often need to ask: could this move be optimal for someone who holds a different belief than the analyst does? Rationalizability gives you that question.

What breaks without it?

Without it, students jump straight from strategies that are never good to Nash equilibrium strategies—skipping the middle ground. They miss that a strategy can survive because one player could hold a coherent belief that makes it their best move, even if that belief turns out not to match everyone else's choices in the final prediction.

Worked Mini-Example

Your opponent might choose left or right. You are considering action X. Your first instinct is that X is bad because you expect opponent to go right. But the rationalizability question is not about your expectation. It is: could opponent be likely to go left? If opponent goes left and you pick X, do you get a higher payoff than your other options? If yes, then even though you personally would guess right, the belief 'opponent will go left' is coherent enough to make X optimal. So X survives rationalizability. The strategy doesn't have to match your favorite forecast—it just has to be somebody's best response under some defensible belief.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck because rationalizability feels permissive—like anything goes. It is permissive on purpose: it widens the net before you lock down predictions. But it is not empty. A strategy still has to be a best response to some belief. You cannot just claim a move is rational because someone somewhere might pick it. It has to be optimal under that belief, which means it must yield a higher expected payoff than the alternative moves.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is mutual consistency. Rationalizability can justify a strategy from a single player's point of view. It does not require all players' beliefs to be true at the same time. A rationalizable strategy may survive because one belief supports it, while the beliefs of the other players conflict with that belief or conflict with each other. Nash equilibrium, by contrast, requires each player's strategy to be a best response to the actual strategies in the proposed outcome, and all beliefs must be correct. That is why rationalizability is useful as a wider criterion but deliberately weaker than equilibrium as a solution concept.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to read rationalizable as synonymous with predicted or correct. It means defensible under some belief. Many rationalizable strategies will never occur because players' actual beliefs point elsewhere. Rationalizable is broader and intentionally weaker than the final prediction.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Watch for models that separate clearly impossible behavior—strategies that are never best responses to any belief—from behavior that is one of several plausible responses under different expectations. Any analysis that asks whether a strategy could be justified by some coherent belief is invoking rationalizability.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: ask whether an action can be made optimal by some coherent belief. If yes, it may be defensible even outside Nash equilibrium. If no belief can rescue it, the action is not merely surprising; it is strategically unjustified. The audit is belief existence, not final coordination.

Connected Concepts

Rationalizability keeps the best-response idea from Nash equilibrium but loosens the requirement that everyone must be right about each other's choices. It also foreshadows Bayesian thinking because beliefs—probabilities over types or situations—become the explicit object that justifies choice, not just invisible background intuition.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A move looks strange at first, but it might be a best response to some belief about the opponent.

Walkthrough: Instead of asking whether the move is part of a mutual equilibrium, ask whether the player could hold a belief that makes the move optimal. If such a belief exists and the belief itself is not ruled out by rationality, the move survives. If no coherent belief can justify it, the move is eliminated.

Lesson: Rationalizability separates unjustifiable choices from choices that merely look odd under your favorite prediction.

Trap: Do not confuse 'not Nash' with 'irrational.' A move can be rationalizable without being in the final equilibrium prediction.

Model move: The modeling move is to ask whether some coherent belief can make the action optimal, instead of demanding that all players' beliefs and actions already close into equilibrium.

Reader action: Try to build a belief that makes the action optimal. If no belief works, identify the exact payoff comparison blocking it; if one does work, check whether that belief relies on already-rejected opponent behavior.

Numbers / table check: Write two belief cases: rival plays left with probability 1, and rival plays right with probability 1. If the action is best in at least one coherent case, it may be rationalizable; if another action beats it in every case, it is not.

Boundary check: The example weakens if the belief used to justify the move depends on opponents playing strategies that have already been ruled out by the same rationality logic.

Rationalizability As Asking What Belief Could Make This Sensible

Start From A Move That Looks Odd

Rationalizability begins with a generous everyday question about people choosing under uncertainty. Suppose a player chooses an action that is not immediately ruled out. Could that action be the best response to some reasonable belief about what others will do? If yes, the action has a possible strategic explanation. It may not be the final prediction, and it may not be part of a Nash equilibrium, but it is not foolish inside every possible belief story.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Dominance removes actions that have no defense. Rationalizability studies the actions that survive a belief-based defense. The concept exists because a player's choice can only be judged after saying what they think others might do. A move that looks strange against one opponent action may be sensible against another expected action. Rationalizability keeps belief inside the analysis instead of pretending the analyst already knows the correct forecast.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to treat rationalizable as meaning likely, correct, or socially good. It means the action can be made optimal under some allowable belief. That is a weaker and broader idea than equilibrium. Another mistake is to let any wild belief justify anything. The course still disciplines beliefs through the deletion process and through the assumption that other players are also reasoning about best responses.

Why It Matters Beyond Early Solution Concepts

Rationalizability matters in legal reasoning, market interpretation, political analysis, and machine learning because observed behavior often has to be interpreted before the analyst knows the actor's beliefs. It also matters near topology because equilibrium can be seen as a stricter self-consistency point, while rationalizability marks a larger set of choices that survive belief-based reasoning. The distinction helps readers understand why fixed-point equilibrium is not the only way to talk about strategic plausibility.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Legal interpretation

An action that seems unreasonable may make sense under one party's belief about the other party. The belief story must still be disciplined.

Machine learning

When agents learn from each other, a move may be sensible under a mistaken but plausible belief. Rationalizability separates that from pure error.
