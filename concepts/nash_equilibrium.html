Nash Equilibrium

A profile of strategies where no player wants to change alone.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is making a prediction that does not immediately fall apart when each participant asks, given what the others are doing, should I switch?

Why does this problem exist?

Nash equilibrium exists because a strategic prediction has to be self-consistent. If one player wants to change after seeing the proposed profile, the profile was not a stable prediction.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Equilibrium requires each strategy to be a best response to the others. The useful relation is `a_i in BR_i(a_-i) for every player i.`. In plain language: For each player i, their chosen action a_i must belong to the set of best responses against the other players' actions.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version takes best response and closes the loop. A Nash equilibrium is a whole profile where every player’s action passes the best-response test against the others. It is a fixed point of strategic expectations: each person’s plan makes sense because the others’ plans are what they are. The course uses this to discipline prediction. A proposed outcome cannot merely sound plausible or efficient; it must survive each participant’s unilateral deviation check.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is mutual best response. For every player i, the chosen action a_i must sit inside BR_i(a_-i). The notation says no one can improve by changing only their own component while the rest of the profile is held still.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because strategic prediction is circular. Each player’s best action depends on the others’ actions, so the model needs a profile where all those conditional choices fit together. Nash equilibrium is the formal closure of the best-response loop.

Why is this concept important?

Nash matters because it gives the course a baseline standard for strategic stability. It does not promise that the outcome is good; it says the proposed behavior survives each player privately checking whether a one-person switch would pay.

What breaks without it?

Without Nash equilibrium, payoff tables would produce stories but not disciplined predictions. Any outcome could be proposed even if one participant has an obvious profitable deviation once everyone else behavior is named.

Worked Mini-Example

At a four-way stop, everyone going at once is not stable if each driver would rather wait or avoid collision given the others. A stable traffic convention must make each driver's move sensible given the convention.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by importing the everyday meaning of equilibrium as balance or goodness. Nash equilibrium can be inefficient or ugly. Its claim is narrower: no single player wants to move alone.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is one-person deviation from a fixed profile. Nash does not handle noncredible threats, belief updating, coalitional deviations, or welfare without extra structure.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to hear equilibrium and think socially best. Nash can describe bad outcomes, including prisoner's dilemma, because it is about unilateral incentives, not collective welfare.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for claims that a behavior pattern persists because no individual participant can gain by deviating alone.

Connected Concepts

Nash connects backward to best response and forward to mixed strategies, subgame perfection, Bayesian Nash, and PBE. Each refinement keeps the same no-profitable-deviation core while repairing a missing feature such as time or private information.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Two coffee shops each choose a price, and each shop's best choice depends on the other's price.

Walkthrough: Start with a proposed pair of prices. Ask whether shop A wants to change while shop B's price stays fixed. Then ask the same question for shop B. If neither shop wants a one-sided change, the pair passes the Nash test.

Lesson: Nash equilibrium is mutual best-response consistency, not a promise that the outcome is good for everyone.

Trap: Do not read equilibrium as fairness, efficiency, or happiness; it only means no profitable unilateral deviation.
