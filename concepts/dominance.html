Dominance

A test to cross off moves that are worse than some other move in every possible situation your opponent faces you with.

What real-world problem is this about?

In any game, some moves are just worse. They lose to another move no matter what the other person tries. But if you list all possible moves, the bad ones can hide the real choices that matter. Dominance lets you cross off the hopeless ones first.

Why does this problem exist?

You can sometimes reject a move without guessing what the other side will do. If one move always beats another move — no matter which choice the opponent makes — then the losing move is dead weight. You do not need to predict the opponent to trash it.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

The math compares two moves by looking at what each one gives you against every possible opponent move. Write it as: move A dominates move B if your score from A is always at least as high as your score from B, and sometimes strictly higher. The inequality holds for every opponent choice; that is what makes it dominance.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The full lecture shows a rare moment: you can throw out a move without any belief about the opponent. To test dominance, take two moves and compare them against every possible opponent response. If one move always scores at least as high as the other, and sometimes strictly higher, the inferior move is gone. The key phrase is 'against every possible opponent response' — that universal check is what makes dominance so strong but also so rare. Most moves are not dominated even though they might fail other tests later on.

Mathematical Intuition

The intuition behind the math is a single comparison that works everywhere. You line up two moves and check the payoff in each column (or row). If one move's payoff is always bigger or equal, dominance holds. Because the comparison does not change from one opponent choice to another, no belief can save the dominated move.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

Math exists here because some moves can be thrown out by proof, not by guess. Dominance formalizes 'no matter what the opponent does' as a list of inequalities. Each inequality is a check against one opponent move. If all the inequalities pass, you have a mathematical fact that the move is dominated, and no amount of belief-changing will fix it.

Why is this concept important?

This is one of the few places where clear thinking alone — without any belief about the opponent — tells you a move is bad. If you can prove a move loses in every case, you can eliminate it before you even think about best responses or equilibrium. This makes the problem smaller and cleaner.

What breaks without it?

If you keep every possible move alive, the board stays cluttered. Some moves have no reason to exist, but they still sit in the analysis like noise. Without dominance, you would not know which moves to ignore first, so finding the real strategic solution gets harder before you ever reach concepts like best response.

Worked Mini-Example

Suppose you are a shop owner and can price high or low. If pricing low always makes you more money than pricing high — whether your rival prices low or high — then high pricing is dominated. You cross it off without having to guess what the rival will do.

Where Students Get Stuck

A common trap is deleting a move because it performs badly when you guess the opponent will do something specific. That is not dominance. If the move is bad only against one opponent choice but good against others, it is not dominated — you just dislike it under your own prediction of what the opponent will do.

Where The Idea Stops Working

Dominance is powerful when it applies, but it does not cover everything. Many moves are alive because they are not dominated — even though they might fail a best response test or not appear in equilibrium. That is where other tools take over: beliefs about the opponent, best responses to those beliefs, and finally equilibrium where beliefs and moves reinforce each other.

Common Misunderstanding

People often think a move is dominated because it seems unlikely to work or because they do not like it. Real dominance means you compare across all opponent moves and prove it is always worse. A move that is bad under one opponent choice but useful under another is not dominated — that is a belief problem, not a dominance problem.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you see an argument that crosses off a move before computing equilibrium — because one move always loses to another — you are seeing dominance at work.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: a dominated action loses by comparison, not by vibe. Before deleting it, verify the inequality across every modeled opponent contingency. If the argument uses what the opponent is likely to do, it is belief-based rather than dominance-based.

Connected Concepts

Dominance is the start of a chain. After dominance, you move to iterated deletion (cross off dominated moves, then check again) and rationalizability (what moves could a thinking opponent play). Both use dominance as a building block. Dominance also explains why dominated strategies do not appear in equilibrium — no rational player would ever pick a move that is dominated.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A firm chooses high price or low price while a rival chooses aggressive or calm behavior.

Walkthrough: Compare high price and low price under the rival's aggressive move, then compare them under the rival's calm move. If low price gives at least as much payoff in both comparisons and more in one, high price is dominated. The firm does not need a forecast about the rival to remove high price.

Lesson: Dominance is powerful because it is a no-matter-what comparison.

Trap: Do not delete an action because it feels unlikely; delete it only when the inequality survives every modeled contingency.

Model move: The modeling move is to compare two actions while refusing to forecast the rival. Hold each rival action fixed in turn and check whether one option survives every column or contingency.

Reader action: Compare the candidate actions under each rival move separately. Put a mark beside any contingency where the supposedly dominated action ties or wins; one such mark is enough to block the strong dominance claim.

Numbers / table check: Use columns aggressive and calm. If low price gives 4 and 6 while high price gives 3 and 5, low price strictly dominates. If high price gives 7 in the calm column, dominance fails even if low price looked safer elsewhere.

Boundary check: The example becomes fragile with weak dominance, missing actions, or payoff ties, because deletion order and interpretation can start carrying the conclusion.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Dominance Inequality card is the exact test behind deleting a move. It says: compare two actions while holding each opponent contingency fixed, and do that for every contingency the model allows. The key word is every. A strategy that loses under one likely story is not dominated yet; it must fail across the full table. This is why dominance can prune before beliefs or equilibrium are solved.

Dominance As Removing A Move With No Good Defense

Start From Comparing Two Choices

Dominance starts with a direct comparison. Take one player and compare two possible actions while holding fixed each possible thing the others might do. If one action is never worse and sometimes better, the worse action has no good defense in the model. The player should not need a detailed prediction about others to reject it. The dominated move is bad across the relevant cases.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Before solving a whole game, the course often asks whether some choices can be ruled out immediately. Dominance gives a first cleanup tool. It does not require a full equilibrium story. It only asks whether one action loses to another action across the board. This matters because large games can contain options that distract the reader. Removing a dominated action can reveal the real strategic pressure that remains.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to say an action is dominated because it looks unattractive in one likely case. That is not enough. Dominance is a strong claim about all relevant opponent actions. Another mistake is to treat weak and strict dominance as the same. Weak comparisons can be delicate because deleting a weakly dominated action may change what remains in ways that depend on the order of deletion or on fine details of belief.

Why It Matters Beyond Classroom Tables

Dominance matters in contract choices, bidding, product strategy, voting rules, and policy design because it identifies options that no reasonable model participant should need. In computer science and operations work, similar pruning ideas remove impossible or inferior branches before a search continues. In topology-heavy equilibrium work, dominance does not replace fixed-point reasoning, but it can simplify the strategy space before asking whether a stable point exists.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Search and planning

A planner can discard choices that are never better than another available choice. That reduces clutter before deeper reasoning begins, while still forcing the planner to prove the comparison across all relevant cases.

Voting design

A voting rule can create dominated voting actions. Finding them shows where the rule asks people to act strategically against their sincere ranking, and where a simpler rule might remove needless manipulation.
