Bayesian Nash Equilibrium

A locked-in plan where each hidden type of player does the best it can, given what it expects the others to do. When you don't know someone's goal or strength, you make a type-specific move—and in equilibrium, no type wants to abandon its assigned move.

What real-world problem is this about?

Imagine you are bidding for something, but sellers do not know if you will pay high or low, and you do not know if other bidders are rich or poor. Each person's best move depends on what they guess about the hidden information around the table. The riddle: whose plan holds together when no one can see the full picture?

Why does this problem exist?

Bayesian Nash equilibrium must exist because the world is full of hidden stuff—you don't know someone's wealth, intent, or ability until they act. Each hidden type needs its own survival test: if I am this type, and I hold this belief about what others are like, would I still want to do my assigned move? Only when every type passes that test does a plan stick.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief is a guess about what other people are like, written as odds—the probability of each possibility. When you have a hidden type (your strength, your goal), your score is an average over all the possible types of others you might face. The sum is: score = sum over all other types, each weighted by its probability times what you get from that matchup. In short: expected score = add up (chance of this type) times (what you earn if you meet this type), for all types.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

Start with Nash equilibrium: a plan where no one wants to deviate alone. Now add hidden information: some players do not know other players' types. You need a new equilibrium concept that keeps the 'no one wants to deviate' idea but uses type-indexed plans and belief-based best responses. The steps: write down a strategy for each type of each player; write down a belief—odds for each player's type; check that each type, given its beliefs, has no profitable deviation in expected payoff; if all types pass, you have Bayesian Nash equilibrium. The leap from Nash to Bayesian Nash is: equilibrium is no longer about one move per player, but about consistency across all types' plans at once.

Mathematical Intuition

The intuition is a web of mutual best-response plans, threaded through types and beliefs. Hold fixed the strategy rules of all other players and the odds of their types. For your type, calculate the expected score if you follow your assigned move, and compare it to the expected score if you deviate. If your type is best-replying, and all other types are also best-replying (against the same beliefs), then the whole plan holds together. It is not just mutual stability of actions; it is mutual stability of type-contingent plans against a shared belief system.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The math exists because hidden types create hidden best-response conditions that simple reasoning can miss. A single action per player is not enough when players have types—each type needs its own strategy and its own best-response test. Bayesian Nash formalizes this: it writes down (1) a strategy function for each player (mapping that player's type to an action), (2) a belief distribution over types, and (3) a consistency condition: each type's action must be a best reply in expectation. Without the math, you can only guess whether a proposal works; with it, you can verify it rigorously.

Why is this concept important?

Bayesian Nash connects the core lesson of equilibrium—each person's move is stable because it is a best reply—to the messy real world, where no one has full information. It says: even when you do not know who you are talking to, your plan can still be stable if every possible type you could be wants to stick to its part of the plan.

What breaks without it?

Without a Bayesian Nash lens, you cannot tell if a type-based strategy actually works. You might write down: 'rich bidders bid high, poor bidders bid low,' but you'd have no way to check if rich bidders actually want to bid high once you do the math from their point of view. The strategy might fail for one hidden type even though it sounds sensible when you average everyone together.

Worked Mini-Example

In an auction, some bidders are willing to pay high (their true value is high) and others are willing to pay low (their true value is low). A Bayesian Nash equilibrium is a pair of bidding rules—one for high-value bidders, one for low-value bidders—such that each type is bidding optimally. If you are a high-value bidder, your rule should bid at the level that beats low-value bidders when they follow their rule, and you believe low-value bidders follow their rule. If you are low-value, your rule should bid to maximize your own score against the high-value bidders' rule. Both types pass the best-response test simultaneously.

Where Students Get Stuck

A trap: students check only the average player—'on average, does anybody want to deviate?'—and call it done. Bayesian Nash is stricter. A proposed plan can fail because one hidden type wants to deviate, even if the pooled behavior or the average player looks stable. You must check every type separately, even if they are rare. One type's defection can unravel the whole equilibrium.

Where The Idea Stops Working

Bayesian Nash handles one scenario: players have hidden types, but those types do not change during the game. It answers the question: 'What is stable when no one knows who they are playing with?' But real games often unfold over time, and early moves reveal information. If players act in sequence, later players can update their beliefs based on what earlier players did. That is where perfect Bayesian equilibrium takes over—it adds the requirement that beliefs must be updated rationally as the game proceeds. Bayesian Nash is the static foundation; perfect Bayesian equilibrium adds the dynamic layer where observed play shapes beliefs.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to think of a player as one unit—to ask 'does this player want to deviate?' Instead, Bayesian Nash requires that you ask it separately for each type. A strategy is not an action; it is a function: given my hidden type, I take this action. If some types deviate while others do not, it is not an equilibrium; every type must be willing to stick to its role.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for type-indexed strategies—rules that say 'if you are type X, do action Y'—and expected-payoff best-response conditions—statements that say 'this type, given its beliefs, maximizes its expected score by following this rule.' When you see a model where players are privately informed about something and you need to check if a proposed plan is stable, Bayesian Nash is the tool.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: equilibrium under hidden information is a rule checked type by type. Do not test only the average player. Hold the other type-contingent rules fixed and ask whether each type has a profitable expected-payoff deviation. One failing type breaks the whole proposed rule. The strategy is a function.

Connected Concepts

Bayesian Nash equilibrium bridges Nash equilibrium (the core concept of stability in a game) with expected utility (how to measure a score when things are uncertain), types (hidden information about who each player is), and beliefs (guesses about what others are like). Later, the course adds timing and history: if players move in sequence and learn from earlier moves, you need a refinement called perfect Bayesian equilibrium, which also updates beliefs as the game unfolds.

Worked Example Card

Setup: High-value and low-value bidders use different bidding rules in a first-price auction.

Walkthrough: A proposed equilibrium gives each type a bid. To test it, hold the distribution of rival bids fixed and ask whether each type wants to change its own bid. If no type wants to deviate, the strategy rule passes the Bayesian Nash test.

Lesson: Bayesian Nash equilibrium is mutual best response across hidden types.

Trap: Do not check only the average bidder; each type needs its own incentive check.

Model move: The modeling move is to test a whole strategy function, not one action. For each type, hold the rivals' type-contingent rules fixed and check whether that type wants to deviate.

Reader action: For each type, hold the other types' strategy rules fixed and check whether that type wants a different action. A single profitable deviation by one type breaks the proposed equilibrium rule. Do the check type by type so averaging does not hide a failing incentive.

Numbers / table check: Make rows for low-value and high-value bidders. In each row, compare assigned bid with one alternate bid using expected payoff. If the low type passes but the high type earns more by deviating, the Bayesian Nash candidate fails.

Boundary check: The example is incomplete if some type has a profitable deviation, if beliefs are misspecified, or if the type distribution changes with participation.

Bayesian Nash Equilibrium As Stability For Each Type

Start From Each Type Having A Plan

Bayesian Nash equilibrium extends the no-one-wants-to-move idea to games with hidden information. A strategy is no longer just one action for a player. It can be a plan for each possible type of that player. A high-value bidder may bid differently from a low-value bidder. A low-cost firm may choose differently from a high-cost firm. The equilibrium asks whether each type is best responding given beliefs about everyone else.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Nash equilibrium assumes the relevant payoff facts are known. Bayesian games do not. Bayesian Nash exists because every hidden type needs its own incentive check. The low type must not want to switch from its plan, and the high type must not want to switch from its plan, given beliefs and other players' type-contingent strategies. Stability must hold before private information is fully revealed.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to check only the player as a whole. In a Bayesian setting, each type is a different decision problem. Another mistake is to forget beliefs. A plan can look strange until the reader asks what the type thinks others are likely to be. Bayesian Nash equilibrium is not just Nash with extra vocabulary; it changes what a strategy is and where best-response checks happen.

Why It Matters Beyond Auctions

Bayesian Nash matters in auctions, insurance, hiring, procurement, security, and any setting where hidden facts shape action. It also connects to fixed-point reasoning because equilibrium may be proven over spaces of type-contingent strategies. The topology can show a self-consistent plan exists, but the plain question remains: is every type willing to follow its assigned plan under its beliefs? This makes the concept useful far beyond bidding: any hidden-information setting needs a plan that each possible type can defend.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Procurement

Low-cost and high-cost firms may bid differently. Bayesian Nash checks whether each cost type is willing to follow its bidding rule.

Hiring

Different worker types may choose different signals or applications. Employers respond using beliefs about which type they face.
