Finitely Repeated Games

Playing the same game over a fixed number of rounds, where everyone knows how many rounds there will be.

What real-world problem is this about?

Does playing something several times instead of once change how people act? Does knowing when it will end change the answer?

Why does this problem exist?

When you know the last round is coming, nobody can punish you after it happens. That creates a chain: if period 10 is the last, then in period 9, threatening to punish someone in period 10 sounds hollow—because period 10 happens the same way no matter what. This unraveling spreads backward through every earlier round, undoing the trust that would exist in an endless game.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Start from the very last period and work backward. In the last round, no future exists, so no threat of later punishment works. Predict what happens there first. Then move to the second-to-last period—you now know what the last period will bring, so you can figure out what people do here, given what they know is coming next. Keep stepping backward. At any point in time, a player picks the choice that leads to the best outcome they can achieve from that moment onward. This backward chain from a known endpoint is how you predict behavior in finitely repeated games.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

Start by drawing what happens in the last round—a single, standalone game. Show that in a one-time game, people often compete hard. Then ask: if you move to the second-to-last round, can you threaten future punishment to make people cooperate now? The answer is no, because future means the last round, which is already predictable. The threat sounds hollow. Step backward through the whole game. At each stage, the player sees the period ahead as locked in, so threats lose teeth. This is the backward-induction chain: it starts at the known end and propagates toward the present, demolishing the trust that would otherwise grow from repetition.

Mathematical Intuition

The endpoint is an anchor. The final period is fixed—it happens the same way whether you cooperate earlier or not. That outcome becomes the baseline for all earlier decisions. When deciding what to do in round 5 of a 30-round game, a player traces the logic forward: what happens in round 6? In round 7? If they can follow the chain all the way to round 30 and see it is locked in, then rounds 6 through 30 look predictable and unchangeable. That predictability destroys punishment threats—because punishment means "I will do something bad to you later," but later is already determined. The backward chain from a known endpoint converts what looks like future discipline into an empty promise.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

A known horizon changes incentives at every single period. The model must track how many rounds remain at each date so last-period logic can propagate backward. Finitely repeated games are not just one-shot games stacked in a list—they are a time-indexed strategic object where the endpoint shapes all earlier choices.

Why is this concept important?

Finitely repeated games teach a hard truth: repetition alone does not guarantee cooperation. A known endpoint unravels the incentives that would otherwise make cooperation work. Understanding this separates games where trust grows over time from games where a visible finish line destroys it.

What breaks without it?

Without studying finite repetition, people mistakenly think repetition always builds trust. The finite case forces the question: what if everyone knows when the relationship ends? That question reveals why the endpoint is strategically powerful.

Worked Mini-Example

Two firms know they will meet in the market for exactly 30 days. On day 30, breaking a promise and stealing a customer cannot be punished on day 31—there is no day 31. That makes trust harder on day 30. But if day 30 is predictable, why would a firm sacrifice profit on day 29 to punish someone, when they know day 30 will happen anyway? The problem spreads backward.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students count rounds and think more rounds mean more cooperation. They miss the question: at each date, does the relationship have a strategic future? A game ending at a known date is not the same as an ongoing relationship. The trap is confusing repetition with future consequences.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the known finish date. If the horizon is uncertain (players do not know when it ends), or if reputational effects exist (not just punishment this round but harm to your name), or if players cannot observe whether someone cheated, the unraveling logic breaks down. The finite case is a teaching tool: it blocks the lazy intuition that more repetition always builds cooperation. The real lesson is that what matters is whether each round still has a future that can create consequences for current behavior. That future pressure is what the lecture warns about.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is assuming more rounds automatically mean more cooperation. A known endpoint can undo that logic entirely.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for settings where an endpoint is known and trace the logic backward. Watch for situations where a final date might undo the trust that would otherwise exist in a relationship.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: a known final round can erase discipline before the final round arrives. Solve the last period first, then move backward. Repetition helps only when future rewards or punishments still have force at the moment of temptation. If cooperation survives, identify the extra post-game or reputation force. Otherwise the endpoint logic returns.

Connected Concepts

Finite repetition is the contrast case for infinite repetition. It shows why assumptions matter—if you remove the known endpoint or add uncertainty about when the game ends, cooperation becomes possible again. That switch makes the infinite-repetition material come alive.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Two firms compete on price for exactly ten days, and both firms know the relationship ends after day ten.

Walkthrough: On day ten, cheating cannot be punished on day eleven because there is no day eleven. Once day ten cooperation loses discipline, day nine threats become weaker too. The logic can unravel backward through the finite horizon.

Lesson: A known endpoint can destroy future punishment before the endpoint arrives.

Trap: Do not assume repetition automatically creates cooperation when the ending is common knowledge.

Model move: The modeling move is to start at the known final round and reason backward. If the last round has no future discipline, earlier threats can lose force by the same logic.

Reader action: Solve the last round first and write what future punishment is unavailable there. Then move to the next-to-last round and ask whether the solved final round can still discipline behavior. Continue backward until you find where cooperation loses support or why an added assumption stops the unraveling.

Numbers / table check: Create a ten-row timeline and start at row 10. If defection beats cooperation in row 10, mark row 10 solved. Then ask row 9 whether punishment in row 10 is still available. The table shows the unraveling instead of merely naming it.

Boundary check: The unraveling conclusion changes if the endpoint is uncertain, players care about reputation after the model ends, or punishment can occur outside the repeated game.

Equation Walkthroughs

Use Backward Induction to read finite repetition. The known final round has no later round to punish cheating, so its answer can remove discipline from the next-to-last round, and so on. The calculation walks backward because each earlier period depends on what future punishment remains. If the ending is uncertain, reputation continues outside the model, or punishment can happen elsewhere, the simple unraveling logic may no longer apply.

Finitely Repeated Games As The Last Round Pushing Backward

Start From A Relationship With A Known End

A finitely repeated game is a repeated relationship with a known last round. People meet more than once, but everyone knows when the interaction stops. That known ending changes the logic. In the last round, there is no future punishment left inside the model. If cooperation requires fear of later punishment, the last round may unravel first, and earlier rounds can be affected by that future endpoint.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Repeated play can support cooperation when the future matters, but a known final period weakens that force. The concept exists because the course needs to separate ongoing relationships from relationships with a fixed end. A worker near final review, a firm near the last contract, or a player near the final round may face different incentives. The end of the relationship can reach backward into present behavior.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to assume that repetition always helps cooperation. If everyone knows the final round and the stage game has a unique noncooperative equilibrium, backward reasoning can make cooperation unravel. Another mistake is to apply the stark unraveling result without checking the assumptions. Reputation, incomplete information, social norms, outside enforcement, or multiple equilibria can change the conclusion. The result is strongest when the endpoint is truly known, behavior is observed, and no outside relationship carries consequences beyond the modeled last round.

Why It Matters Beyond Repeated Prisoners Dilemmas

Finitely repeated games matter in employment contracts, supply relationships, political terms, school projects, and temporary alliances. They also clarify the boundary of fixed-point existence thinking. A stable plan in a repeated game must respect the known time horizon. The topology of the strategy space cannot make future punishment meaningful after the modeled future has ended. The lesson is practical: when a relationship has a visible end, the analyst should ask which incentives survive the end and which incentives vanish.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Term-limited politics

A politician facing a final term may have weaker future electoral discipline. The known endpoint changes present incentives.

Project teams

A group with a final deadline may cooperate early but slack near the end if no future response remains inside the relationship.
