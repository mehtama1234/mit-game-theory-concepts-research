Infinitely Repeated Games

A relationship where you don't know when it ends, so the threat of getting punished in the future can change what you do right now.

What real-world problem is this about?

When you and someone else interact over and over, each of you wants to cheat today to get an instant reward — but you're worried the other person will punish you tomorrow if you do.

Why does this problem exist?

Because there's no fixed last round, punishment can always happen next turn. That means the future isn't just something that will happen to you; it becomes a tool you can use right now to convince the other person not to cheat. When the last round is known, punishment is useless on that last turn, so it breaks down earlier and earlier — but with no known ending, that trap vanishes.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A discount factor delta (a number from 0 to 1) tells you how much today's score matters compared to tomorrow's score. The total score is: U = u_0 + delta*u_1 + delta^2*u_2 + ..., where u_t is the score in round t. If delta is close to 1, you care almost equally about today and tomorrow. If delta is close to 0, you only care about right now.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

In the lecture, we start with a one-shot game — say two firms choosing prices. Each firm would cheat (undercut the cartel price) if it knew there was only one round. But we erase the known last round. Now each firm considers: if I undercut today, the other firm punishes me for the next 100 rounds or forever. I gain a little today, but I lose a lot tomorrow (discounted by delta). For high enough delta, this loss is larger than the gain, so the firm does not undercut. We write this as an inequality: one-period gain from cheating ≤ discounted sum of losses from punishment. A trigger strategy is a continuation rule that says: 'I cooperate as long as you do; the moment you deviate, I punish forever.' This strategy is not assumed to be moral — it is checked to see if it is profitable to follow, given the payoffs.

Mathematical Intuition

Imagine your score is made of today's payoff plus tomorrow's payoff (shrunk by delta) plus the day after tomorrow's payoff (shrunk by delta squared), and so on. If delta is high, those future payoffs are almost as big as today's payoff, so they count in your decision. If delta is low, the future payoffs shrivel to nearly zero. A punishment strategy is a recipe for what happens if someone deviates — it changes the future payoffs. Cooperation is stable only when the payoff math says that staying in line is better than cheating today.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

In an ongoing relationship, today's choice has consequences for your future payoffs. To say 'the future matters' is true but vague. To make it precise, you need the discount factor (how much today's score compared to tomorrow's) and the continuation payoff (what you earn after today). These mathematical objects let you write down an inequality: gain from cheating ≤ discounted punishment loss. Without that math, 'the future will punish me' remains just a slogan, not a reason you can verify. The math turns the vague idea into a check: does this strategy actually work given these payoffs?

Why is this concept important?

This topic shows a real path to cooperation that does not rely on goodness or guilt. If the cost of being punished tomorrow is big enough (because delta is high), it becomes rational to cooperate today, even if you are purely selfish. The future stops being something that happens to you and becomes a reason to behave differently now.

What breaks without it?

Without infinite repetition, you'd only have the case where the last round is known — and in that round, punishment fails because it cannot happen afterward. You would lose the simplest model showing how ongoing relationships can make people behave decently toward each other. You would also miss why impatience (a low delta) is a real threat to cooperation.

Worked Mini-Example

Two oil companies both need to decide on a pump rate. If they pump slowly together, they each earn steady profits forever. If one pumps fast today, it makes more money right now but triggers a price war tomorrow. The fast-pumping company gains $10 million today but then loses $3 million per year forever after. With a discount factor of 0.9, the present value of losses is huge — bigger than the $10 million gain. So the company chooses not to pump fast.

Where Students Get Stuck

A student hears 'infinite repetition' and thinks the problem is solved — cooperation will happen automatically. But that is wrong. Infinite repetition is necessary, but not enough. The cooperation strategy must also satisfy the payoff inequality. If the punishment payoff is too small, if the discount factor is too low (the other player only cares about right now), or if the one-time cheat gain is very large, then cheating still pays. The student must check the inequality, not just invoke repetition.

Where The Idea Stops Working

Infinite repetition is not a automatic cure. It helps only if three things are true: the punishment payoff is real and known, the punishment can actually happen (both players see what happens each round and can respond), and the discount factor is high enough. If any of these fails, then even though the game repeats infinitely, cooperation may not hold. This is why the lecture separates mathematical infinity from economic reality. 'Infinite' in the math means no commonly known last round — it does not mean the world literally never ends. What matters is that punishment retains its power because players always face a future. If a player expects the future to lose value (they might leave the industry, the world might end, enforcement might break down), then the punishment threat weakens. The course also uses this to show why some cartels are stable (high delta, low detection cost, strong punishment) while others collapse (impatient firms, hard to detect, weak punishment).

Common Misunderstanding

Students often think 'infinite' means literally playing forever, which sounds impossible. The real meaning is that no one knows when the game ends, or it is a relationship that continues with value extending far into the future. It does not require the world to exist forever.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you see a real situation, look for these signs: trigger strategies (someone says 'I will do X as long as you do Y; if you cheat, I will do Z forever'), continuation values (someone mentions what a relationship is worth over time), and payoff comparisons (someone weighs today's gain against tomorrow's loss). These are the fingerprints of infinite-repetition logic.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: indefinite repetition supports cooperation only through an incentive inequality. Compare today's cheating gain with the discounted value of future punishment or lost cooperation. The phrase long relationship does no work unless monitoring and punishment are credible.

Connected Concepts

Infinite repetition uses the same ideas as finite-horizon games (payoff comparisons, strategy profiles), but removes the cliff where the last round's punishment becomes powerless. It connects to the folk theorem (which says many outcomes can be equilibria if repetition is long enough), to discounting (how we trade off today against tomorrow), and to subgame perfection (does a punishment strategy actually get carried out). It also explains how implicit cartels or reputation effects work in real business and politics.

Infinite Repetition As The Future Pushing Back

Start From A Relationship That Continues

An infinitely repeated game is not about literal immortality. It is about a relationship with no known final round inside the model. A firm expects to meet its rival again. A worker expects future reviews. A neighbor expects tomorrow to arrive after today. Because the relationship continues, today's gain from cheating may come with tomorrow's loss from punishment, distrust, or refusal to cooperate.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

One-shot reasoning can make cooperation look impossible because each person sees a short-term temptation. Repetition changes the calculation. If the future is valuable enough and actions are observed well enough, a player may give up a tempting gain today to protect future payoffs. The concept exists because many social and market settings are not one-time encounters. The same people, firms, or institutions keep meeting, and memory can discipline behavior.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to say repeated play automatically creates cooperation. It does not. The future must matter, the bad action must be noticed, and the punishment must be credible. If players are impatient, if monitoring is noisy, or if punishment hurts the punisher too much, cooperation can break. The repeated-game lesson is not optimism. It is a careful comparison between today's temptation and tomorrow's cost.

Why It Matters Beyond Firms

The idea matters in workplace trust, online reputation, community rules, supply chains, diplomacy, and climate agreements. It explains how present behavior can be shaped by expected future treatment. In biology, repeated interaction can help explain stable cooperation among organisms under the right conditions. In distributed systems, repeated contact and reputation can discourage harmful behavior when no single central enforcer controls every action.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Online marketplaces

A seller may avoid a short-term scam because bad ratings harm future sales. The future is part of the present payoff calculation, and that only works when buyers can observe enough history to punish bad behavior.

Climate agreements

Countries may cooperate when future trade, sanctions, monitoring, and reputation make cheating costly enough. The hard part is making those future responses believable, visible, and worth carrying out after the cheating has already happened.
