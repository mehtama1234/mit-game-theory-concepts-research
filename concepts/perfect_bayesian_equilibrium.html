Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium

A settled state in a game that unfolds over time, where players know some things but not others, and their beliefs—their best guesses about the hidden facts—stay consistent with what they actually observe as the game unfolds.

What real-world problem is this about?

When a game happens step by step and players have secret information: after you see someone's move, you must update your guess about what they know, then decide your own next move based on that updated guess. The puzzle is to find a state where everyone's guesses make sense with what they've seen, and everyone's actions are rational given their guesses.

Why does this problem exist?

A settled state needs two things working together: each player's later moves must make sense—they must be the best they can do given their updated guesses—and their guesses about secrets must match up with the pattern of moves they actually see. Without both, the state falls apart.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A guess about an unseen fact is a list of odds—like 'this type has a 30% chance, that type has 70%.' When you calculate the payoff from a move, you average the payoff across all the secret possibilities, weighted by those odds. In symbols: theta stands for the unseen type or state; p(theta) is its probability in your guess; the payoff you expect is E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta)—you add up u(a, theta) for each possible theta, each weighted by how likely you think it is.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

Build the picture: a player makes a move; others observe it; they update their guesses about what type the player is or what they know; they make their own move based on the updated guess; the first player anticipated all this and acted anyway. For this to be a settled state, two things must click: the initial move must have been optimal given what the first player expected the others to believe after seeing it; the update by the others must be correct—if Bayes' rule applies, it must be Bayes' rule. This coupling of forward-looking strategy and backward-learning belief is the core: beliefs shape optimal moves, and observed moves shape beliefs.

Mathematical Intuition

Picture a feedback loop. Beliefs drive the strategy (I choose X because I expect you to believe Y and respond by doing Z). Strategies drive the belief update (seeing X happen makes you update toward the types or facts that make X likely). Neither piece works without the other. You cannot say 'this strategy is optimal' until you have pinned down the beliefs, and you cannot say 'this belief update is valid' until you have checked that the strategy that produced the observed history is what rational play actually looks like.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The math exists because you need a language for both what players do and what they believe. Strategies (rules for action) and beliefs (probability assignments to unseen facts) have to be tied together in a way that ordinary game solving does not require. Bayes' rule is the glue: it says how beliefs update when you observe data. By writing out both pieces and connecting them with Bayes' rule, the math forces you to check whether a proposed state is actually consistent—whether moves make sense given beliefs, and beliefs make sense given moves.

Why is this concept important?

Games with hidden information and time both happening at once are everywhere: a worker interviewing for a job (the boss does not know if the worker is productive), one company bidding against another (nobody sees the other side's budget limit), a negotiator deciding whether to make a big offer (not knowing the other side's desperation). In all these, you need both a plan for each move and a running update of your guesses. This settled state tells you when both the plan and the guesses are correct at once.

What breaks without it?

Without this frame, a game model might say 'this player sends a signal' without explaining what beliefs make the signal worth sending, or why the other player would answer that way. The moves look like they work, but the hidden guesses that justify them are never checked. You end up with advice that sounds good but has no foundation—the beliefs and moves do not actually support each other.

Worked Mini-Example

A job applicant picks whether to get additional schooling. An employer sees the schooling choice and must guess the worker's actual ability. If the proposal says 'only high-ability workers get schooling,' then when the employer sees schooling, Bayes' rule says 'shift your belief toward high-ability.' The employer then makes a hiring offer. That offer shapes whether schooling was worth the cost. A settled state requires: the schooling choice is optimal for each type given the offer the employer will make, and the employer's belief after observing schooling is exactly what Bayes' rule says it should be.

Where Students Get Stuck

Many students solve the strategies first, then add beliefs as an explanation. But if off-path beliefs—guesses about what happens if the game goes somewhere unexpected—are left vague or arbitrary, the student misses that those beliefs control what happens if the game does reach that fork. A complete answer pins down both the strategies players follow and the beliefs they hold at every possible fork, including the ones they do not expect to reach.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The hard boundary sits at off-path beliefs. Suppose the equilibrium says 'type A never sends signal X.' Then if signal X occurs, Bayes' rule cannot be applied directly—there is no historical frequency to calculate from. The model still requires you to say what the receiver believes after seeing X, because if that unlikely event happens, the receiver's belief will shape their response. Different specifications of that off-path belief can lead to different equilibria, all of which look 'correct' on the main path the game is supposed to follow. The practical test: (1) Check that given the stated beliefs at each fork, the stated moves are optimal. (2) Check that wherever the game actually travels, beliefs are updated correctly by Bayes' rule. (3) Check that off-path beliefs (at unreached forks) are specified clearly, because if the game surprisingly goes there, those beliefs become load-bearing. If strategies look sensible given beliefs, but the beliefs are unsupported inventions, or if beliefs look plausible by Bayes' rule but the moves they imply are not optimal, the state is not settled.

Common Misunderstanding

The trap is to write down strategies and then sprinkle in beliefs as decoration. But beliefs are not optional commentary. In a game with hidden facts and time, a player's next move hinges on their current belief, so the belief is doing real work. If the belief is wrong or not specified, the whole claim falls apart.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for games that unfold over time and players have incomplete information—they do not know some fact about each other or the world. Watch for these signals: players observe some earlier move and must form a new belief about an unseen type or hidden state; later moves depend on those updated beliefs; the problem asks you to verify that both the strategy moves and the belief updates are correct. Common places: a sender can choose whether to signal their type (high quality picking expensive education, low quality picking cheap school); a buyer can make an opening offer but does not know the seller's reservation price (the minimum the seller will accept), so the offer revises the buyer's guess about that minimum; negotiators exchange offers over time, each one a signal about desperation or patience. If a model mentions beliefs alongside dynamic strategies, or asks you to check off-path beliefs, you are looking at this equilibrium concept.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: PBE requires strategies and beliefs to fit together. On-path beliefs must update by Bayes rule; continuation actions must be optimal under those beliefs. Off-path freedom should be labeled, because it can drive the result. Inspect inference and sequential choice as one object. Neither half is optional.

Connected Concepts

This concept ties together extensive form (games over time), Bayesian games (hidden types), signals, types, and subgame perfection (checking rationality at each fork). The deep connection is: subgame perfection checked rationality at each fork of a visible game; this does the same job for games where some facts are hidden. It is where belief-updating from data meets rational choice over time.

Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium As Beliefs And Actions Moving Together

Start From A Dynamic Game With Hidden Information

Perfect Bayesian equilibrium is used when a game has both time and private information. A player observes an action, but not always the hidden type that produced it. The player must form a belief, then choose a response. The sender or earlier mover must also want to act as planned given how later players will interpret the action. Beliefs and actions discipline each other.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Bayesian Nash handles hidden information in a more static way, and subgame perfection handles credibility in games with clearer information. Perfect Bayesian equilibrium exists because signaling and other dynamic information games need both checks at once. Players must act optimally after histories, and beliefs should be updated by Bayes' rule when the observed behavior makes that possible. A proposed story must explain both what people do and what they infer.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to state strategies without beliefs, or beliefs without checking whether actions are optimal under them. In a signaling game, a receiver's response depends on what the signal says about type. But the signal says something only if the sender types actually want to send different signals. PBE forces that loop to close. It does not let the analyst freely assign beliefs just to save a favorite outcome.

Why It Matters Beyond Signaling Models

Perfect Bayesian equilibrium matters in bargaining with incomplete information, reputation, education, warranties, regulation, and diplomacy. It also relates to fixed-point thinking because strategies and beliefs form a coupled object. The mathematical challenge is to find a self-consistent pair, but the everyday meaning is clear: after people observe behavior, their guesses and future actions must still make sense. This is why the concept is demanding: it does not let the story choose convenient beliefs after the fact.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Reputation

A firm may act tough early so others believe it is a tough type. PBE asks whether that action and belief are mutually supported.

Warranties

A warranty can change beliefs about product quality only if different seller types have different incentives to offer it.
