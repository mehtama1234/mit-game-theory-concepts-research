Implicit Cartels

Firms keep prices high without a signed agreement because they are scared of what rivals will do to them later. If I cheat today by cutting my price, you will punish me tomorrow by cutting yours even more, which hurts both of us. So I stay quiet and we both keep prices up.

What real-world problem is this about?

Gas stations on the same street sometimes sell at nearly the same price, even though nobody wrote down 'don't cut price.' How does that happen? Do they need a secret phone call, or can each one figure out independently: if I cut, they will hurt me back?

Why does this problem exist?

Implicit cartels happen because firms meet each other over and over in the market. When you know you will see me again tomorrow, you care more about how I treat you today. If I undercut your price today to steal your customers, I know you will cut prices even lower tomorrow to get even. So I think: it is not worth it. The threat of tomorrow's retaliation changes what I do today, even without a call or a contract.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

There is a number, written delta (δ), that says how much you care about tomorrow compared to today. If delta is close to 1, tomorrow feels almost as important as today. If delta is close to 0, tomorrow feels worthless and today is all that matters. The profit you get over many days looks like: payoff today + (delta × payoff tomorrow) + (delta² × payoff the next day) + and so on. This formula is what lets us write down when cheating is worth it or not worth it.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The full lecture starts with the folk theorem: in infinite repeated games, many outcomes are possible if firms can monitor each other and punish cheating. Implicit cartels are one outcome where high prices stick. A firm considers: I could cheat and make extra today, but if rivals see it and retaliate with lower prices for years afterward, the future damage outweighs today's gain. This is collusion that is not explicit because the market structure itself — repeated play, observable prices, credible punishment — creates the incentive. The lecture also points out why cooperation among firms can be socially bad. In prisoner's dilemma, cooperation is good. In the cartel, cooperation among firms means high prices for customers, which is why antitrust law exists.

Mathematical Intuition

The intuition is simple: compare two numbers: (1) the profit from staying quiet and high, spread across the future, and (2) the profit from cheating once, then facing punishment forever. If number 1 is bigger, stay quiet. If number 2 is bigger, cheat. That comparison is really just a version of 'how much do you care about the future?' If you care a lot (delta high), the future pain from punishment is huge and cheating looks bad. If you don't care (delta low), today's gain dominates and cheating looks good.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The math has to exist because tacit collusion is a puzzle: how can rival firms coordinate without talking? The math solves it by representing a comparison across time. The model writes down: today's gain from cheating, future losses from punishment, and the discount factor that trades today against tomorrow. Without a formula, the story is vague: 'firms are scared of each other.' With a formula, you can check: given these numbers (costs, prices, delta), does the cartel hold or break?

Why is this concept important?

Implicit cartels show that firms don't need to break the law with secret meetings to keep prices high. They just need to worry about the future. This is important to antitrust (the government people who police unfair pricing), because they have to figure out: are high prices a sign of a conspiracy, or just smart firms playing it safe with the fear of punishment?

What breaks without it?

Without this idea, the course would make repeated games feel like abstract math with no real application. Adding implicit cartels shows that discounting (caring about the future), monitoring (seeing what others do), and punishment (responding to cheating) actually shape how real firms set prices. It ties abstract game theory back to actual markets.

Worked Mini-Example

Two nearby gas stations both charge $3 a gallon. Station A could cut to $2.90 and steal all customers today, making more money right now. But station B sees the cut and knows what to do: cut to $2.80, then $2.70, starting a price war where both make almost nothing. Station A knows this will happen, so it thinks: making extra today is not worth the price war tomorrow. Both stay quiet at $3, even without talking about it.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often think: 'Collusion needs a phone call or a meeting.' But the model shows: collusion can be silent. Each firm, acting alone, figures out that quiet cooperation is its best move, given that it expects the others to do the same. No handshake or signature is needed if prices, quantities, or market shares are public enough that firms can see cheating and respond.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The model explains incentives, not proof. It shows when tacit collusion is theoretically possible. In the real world, you need more: Can firms actually see each other's prices? Is there too much noise from demand shocks or new entry? Do antitrust laws make punishment impossible? A real case of cartel behavior needs evidence about communication, prices over time, and market structure, not just the model.

Common Misunderstanding

Many assume that high prices without a contract mean there is no collusion happening. But game theory says: quiet collusion is possible when the future is scary enough. The mistake is not seeing that strategic reasoning alone can produce price-fixing behavior, no meeting needed.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for industries where prices are public and stable, firms meet the same rivals over years, cost shocks are small (so price cuts are obviously cheating), and entry or exit is rare. Those are places where tacit collusion has room to work.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: high prices can come from tacit repeated-game discipline, but the model is not legal proof of collusion. Identify cheating gains, monitoring quality, and punishment incentives before saying a cartel-like outcome is self-enforcing. Demand noise and entry can break the punishment story.

Connected Concepts

Implicit cartels tie together infinite repetition (going on forever), trigger strategies (punish forever if cheated once), incentive compatibility (making sure the choice to stay quiet is worth it), and industrial organization (how real markets work). They also show that cooperation between firms is not always good for society — when firms cooperate to raise prices, customers get hurt.

Implicit Cartels As Cooperation Without A Spoken Deal

Start From Firms That Keep Meeting

An implicit cartel is a situation where firms act as if they are restraining competition even without an explicit agreement. The everyday picture is repeated rivalry. Each firm may want to cut price today and steal customers, but it also knows rivals can respond tomorrow. High prices can survive when the future loss from retaliation outweighs the short-term gain from undercutting.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Competition policy cannot look only for written contracts or secret meetings. Firms may coordinate through repeated market behavior, public price changes, or mutual understanding of punishment. The concept exists because repeated-game incentives can make collusion self-enforcing without a formal cartel. The model asks whether each firm would rather keep the high-price pattern than cheat and face future punishment.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to treat every high price as proof of an implicit cartel. High prices can also come from costs, product quality, capacity limits, search frictions, or lawful differentiation. Another mistake is to ignore credibility. If firms cannot observe cheating or cannot punish profitably, the cartel-like outcome may not be stable. The repeated-game story needs evidence, not only suspicion.

Why It Matters Beyond Antitrust

Implicit cartel reasoning matters in platform markets, procurement, labor markets, and any repeated setting where rivals watch one another. It also shows why topology or fixed-point existence is not enough by itself. A stable collusive pattern may exist mathematically, but policy needs to know whether monitoring, punishment, and market institutions make that pattern realistic and harmful. The plain-language question is whether future rivalry supplies enough discipline to replace an explicit contract. This makes the concept useful but also dangerous: it can explain quiet coordination, yet it must be tied to market facts before accusing firms of collusion.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Antitrust review

Regulators must separate lawful parallel behavior from repeated-game coordination. Evidence about monitoring and punishment is central. The review should ask who observes price cuts, how quickly rivals respond, and whether punishment would be profitable.

Procurement markets

Repeated bidders may learn to rotate wins or avoid aggressive bids. The pattern can persist without a written agreement.
