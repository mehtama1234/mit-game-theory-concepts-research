Implicit Cartels

Firms may keep prices high without an explicit agreement when future punishment disciplines cheating.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is how firms might keep prices high without signing an explicit cartel agreement.

Why does this problem exist?

Implicit cartels exist because repeated market interaction can make cheating costly. A firm may avoid undercutting today because rivals can punish tomorrow.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A discount factor delta weights future payoffs in an intertemporal sum. The useful relation is `U = u_0 + delta u_1 + delta^2 u_2 + ...`. In plain language: u_t is payoff in period t; delta between 0 and 1 controls how strongly the future matters.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version applies repeated-game discipline to firms. A firm may want to cut price today, but if rivals respond with future low prices, the deviation can be unprofitable. The cartel is implicit when no explicit contract is needed; the strategic structure itself supplies discipline through monitoring and punishment. This also shows why cooperation among firms can be socially harmful even when cooperation sounded good in prisoner’s dilemma examples.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is the same deviation inequality from repeated games, translated into profit. The gain from undercutting today is compared with the discounted loss from future price competition.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because tacit collusion depends on a comparison across time. The model must represent the current profit from cheating and the discounted loss from future punishment. That object turns cartel stability into an incentive constraint instead of a story about firms being friendly.

Why is this concept important?

Implicit cartels matter because they show repeated-game theory inside markets. Firms may sustain high prices without an explicit contract when future punishment makes undercutting unattractive.

What breaks without it?

Without the cartel application, repeated-game ideas could feel abstract. The course would miss how continuation values, monitoring, and punishment shape real pricing incentives and antitrust concerns.

Worked Mini-Example

A gas station can cut price today and steal customers, but nearby stations may respond with a price war. Tacit collusion asks when fear of that future makes quiet cooperation stable.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by equating collusion with communication. The model can produce tacit collusion if prices, market shares, or other signals let firms detect and punish deviations.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is monitoring and legality. Tacit collusion requires that firms can detect deviations and respond; market noise, entry, and antitrust constraints can break the mechanism. In lecture terms, the important boundary is that the model is explaining an incentive mechanism, not proving that firms actually coordinated in any given market. Evidence about prices, demand shocks, communication, and monitoring would be needed before applying the concept outside the classroom example. The concept also depends on market observability. If firms cannot tell whether a low price came from cheating, demand noise, cost shocks, or a new entrant, punishment may be too blunt or too costly to sustain the cartel path.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to assume high prices prove explicit agreement. Repeated-game incentives can sometimes generate similar outcomes without a formal contract.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for industries with repeated price interaction, observability of deviations, and credible punishment paths.

Connected Concepts

Implicit cartels connect infinite repetition, folk theorem logic, incentive compatibility, and market design. They also show where strategic cooperation can be socially harmful rather than beneficial.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Nearby gas stations keep prices high without signing a contract.

Walkthrough: Each station could cut price today and steal customers. But if rivals respond with a price war, the future loss may outweigh the current gain. The cartel-like outcome survives only if monitoring and punishment make undercutting unattractive.

Lesson: Tacit collusion is repeated-game discipline applied to market competition.

Trap: Do not assume high prices prove an explicit agreement; the model studies when incentives can coordinate behavior without one.
