Backward Induction

Solving a sequential game by starting at the end.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that early choices in a tree depend on what later players will actually do, not what they announce they might do.

Why does this problem exist?

Backward induction exists because the future constrains the present. If we can solve the last decision first, that answer becomes an input into the previous decision.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Backward induction assigns optimal choices from terminal decision nodes back to the start. The useful relation is `V(node) = max_action V(next node).`. In plain language: V is continuation value; at each decision point the player chooses the next branch with the best continuation value.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version begins at the end because the end is where future speculation stops. At a final decision node, the moving player simply chooses the best available branch. Once that branch is known, the earlier node can treat the solved continuation as a value. Repeating that step back to the root gives a path through the tree. The method teaches that an early action is rational only after the later consequences it anticipates are themselves rational.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is recursion over continuation values. A node’s value is determined by the best value available at the next nodes for the player moving there. The game is solved by replacing future subtrees with their strategic value.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because sequential choice has nested future problems inside current decisions. A continuation value lets the solved future replace a whole subtree. Without that object, the analyst would have to carry every possible future story instead of using the later incentive calculation.

Why is this concept important?

Backward induction matters because sequential rationality starts at the end. The course uses it to show that earlier decisions already contain beliefs about later incentives, so solving forward without checking the end can validate empty threats.

What breaks without it?

Without backward induction, a game tree would be a picture of possible histories but not a method for evaluating them. The model could accept plans that later players would abandon once their decision node is reached.

Worked Mini-Example

If a parent threatens a punishment they will not want to impose later, a child may ignore it. Backward induction exposes that by checking the later choice first.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by reading the tree chronologically. Players live forward, but the analyst solves backward because later incentives determine which earlier expectations are credible.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the tree. Backward induction is only as good as the modeled histories, payoffs, and information. Hidden information or imperfect observation requires richer tools. The boundary becomes visible when real players have limited foresight or when the tree is not common knowledge. The course model assumes the relevant future choices are understood well enough to be folded backward.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to reason from the start while treating later threats as fixed facts.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for finite sequential games where the last move can be solved and folded back into earlier choices.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: solve the future choice where a threat or promise would actually be carried out. If the future player would not choose it there, the earlier statement should not shape present behavior unless commitment or reputation changes the payoff.

Connected Concepts

Backward induction connects extensive form to subgame perfection, bargaining, finite repetition, and one-shot deviation. It is the recursion primitive behind the course treatment of time.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A parent threatens a punishment they will not want to impose later.

Walkthrough: Start at the later moment when the punishment would be chosen. If imposing it is costly and no longer useful, the parent will not choose it there. Knowing that, the earlier threat loses force.

Lesson: Backward induction tests future credibility before trusting present threats.

Trap: Do not solve the first move first just because it appears first in the story.

Model move: The modeling move is to let future incentives discipline present claims. Solve the last real choice first, replace it with its continuation value, and pull that value backward through earlier decisions.

Reader action: Start at terminal choices and replace each solved future node with its value before moving earlier. When an earlier threat depends on a later action, test the later action first. Write the replacement value on the tree so the earlier decision is solved against the real continuation.

Numbers / table check: At the final node, suppose punish gives 1 and forgive gives 3. Replace the whole final decision with forgive and value 3. Then solve the earlier move using 3, not the announced punishment branch.

Boundary check: The example breaks if later players can renegotiate, have hidden information, care about outside reputation, or face costs not represented in the tree's continuation payoffs.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Backward Induction card is the procedure behind this concept. Start at the last real choices, solve what the moving player will want there, replace those future nodes with their values, and move one step earlier. The math matters because early threats and promises inherit their force from later incentives. Backward Induction As Solving The Future First

Start From The Last Real Choice

Backward induction is the habit of walking to the end of a game tree before judging the beginning. If a later player will eventually choose between accepting and rejecting an offer, start there. Ask what that player will want at that later point. Then carry that answer one step earlier. The early player should not plan around a later threat or promise unless that later action will still make sense when the time comes.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Time changes strategy because words said early can become empty later. A person may threaten to punish, refuse, walk away, or fight, but the model has to ask whether the person will actually want to do that after the earlier choices have already happened. Backward induction exists to remove plans that depend on later actions no one would carry out. It replaces announced resolve with a step-by-step check of later incentives.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to take the first statement in a sequential game at face value. In everyday life, people often make threats that sound strong because they are spoken early. In the model, early speech does not settle credibility. A later decision maker will face a new choice with the past already fixed. Backward induction asks what that person wants then, not what sounded useful before.

Why It Matters Beyond Bargaining

Backward induction matters in negotiations, entry deterrence, litigation, war threats, hiring processes, and product launches. Any time a choice today is meant to shape a later response, the later response needs its own incentive check. The same idea appears in computer science when an algorithm solves a finite decision tree from the leaves back to the root. It appears in law and policy when a rule must predict how people will respond after enforcement becomes costly.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Law and settlement

A lawsuit threat affects settlement only if trial would still be worth pursuing later. The later cost of carrying out the threat shapes the earlier bargain.

Computer science

Finite decision trees can be solved from the end back to the start. The method is the same plain idea: later values guide earlier choices.
