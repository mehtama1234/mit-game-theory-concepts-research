Backward Induction

Read the deep dive — seen on a real run →

A method for solving games where players move one after another: you start at the very last decision and work your way backward to the start, solving what each player will choose at each step.

What real-world problem is this about?

When one person moves today and another person moves tomorrow, today's person is trying to guess what tomorrow's person will do—but people often say they'll do something harsh later, then don't do it when the moment comes. You need a way to figure out which future threats are real and which are bluffing.

Why does this problem exist?

The reason backward induction exists is that the future always changes what makes sense in the present. If you can solve the last step first—figure out what someone will definitely choose when they get there—then you know exactly what to expect, and that becomes your roadmap for what to do one step earlier. You solve future pieces, then use those solved pieces to solve the present.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Backward induction works like this: at the very end, each player picks the choice that gives them the best score. You write down that score. Then you step back one choice: the player moving there now knows what happened at the end, so they pick the move that leads to the best score they saw written down. Keep stepping backward, and at each step the player picks the move leading to the best path they learned about. In symbols: the value V of a decision is the highest value available one step ahead. At each point, pick max_action V(next node).

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version of backward induction works as follows. Start at the last decision node—the place in the tree where one player moves and then the game ends. That player simply looks at the payoffs of their available branches and picks the highest one. Write that payoff on the node. Now move back one step to an earlier decision node. The player moving there sees several branches leading ahead. Some branches lead directly to payoffs (the ends of the game). Other branches lead to the node you just solved, which now has a payoff written on it. The player picks the branch leading to the highest payoff, whether that payoff is immediate or one of the solved values you wrote down. Write that payoff on this node too. Continue backward through the tree: at each node, the player moving there picks the branch leading to the highest payoff (including all the solved values from later nodes), and you write that payoff on the node. When you reach the start of the tree, you are done. The path of choices that leads from each player picking their best branch at each node is the backward-induction solution. It shows what will actually happen.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is that a game tree contains nested puzzles: the last decision is a simple puzzle (pick the highest score). The earlier decision is a harder puzzle, but once you have solved the last one, you can plug its answer into the earlier puzzle and make it simpler too. Backward induction solves by recursion: each node's value equals the best value the player at that node can reach at the next layer. You replace future subtrees (all the complicated paths ahead) with single numbers (the best scores from those subtrees), so the player at an earlier node does not need to think about distant futures—just about one step ahead and the scores you have already calculated.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because sequential choice creates a problem within a problem within a problem: the very last decision stands alone, but the second-to-last decision contains the last decision inside it, and the first decision contains all the rest. Without a notation for solved subtrees, you would have to carry every possible future story forward in your mind. A continuation value—a single number representing the best payoff from here onward—lets you replace an entire future subtree with one number. Without this object, the analyst would be stuck: you would have to write out every possible branch and every possible future to check whether a plan was credible. With continuation values, you just calculate once and reuse the answer.

Why is this concept important?

Backward induction is the method that turns the idea of sequential rationality into something you can actually calculate. In a strategic situation where people move one at a time, earlier choices only make sense if the later choices they depend on are actually rational—not just hoped for. This course uses backward induction to show that when you write down a threat (I will punish you later), the threat is only believable if the punished player's rational later self would actually carry it out. That is how you catch bluffs.

What breaks without it?

Without backward induction, a game tree—a picture showing every possible history and every choice that could be made—is just a picture. You can draw all the paths, but you have no way to tell which ones will actually happen and which are empty stories. You could watch a player write down a plan (I will do A now and then B later) and have no method to check whether that plan makes sense when the later moment arrives. You would accept threats that nobody would carry out.

Worked Mini-Example

A parent and child: it is day 1 and the child decides whether to be misbehave or obey. If misbehave, it moves to day 2 where the parent decides whether to punish or ignore. If the child obeys on day 1, the game ends and the child is happy. Backward induction: start at day 2. The parent sees that punishment hurts the parent (−5 points) while ignoring costs nothing (0 points). So the parent will ignore. Now the child on day 1 knows: if I misbehave, the parent will ignore it and I get +10. If I obey, I get +5. So the child misbehaves. The parent's threat to punish is not credible because the parent would not carry it out when the time came, and the child understood this.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by trying to read the tree the way a player would actually experience it—forward in time. The player is born at the start and encounters choices one by one, so it feels natural to solve from the start forward. But the analyst solving the game cannot do that because the analyst would have to guess what the future players will do. Backward induction flips the order: you solve from the end backward because the end is the only place where there is no ambiguity. Later incentives determine which earlier expectations are credible. Once you know what someone will definitely do at the end, you can work backward with certainty.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary of backward induction is the tree itself: the set of possible histories, the information available at each node, and the payoffs at the end. Backward induction only works if the tree is correct. If hidden information exists—if players do not know all the past moves or do not know each other's goals—the tree must be richer, and backward induction alone is not enough. Imperfect observation means a player cannot tell which node they are at, so they cannot use different choices at different nodes that look the same to them. The method is powerful but scoped: it solves games where players see all past moves and know all the payoffs and decision points. The course assumes the relevant future choices are understood clearly enough to be folded backward. When real people have limited foresight or doubt about the future, the assumptions break and the model must expand.

Common Misunderstanding

The common mistake is to read the tree as though you are the player moving first, imagining the future as a story you want to believe. Students think: I move now, and I hope the other player carries out their threat because that threat keeps me in line. But backward induction forces a different question: will the other player actually do it when they face that choice? If the answer is no, your hope is irrelevant. The mistake is reasoning forward and treating later threats as if they were locked in.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for finite sequential games—situations where people move one at a time for a known number of steps and then the game ends. If you can identify the very last move and solve it, and if the player moving earlier can predict the later move, backward induction applies. Any situation where you need to check whether a threat is credible by asking will the person actually do it when they face that choice is a place where backward induction helps you think.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: solve the future choice where a threat or promise would actually be carried out. If the future player would not choose it there, the earlier statement should not shape present behavior unless commitment or reputation changes the payoff.

Connected Concepts

Backward induction connects directly to subgame perfection—a principle saying that every threat and promise must be rational at every point in the tree. It also connects to the course treatment of bargaining (how two people split a pie through offers and counteroffers, each delayed), finite repetition (playing the same game multiple times in a row), and one-shot deviations (checking whether changing just one choice would ever help). All of these use backward reasoning to determine what is credible. The method is the recursion primitive running through the course: if you can solve the future, the present becomes a simple optimization.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A parent threatens a punishment they will not want to impose later.

Walkthrough: Start at the later moment when the punishment would be chosen. If imposing it is costly and no longer useful, the parent will not choose it there. Knowing that, the earlier threat loses force.

Lesson: Backward induction tests future credibility before trusting present threats.

Trap: Do not solve the first move first just because it appears first in the story.

Model move: The modeling move is to let future incentives discipline present claims. Solve the last real choice first, replace it with its continuation value, and pull that value backward through earlier decisions.

Reader action: Start at terminal choices and replace each solved future node with its value before moving earlier. When an earlier threat depends on a later action, test the later action first. Write the replacement value on the tree so the earlier decision is solved against the real continuation.

Numbers / table check: At the final node, suppose punish gives 1 and forgive gives 3. Replace the whole final decision with forgive and value 3. Then solve the earlier move using 3, not the announced punishment branch.

Boundary check: The example breaks if later players can renegotiate, have hidden information, care about outside reputation, or face costs not represented in the tree's continuation payoffs.

Backward Induction As Solving The Future First

Start From The Last Real Choice

Backward induction is the habit of walking to the end of a game tree before judging the beginning. If a later player will eventually choose between accepting and rejecting an offer, start there. Ask what that player will want at that later point. Then carry that answer one step earlier. The early player should not plan around a later threat or promise unless that later action will still make sense when the time comes.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Time changes strategy because words said early can become empty later. A person may threaten to punish, refuse, walk away, or fight, but the model has to ask whether the person will actually want to do that after the earlier choices have already happened. Backward induction exists to remove plans that depend on later actions no one would carry out. It replaces announced resolve with a step-by-step check of later incentives.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to take the first statement in a sequential game at face value. In everyday life, people often make threats that sound strong because they are spoken early. In the model, early speech does not settle credibility. A later decision maker will face a new choice with the past already fixed. Backward induction asks what that person wants then, not what sounded useful before.

Why It Matters Beyond Bargaining

Backward induction matters in negotiations, entry deterrence, litigation, war threats, hiring processes, and product launches. Any time a choice today is meant to shape a later response, the later response needs its own incentive check. The same idea appears in computer science when an algorithm solves a finite decision tree from the leaves back to the root. It appears in law and policy when a rule must predict how people will respond after enforcement becomes costly.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Law and settlement

A lawsuit threat affects settlement only if trial would still be worth pursuing later. The later cost of carrying out the threat shapes the earlier bargain.

Computer science

Finite decision trees can be solved from the end back to the start. The method is the same plain idea: later values guide earlier choices.
