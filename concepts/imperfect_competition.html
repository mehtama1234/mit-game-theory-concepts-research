Imperfect Competition

A market setting where a firm's own price or quantity choice can move its payoff because rivals and buyers react.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that many real markets sit between a street market with countless sellers and a single monopolist. A firm may be large enough that its price or quantity choice moves the market, but it still has rivals whose choices push back.

Why does this problem exist?

Imperfect competition exists because scarcity, location, patents, brands, search costs, and product differences can give firms market power. Once a firm is not a pure price taker, its action affects demand, rival payoffs, and its own profit, so ordinary one-person maximization is no longer enough.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A payoff function u_i(a_i, a_-i) maps everyone's actions to player i's payoff. The useful relation is `u_i(a_i, a_-i).`. In plain language: i is one player; a_i is that player's action; a_-i means everyone else's actions; u_i is the payoff assigned to the whole action profile.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version starts from two extremes. In perfect competition, one seller is too small to move price. In monopoly, one seller faces the whole market alone. Imperfect competition is the middle case: a few firms have market power, so their own choices affect price or demand, but rivals still matter. That is why the lecture can begin with demand curves and markups but eventually has to introduce Cournot and Bertrand models. The mathematical move is to turn a market into a game among firms. Each firm has a payoff formula, and each payoff depends on both its own action and the rival's action.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is strategic profit maximization. Profit is not just revenue minus cost; in these models, revenue depends on the market price, and the market price depends on the total choices of firms. A best-response curve records the quantity or price that maximizes one firm's profit for each rival action.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because words like competition and market power are too loose. The model must say what firms choose, how choices determine price or demand, and how profit changes when rivals act. Best responses make the strategic dependence explicit.

Why is this concept important?

Imperfect competition matters because it explains markups, undercutting, entry, quantity competition, and why market outcomes can differ from both perfect competition and monopoly. It is the bridge from payoff tables to economic applications where firms are strategic players.

What breaks without it?

Without this concept, the course could not explain why a firm may charge above cost, why price competition can be brutal, or why changing the modeling choice from quantity to price can change the prediction. Market behavior would be described as if firms either had no power or total power.

Worked Mini-Example

Two nearby coffee shops both choose how much to sell or what price to charge. If one shop raises price, some customers leave; if one shop expands quantity, the market price may fall. The best choice is therefore not just a cost calculation. It depends on the rival's choice and on how customers respond.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by mixing up the market story with the strategic object. The story may be coffee shops, patents, or internet providers, but the object is the payoff map from firm choices to profits.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is model specification. Cournot and Bertrand are not universal laws of competition; they are different ways to represent what firms choose. The lecture itself flags this by contrasting quantity choice and price choice. In real markets, capacity constraints, product differentiation, search frictions, contracts, and regulation can change which representation fits. The concept is strongest when the analyst can defend why price, quantity, entry, or another action is the strategic variable.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to treat imperfect competition as just monopoly with more firms. The whole point is that each firm has some power but not complete power. The strategic object is the interaction among firms, not only the demand curve facing one seller.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for a model where firms choose prices, quantities, entry, capacity, or product features while knowing that rivals' actions change the payoff. If the paper asks for a best response by a firm rather than a single optimal monopoly price, this concept is active.

Connected Concepts

Imperfect competition connects strategic-form games, best response, Nash equilibrium, implicit cartels, and one-shot deviation. The Cournot and Bertrand contrast also previews why model representation matters: changing the action from quantity to price can change the strategic prediction.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Two coffee shops choose quantities or prices in the same neighborhood.

Walkthrough: If one shop expands output, market price may fall and change the other's best quantity. If one shop raises price, customers may shift. Each firm's choice is evaluated against the rival's choice, not in isolation.

Lesson: Imperfect competition turns market power into a strategic best-response problem.

Trap: Do not use the perfect-competition story when each firm is large enough to move the other firm's payoff.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Best Response card shows why firms cannot choose price or quantity in isolation. Each firm's move is evaluated against rivals' moves, so market outcomes come from intersecting response incentives.
