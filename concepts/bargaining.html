Bargaining

Two people (or groups, or countries) have something they could gain by working together. But they disagree on how to split that gain. One side wants more, the other wants more. Bargaining is the math of what actually happens when they negotiate—who gets what, and why some deals fall apart or take a long time.

What real-world problem is this about?

When a seller and buyer meet on a used car, both would prefer a deal to no deal. But the seller wants the price high, the buyer wants it low. Each can walk away. Each can wait. Each can reject offers. Bargaining asks: what price will actually stick, and how long will it take to agree?

Why does this problem exist?

Bargaining exists because when two sides can both gain from working together, but they fight over the split, something has to give. If you can walk away (you have a backup plan), you have power. If you can wait, you have power. If you refuse an offer, the deal dies and you get less. The tension between wanting to agree and wanting a bigger slice of the pie is the center of bargaining.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A payoff function u_i(a_i, a_-i) assigns a player a score that depends on two things: that player's action (a_i) and everyone else's actions (a_-i). For bargaining: if a player rejects an offer, u_i drops to their outside option (their fallback payoff). If they accept, u_i becomes their share of the deal. The math ties each action to its score, so you can work backward from payoffs to figure out who will agree to what.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The full lecture starts with two parties who both gain from a deal but disagree on the split. Offers can be accepted or rejected. If rejected, delay follows (each round costs them time and discounts their future payoff). Outside options set the floor—if an offer is worse than walking away, they walk away. Patience is a weapon—the party that can wait longer or faces less time pressure can demand more. The course uses backward induction: in round 3, the player making the offer knows they will get rejected if they demand too much, so they settle. In round 2, the rejector in round 3 knows what will happen, so they hold out. In round 1, the original offerer knows both rounds ahead, so they demand most of the surplus. The prediction: power comes from who moves first, who can afford to wait, and who has the better outside option.

Mathematical Intuition

Two ideas drive the math. First: payoff mapping ties every action to a score, so you can calculate who benefits from each choice. Second: continuation value—if you reject this offer today, what is the deal worth to you tomorrow after delay? If tomorrow's deal is worth less to you (because you are impatient or time-sensitive), today's offer looks better, so you accept. If you are patient or have a rich outside option, you can afford to reject and wait. The person who can afford to wait longest demands the biggest slice.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical model has to exist because you cannot guess the split from the size of the surplus alone. A huge surplus (say, 1 million dollars) can be split 999k-to-1k if one side has all the power. The split comes from outside options, impatience, timing, and information—not from how much value is available. To explain who gets what, you have to put these factors into a model and solve it. Without the math, you have only intuition, which misses how use (outside options, patience, timing) shapes the deal.

Why is this concept important?

In real life, negotiation happens everywhere. Wage talks, mergers, peace treaties, even roommates splitting rent. In all of these, the value created by agreement is real, but how it splits between the parties is not written in stone. The split depends on who can afford to wait, who has other options, who has private information, and when offers must be made. Game theory shows how these forces shape the actual split.

What breaks without it?

Without bargaining theory, you might say agreement is efficient (everyone is better off than if they quit) but then shrug about division. The course would skip the hard part: who gets 60% and who gets 40%? Why? Bargaining theory explains that the split comes from each side's outside option (what they get if talks fail), how much delay costs each side, and who has the power to make an offer. Leave out that tool and you have no way to predict what deal actually happens.

Worked Mini-Example

A seller owns a car and values it at 8,000 dollars (they need 8,000 to be happy). A buyer values it at 10,000 dollars (they would be thrilled to pay 10,000). If they trade at any price from 8,000 to 10,000, the surplus split happens—whoever holds out longer or makes the first offer captures more of that 2,000 gap. If the buyer makes the offer first and the seller has no outside option (no other buyer), the seller might get 8,200 and the buyer gets 1,800 of the surplus. If they have equal power, maybe 9,000 each.

Where Students Get Stuck

The trap is thinking: the split ought to be fair, so that must be the answer. Game theory does not care about fair. It asks: given the outside options, the patience of each side, and the timing of offers, which split will actually happen and stay stable? A 30-70 split might be predicted if one side has all the power. Students have to let go of the idea that the answer should be nice or equal.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary of the model is how well you have captured the real outside options, patience, and information. If the course models wrong what someone gets if talks fail, the predicted split will be wrong. In real life, identifying the outside option is hard—does a seller of a house have one buyer or ten? Does a worker have other job offers? The bargaining model explains one mechanism for how these forces shape the split, but you have to accurately spot what the outside options, time pressures, and information asymmetries actually are in each situation.

Common Misunderstanding

Many students think bargaining power is vague—something like charisma or stubbornness. The model shows it is not. Power is concrete: it is your outside option (if talks fail, what do you get?), your patience (how much does waiting hurt?), and your timing (can you make the offer, or must you wait for one?). If the model shows one side gets 70% of the surplus, it is because one of these concrete factors gives them an edge.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you see negotiations over a division (salary talks, merger price, splitting inheritance, peace treaty terms, or any situation where both sides gain from a deal but fight over the split), you are seeing bargaining. Look for these clues: one side makes an offer, the other accepts or rejects; there may be multiple rounds; delay is costly; one side has a backup plan (outside option); one side can wait longer than the other.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: separate whether trade creates surplus from how that surplus is divided. Then inspect outside options, patience, and timing. Bargaining power in the model comes from continuation values, not from verbal toughness or moral deservingness. A better fallback can change the split without changing total surplus. Keep those calculations separate.

Connected Concepts

Bargaining connects to the payoff-mapping idea (every action leads to a score). It uses backward induction (work from the last round back to the first to find which offers stick). It uses discounting (waiting has a cost—future payoffs are worth less today). It links to information because if you do not know the other side's values, you might make an offer that gets rejected. It also connects to mechanism design: if you design the rules of bargaining (who moves first, can they make a new offer, what happens at disagreement), you shape the outcome.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A buyer values a car at 10000 and a seller values keeping it at 8000.

Walkthrough: Trade creates 2000 of surplus because the buyer values the car more than the seller does. Bargaining does not ask whether trade is possible; it asks how that surplus is divided. Patience, outside options, and timing shape the split.

Lesson: Bargaining separates the existence of gains from trade from the fight over who captures them.

Trap: Do not confuse total surplus with each side's payoff.

Model move: The modeling move is to separate the size of the pie from the rule for splitting it. Once surplus exists, patience, outside options, and proposal power explain bargaining leverage.

Reader action: Compute total surplus separately from each side's fallback payoff. Then change one outside option or waiting cost and trace how the feasible split changes without pretending the total gain from trade changed automatically. This separates whether trade should happen from who has leverage once trade is possible.

Numbers / table check: If buyer value is 10000 and seller value is 8000, surplus is 2000. If the seller's outside option rises to 9000, surplus falls to 1000 and the seller's threat point improves. Keep those two effects separate.

Boundary check: The example is incomplete if either side has private information, transaction costs, deadlines, or alternatives that change the true surplus or disagreement payoff.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Discounted Payoff Sum card explains why time changes bargaining power. Waiting transforms future gains into present value, so a formally larger future payoff can be less attractive than a smaller payoff now. In bargaining problems, separate total surplus from disagreement payoffs first, then use discounting to see how delay, patience, or outside options change the split. The math should not hide the basic question: who can afford to wait?

Bargaining As Dividing Surplus Under Pressure

Start From Two Sides Who Could Both Gain

Bargaining begins when people can create value by reaching agreement, but they disagree about how to divide it. A buyer and seller may both prefer trade to no trade. Workers and firms may both prefer employment to separation. Countries may both prefer peace to conflict. The hard part is not only whether agreement is possible. It is who gets how much, who can wait, and what happens if talks fail.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Ordinary talk about fairness does not settle bargaining. Each side has an outside option, a cost of delay, private information, and a reason to push for a better share. Bargaining theory exists because agreement is strategic. An offer is not only a number; it is a test of what the other side will accept, how patient they are, and what they believe will happen later. Timing and information shape the division.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to say the parties should split the surplus evenly because that sounds reasonable. Equal split may happen in some models, but it is not automatic. A patient side, a side with a better outside option, or a side with private information may get more. Another mistake is to ignore delay. Waiting can be a bargaining weapon, but it can also destroy value.

Why It Matters Beyond Negotiation Examples

Bargaining matters in wages, mergers, settlements, household decisions, international agreements, and platform contracts. It also connects to topology because some bargaining solutions are existence or fixed-point claims about feasible sets and preferences. The mathematical object must still answer a plain question: which agreements are possible, which disagreement outcome matters, and why would each side accept the proposed division? The same structure appears whenever people can jointly gain but must decide who captures the gain and who bears the cost of delay.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Labor markets

Wages depend not only on productivity but also on outside options, patience, replacement costs, and what each side believes the other will accept.

Legal settlement

A settlement divides the expected surplus from avoiding trial. Delay, risk, and private information can prevent agreement even when settlement would save costs.
