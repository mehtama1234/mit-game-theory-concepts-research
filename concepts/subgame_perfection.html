Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium

A plan that still makes sense if the game jumps to any point partway through — not just from the start.

What real-world problem is this about?

Sometimes a player says 'I will do something painful to punish you.' That threat only works if the player would actually carry it out if the moment came. But if the painful thing hurts the player too, they might not do it. A plan built on such a hollow threat falls apart once you realize the threat is fake.

Why does this problem exist?

In a game that unfolds over time, your strategy is a full instruction: what you do at the start, what you do if a certain thing happens, what you do after that. Those later instructions matter — even if you hope they never get used. A credible plan must make sense at every point in the game, not only when looking ahead from the beginning.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Work backward from the end of the game. At each decision point, pick the move that gives the best score for what comes next. Write this as: the value at this point equals the highest score you can get from your next move onward. At each step, you choose the branch with the highest future score.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

A strategy in a staged game includes a plan for every scenario, even ones that should not happen. Nash says: 'Does anyone want to switch their move at the start?' Subgame perfection adds: 'At every moment in the game tree, if we reach that moment, would the strategy still tell you to make the same move, or would you want to change it then?' If you would want to change it later, your strategy hides a threat or promise you would not keep — and that is not an equilibrium once players see through it.

Mathematical Intuition

Think of the game tree as a tree of branches. A subgame is any subtree — any branch point and everything below it. The strategy must be an equilibrium at the root of the tree and also an equilibrium inside every subtree. Equilibrium at every part means nobody has a reason to deviate anywhere.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

A strategy written down includes plans for futures that may never occur — branches of the tree the players hope to avoid. Those plans can look great on paper but be impossible to execute. Mathematics formalizes the requirement that every plan, even the ones hidden in branches that never happen, must still be a plan the player would choose if that branch actually arrived. Subgame perfection is the formal statement of this requirement.

Why is this concept important?

Nash equilibrium alone is not enough for games that unfold in stages. You need to check whether your strategy — especially the moves you would make later, after certain histories — would actually be moves you would choose if that moment really arrived. Subgame perfection is the check that makes sure.

What breaks without it?

Without it, you can build strategies on promises you would never keep. A player might say 'if you enter, I will fight back hard' — but if fighting back after entry would lose them money, they would never actually do it. The equilibrium survives only because it hides this broken promise.

Worked Mini-Example

A firm in the market says 'I will cut my price to zero if you enter.' The entrant thinks: 'If I enter and they cut their price to zero, they make no money. They would not stay at zero forever — they would raise prices back up. So their threat to stay at zero is not credible.' Because the threat is not credible, the entrant ignores it and enters.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students get tripped up because they only check whether people want to deviate at the start. They ignore paths that 'should not happen' in equilibrium. But threats only work by affecting what players expect to happen if they deviate. Those off-path scenarios are where hollow threats hide, so the course forces you to look.

Where The Idea Stops Working

Subgame perfection works cleanly for games where every decision point has a clear subgame — a branch where beliefs do not matter because all players know what happened. When hidden information enters — when one player does not know something about another player's history — the picture changes. Some branch points do not form clean subgames because players disagree about what history led there. That is where Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium takes over later in the course. The boundary is this: subgame perfection works where the game tree is fully known; once beliefs enter, we need a stronger tool.

Common Misunderstanding

Students think: 'If it is a Nash equilibrium from the start, it must be fine.' The mistake is forgetting that strategies include moves for scenarios that may never happen. Those hidden moves can be unrealistic, and unrealistic moves do not scare anyone.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Watch for any strategic situation where someone makes a threat or promise, and ask: would they actually follow through? Watch for games that play out in stages. Watch for plans that include moves 'just in case' on branches that should not happen. In all these cases, subgame perfection is the question: would the plan hold up even if that supposedly-impossible branch showed up?

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: Nash at the start is not enough for dynamic games. Jump into each relevant continuation game and rerun the no-deviation test. Subgame perfection rejects strategies that survive only because their threats are never reached. The practical question is whether the plan remains rational after the history it tries to deter.

Connected Concepts

This concept connects backward induction — solving a game by working from end to start — to Nash equilibrium. Later, when we add hidden information to games, we will need an even stronger check called Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium, which adds beliefs about what other players know. Subgame perfection is the stepping stone.

Worked Example Card

Setup: An entrant considers entering a market after the incumbent threatens a price war.

Walkthrough: The proposed strategy may look like a Nash equilibrium if the entrant is scared away. But subgame perfection asks what happens in the subgame after entry. If the incumbent would rather accommodate than fight there, the threat is not credible.

Lesson: Subgame perfection removes equilibria supported by empty threats.

Trap: Do not accept a threat merely because it keeps the game from reaching the place where the threat would be tested.

Model move: The modeling move is to test equilibrium behavior inside every subgame that could be reached. A strategy must remain optimal even after the history that was supposed to be deterred.

Reader action: Jump to the history the threat tries to prevent and solve from there. If the threatened action is not optimal once that history is reached, mark the original strategy profile as failing the subgame test. If no proper subgame starts there, flag that beliefs may be doing the work instead.

Numbers / table check: After entry, compare fight payoff 1 with accommodate payoff 4 for the incumbent. If the proposed equilibrium says fight after entry, the continuation subgame fails even when the entrant stayed out in the proposed path.

Boundary check: The example needs refinement when information sets prevent a proper subgame, or when off-path beliefs rather than subgame structure carry the prediction.

Subgame Perfection As Credibility At Every Later Point

Start From A Plan That Reaches The Future

Subgame perfection asks whether an equilibrium plan remains sensible after every later point that could be reached. A plan can be a Nash equilibrium because nobody wants to change at the very start, yet it may rely on a threat that would be foolish later. Subgame perfection tightens the test. It says the strategy must pass the no-deviation logic not only from the beginning, but also inside each proper continuation of the game.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Sequential games need a stronger credibility check than one-shot strategic-form games. A firm may threaten a price war after entry. A negotiator may threaten to reject a future offer. A parent, manager, or government may announce a punishment. The model has to ask whether that future action will be worthwhile once the later moment arrives. Subgame perfection exists because early incentives and later incentives can differ.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to accept any Nash equilibrium in a game tree as a serious prediction. Some Nash equilibria survive only because they contain off-path threats that no one would actually carry out. Subgame perfection rejects those empty threats when the relevant subgame can be evaluated. It does not solve every dynamic information problem, but it teaches the reader to inspect credibility after histories, not just at the root.

Why It Matters Beyond Entry Games

Subgame perfection matters in bargaining, regulation, litigation, industrial organization, diplomacy, and organizational discipline. It also links to topology and fixed-point thinking through the larger family of equilibrium refinements. Existence of a stable point is not enough when the point is supported by unbelievable future behavior. The refinement asks whether the fixed point has the right internal structure along the time path.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Regulation

A regulator's announced punishment shapes behavior only if the regulator will still want to impose it later. Credibility changes compliance because firms reason about the later enforcement choice, not only the written rule.

Diplomacy

A threat can deter action only when carrying it out later is believable. Subgame perfection makes that future check explicit and separates real deterrence from language that sounds strong early.
