Iterated Deletion

Repeatedly removing bad strategies and seeing what remains.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that once obviously bad moves are removed, new moves may become obviously bad in the smaller game.

Why does this problem exist?

This exists because reasoning about rational players is recursive. I can remove my dominated action, then you can reason that I removed it, then your own comparison may change.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Dominance compares one strategy against another across possible opponent choices. The useful relation is `s dominates t if u_i(s, a_-i) >= u_i(t, a_-i) for all a_-i, with strict inequality somewhere.`. In plain language: The inequality checks every possible action by others; dominance means one strategy is never worse and sometimes better.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version adds recursion to dominance. After dominated strategies are removed, the remaining game is no longer the same game for reasoning purposes. A strategy that was not dominated before can become dominated after the opponent’s unreasonable options disappear. This creates a chain of inference: I rule out your dominated move, you rule out mine, and so on. The operation is simple, but its interpretation is deep because it depends on players understanding rationality and sometimes understanding that others understand it.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is repeated pruning over a shrinking action set. Each round applies the dominance comparison to the reduced game, so the domain of possible opponent actions changes as the procedure proceeds.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because rationality can be layered. Once one impossible action is removed, the remaining problem changes, and new impossibilities can appear. Iterated deletion is the formal procedure that keeps track of that changing action set instead of relying on informal rounds of intuition.

Why is this concept important?

Iterated deletion matters because strategic reasoning can be recursive. Removing one unreasonable choice can make another choice unreasonable in the smaller game, so rationality can propagate through the table in stages.

What breaks without it?

Without iterated deletion, the course would miss how one round of reasoning changes the next. Some games reveal their strategic structure only after obviously dominated actions are removed and the remaining incentives are reconsidered.

Worked Mini-Example

In a guessing game, very high guesses may be impossible for a rational winner; once those are removed, the next-highest guesses become impossible too. The deletion process walks inward.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by treating the order of deletion and the type of dominance as harmless details. With weak dominance especially, those details can change what remains, so the lecture distinction matters.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the deletion rule. Strict deletion has a different force than weak deletion, and common knowledge assumptions matter when interpreting many rounds of pruning. The boundary is why the lecture distinguishes the procedure from equilibrium selection. Surviving deletion means a strategy has not been ruled out by that rationality test; it does not mean the strategy will be played.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to ignore whether the deletion uses strict or weak dominance. Weak deletion can depend on order and needs more care.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for step-by-step elimination arguments where rationality of others is used more than once.

Connected Concepts

Iterated deletion connects dominance to rationalizability and common knowledge. It also contrasts with Nash: deletion rules can narrow the game before equilibrium, but they do not always select a single stable prediction.

Worked Example Card

Setup: Players in a guessing game choose numbers, and very high guesses cannot be optimal once everyone understands the target rule.

Walkthrough: First remove guesses that cannot win against any rational play. After those are gone, the upper bound of reasonable guesses falls. That can make the next-highest guesses impossible too, so the deletion repeats on the smaller game.

Lesson: Iterated deletion shows how one round of rationality can create the next round's reasoning.

Trap: Do not treat surviving deletion as the same thing as being played in equilibrium.

Model move: The modeling move is to let one round of eliminated impossibilities change the next round's strategic environment. Each deletion narrows what rational players need to consider.

Reader action: Keep a deletion log with round number, deleted action, and the payoff comparison that allowed it. Never use a later deletion to justify an earlier one, because the order is part of the argument. When the process stops, name what survived and what the method still has not predicted.

Boundary check: The example stops proving prediction when multiple strategies survive, when weak deletion changes the result, or when common knowledge of rationality is not plausible.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Dominance Inequality card gives the one-round deletion test; iterated deletion repeats the same test after earlier impossible moves have been removed. The mathematical idea is simple but powerful: once the game shrinks, some actions that previously looked possible may lose their support. Keep a deletion log, because the proof depends on what was known and removed at each round, not just on the final surviving list.
