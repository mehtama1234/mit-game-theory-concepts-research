One-Shot Deviation Principle

A test that cuts through checking endless strategy changes by asking one simple question: if a player changes what they do at just one moment, given that they stick to their plan everywhere else, can they get a better score?

What real-world problem is this about?

Imagine you wrote down a complete plan for how to behave in a game that plays out over many moments — what you'd do after every possible thing that could happen. Checking whether someone would actually want to stick to that plan sounds simple: does anyone want to cheat? But there are so many moments and so many ways to cheat that checking all of them seems impossible.

Why does this problem exist?

This principle exists because of a deep fact about how scores add up in games that either have a final ending or where future payoffs count a little less each turn (called discounting). In those settings, if you could make yourself better off by stringing together many small rule-breaks, at least one of those small breaks by itself would have to make you better off at that moment. So instead of hunting for complicated multi-step cheating schemes, you only have to check the simple ones.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

You work backward from the end of the game. At the last decision, you pick the move that gives the best score from that point on. Call that best continuation score V. At each earlier decision, you also pick the move that leads to the best continuation value from there. The formula is: at each point, the best value equals the best score you can get from your next move plus the continuation value after. In shorthand: V(now) = highest(score from next choice + V(what comes after)).

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture solves a real problem. When you write out a complete strategy — what to do after every possible history — you have written an enormous object for a game with many turns. You want to check that nobody gains from switching, but comparing the proposed plan to all alternatives is infeasible. The one-shot deviation principle says: it is enough to check one-action changes at single histories. For each decision point a player faces, and each action available there, ask: if I change that action at that moment and stick to the plan everywhere else, do I score higher? If the answer is no everywhere, the plan survives verification. This transforms a task with infinitely many alternatives into a finite procedure.

Mathematical Intuition

The intuition is that small optimality compounds into large optimality in the right setting. If every tiny local decision you make is a best response given what comes after, then the whole sequence of decisions is a best response to the whole future. Conversely, if you could profit from a large departure from your plan, there must be at least one moment inside that departure where a local change alone would have been profitable. So check the local changes; if none work, the plan is airtight.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because dynamic games create huge spaces of possible strategies. A person's strategy in a game lasting many turns says what to do after countless different things that might happen — that is a massive object to write down and compare. You need a theorem that reduces the verification problem: instead of comparing everything, compare only one-shot deviations at each history. Without this theorem, verifying optimality in dynamic games by hand would be impossible for anything but tiny examples. The theorem makes the course teachable.

Why is this concept important?

When you are verifying that a plan is actually a good plan, you could try changing it in unlimited ways: change it after this history, change it after that history, change it for two histories at once, and so on. That list is endless. This principle says: if you have checked that no change at a single moment helps, you do not need to check combinations. That shrinks the work from impossible to doable.

What breaks without it?

If you do not have this shortcut, you face a choice: either skip checking whether people want to cheat (which is not safe), or write down every possible alternative plan and score them (which takes forever for games that last many turns). For a game where your choice today affects the scores available tomorrow, and tomorrow's scores affect the day after, the number of plans to check grows so fast that you cannot do it by hand.

Worked Mini-Example

You and a friend play a repeated game where you both try to earn points. You both agree on a plan: cooperate this round, and keep cooperating next round as long as nobody cheated. Instead of checking whether someone could wiggle out by rewriting the entire plan, you just ask: if I cheat today but faithfully follow the plan tomorrow (being kind again), does that one-day cheat earn me more? If no, then elaborate multi-round cheating plans are unlikely to work either, because each part of them would have had to improve the score at its own moment.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often think the principle is automatic — usable everywhere — and forget that it depends on the game's structure. They memorize the shortcut but skip verifying that the assumptions (finite horizon, or constant discounting) actually hold in the game at hand. Then they apply it to a game with externalities or state-dependent payoffs and get a wrong answer. The principle is not free; it is only good when the structure justifies it.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is where the theorem's conditions stop holding. The principle works when the game is finite or when future payoffs shrink by a constant discount rate at each step. Outside those settings — in games with perpetual external shocks, or where payoff structures couple together in time-varying ways, or where the future is not discounted — the principle can misfire. There is also a pedagogical boundary: the principle is a labor-saving theorem, not a substitute for thinking about incentives. Students must still identify the histories, the deviations to check, and the logic of whether each deviation is profitable. The principle tells you where to focus, not whether to think.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to use this shortcut everywhere. It only works when the game satisfies specific conditions: either the game ends after a known number of rounds, or future payoffs decrease by a constant factor each turn, or other structural properties hold. If you use the principle in a game where payoffs have complex interdependencies or change unpredictably, the shortcut can fail and you get a false sense of security.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you see a proof that verifies a strategy in a dynamic game, look for whether it replaces a full strategy comparison with a finite list of one-shot deviations. If an author writes, 'Suppose player i deviates at history h by choosing action a' instead of a, holding the rest of the plan fixed, then...' and uses that to conclude the strategy is optimal, they are using this principle. It is a signature move in repeated games and dynamic settings.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: the one-shot deviation principle is a theorem-backed shortcut, not a universal habit. Change exactly one current action, hold the continuation fixed, and ask whether that local temptation pays under conditions where local checks are sufficient. If the conditions fail, longer deviations may be hiding.

Connected Concepts

This principle ties together many ideas in the course. It relies on the same backward-building logic as subgame perfection (where you verify optimality by looking backward from the end). It is how you check strategies in repeated games and explains why trigger strategies (plans that punish cheaters) actually work. And it uses the same core idea: credibility — can you stick to what you said you would do, given that later you still benefit from doing it?

Worked Example Card

Setup: A player in a repeated relationship follows a long plan but considers cheating today.

Walkthrough: Instead of comparing every possible alternate plan, change one action now and then return to the original plan afterward. If that local deviation never pays at any history, the larger plan can pass the check in the relevant setting.

Lesson: The one-shot deviation principle turns a huge dynamic check into local credibility tests.

Trap: Do not use it outside the conditions where local deviations are enough to test the full plan.

Model move: The modeling move is to reduce an enormous dynamic strategy audit to one-step temptation checks. At each history, ask whether changing only the current action is profitable.

Reader action: At each relevant history, change exactly today's action and keep the continuation plan fixed. Record the immediate gain and the continuation loss; do not silently compare to a completely different future plan. The point is to test local temptation, not redesign the whole strategy.

Numbers / table check: Make a two-row deviation table: follow plan gives current payoff 3 plus continuation 10; cheat today gives current payoff 6 plus continuation 4. If 3 + 10 beats 6 + 4, that one-shot deviation does not pay at this history.

Boundary check: The shortcut is unsafe if the game's technical conditions fail, if profitable deviations require coordinated multi-period changes, or if continuation values are not well defined.

One-Shot Deviation As A Local Credibility Check

Start From Changing One Move

The one-shot deviation principle asks whether a player can gain by changing one action at one decision point while keeping the rest of the plan the same. The everyday idea is local checking. Instead of testing every possible rewritten strategy at once, ask whether any single moment contains a profitable small escape. If no such one-step escape exists under the right conditions, the whole plan passes an important credibility test.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Dynamic strategies can be long and complicated. A plan may say what to do after many histories. Checking every alternative plan directly can be overwhelming. The one-shot deviation principle exists because, in the right class of games, profitable mistakes can be detected through these local deviations. It lets the course inspect sequential rationality without turning every dynamic problem into an unmanageable list of complete strategy rewrites.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to treat the principle as a universal shortcut. It depends on conditions such as the structure of the game and payoff continuity or discounting. Another mistake is to check the intended path only. A strategy also says what happens after histories that might not occur. If an off-path continuation gives someone a profitable one-shot deviation, the proposed plan still has a credibility problem.

Why It Matters Beyond Repeated Games

The principle matters in contracts, organizational rules, repeated relationships, and policy enforcement because long plans often fail at one concrete future moment. It also links to fixed-point and dynamic programming ideas: local optimality checks can support a global strategic claim when the mathematical conditions are right. The plain lesson is that big promises must survive small later temptations. It is useful because many failures of cooperation are not grand redesigns of the whole plan; they are small moments where one actor quietly wants to do something else.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Contracts

A long contract can fail because one party wants to deviate at one future clause or enforcement moment. Local checks reveal that pressure.

Organizations

A workplace rule may look stable until a manager has a one-time incentive not to enforce it. The shortcut focuses attention there.
