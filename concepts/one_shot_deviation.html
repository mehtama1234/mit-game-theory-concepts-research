One-Shot Deviation Principle

A shortcut for checking whether changing one move at one point can improve a strategy.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is checking a whole dynamic strategy can be exhausting because a strategy describes what to do after many histories.

Why does this problem exist?

The one-shot deviation principle exists because, in the right finite or discounted settings, profitable complex deviations reveal themselves through a profitable single-period deviation.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Backward induction assigns optimal choices from terminal decision nodes back to the start. The useful relation is `V(node) = max_action V(next node).`. In plain language: V is continuation value; at each decision point the player chooses the next branch with the best continuation value.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture-depth version solves a verification problem. Dynamic strategies are long objects: they say what to do after many histories. Checking every possible alternative strategy can be impossible by hand. Under the principle’s conditions, it is enough to check whether a player can gain by changing one action at one history while otherwise returning to the proposed strategy. That local test can certify the larger plan.

Mathematical Intuition

The mathematical intuition is local optimality implying global optimality in the relevant dynamic environment. If no single stitch can be profitably pulled out, then a longer deviation cannot be assembled profitably from such local changes.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

The mathematical object has to exist because dynamic strategies are too large to compare naively. A local deviation test gives the course a finite, teachable way to verify optimality under the right assumptions. It turns the huge space of possible strategy changes into a sequence of one-history checks.

Why is this concept important?

The one-shot deviation principle matters because dynamic strategies can contain many possible deviations. The principle gives a manageable local test when its conditions hold: if no single-period change helps, longer deviations need not be separately checked.

What breaks without it?

Without this principle, verifying repeated or dynamic equilibria can become intractable. Students may either skip deviation checks or drown in too many possible alternative histories.

Worked Mini-Example

Instead of checking every possible plan for a repeated-game player, check whether cheating today and returning to the plan tomorrow pays. If even that one-step cheat fails everywhere, the larger plan may be safe.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often get stuck by treating the principle as universally available. It depends on the structure of the dynamic game, so the shortcut must travel with its assumptions.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary is the theorem conditions. The shortcut cannot be used blindly in every dynamic game; the structure must justify local checks standing in for global deviations. The boundary is also pedagogical: the principle is a theorem that saves work, not a license to ignore dynamic incentives. Students still need to know what histories and one-step deviations must be checked.

Common Misunderstanding

The mistake is to use the shortcut without checking the assumptions that make local deviations enough.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for dynamic incentive proofs that replace many possible deviations with a one-period deviation inequality.

Connected Concepts

One-shot deviation connects subgame perfection to repeated games and trigger strategies. It uses the same credibility primitive as backward induction but applies it to richer strategy plans.

Worked Example Card

Setup: A player in a repeated relationship follows a long plan but considers cheating today.

Walkthrough: Instead of comparing every possible alternate plan, change one action now and then return to the original plan afterward. If that local deviation never pays at any history, the larger plan can pass the check in the relevant setting.

Lesson: The one-shot deviation principle turns a huge dynamic check into local credibility tests.

Trap: Do not use it outside the conditions where local deviations are enough to test the full plan.

Equation Walkthroughs

The Backward Induction card shows why one local deviation can reveal a broken sequential plan. If a player would switch at a future node, the earlier strategy profile was never fully credible.
