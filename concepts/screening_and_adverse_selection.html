Screening And Adverse Selection

When one person or firm offers a deal without knowing who the other person really is—their quality, health, or risk—they end up attracting a worse type of person than they expected. The reason: the deal itself acts like a filter, pulling in the people for whom the offer is most valuable, often because they stand to gain more from having it. That filtering can make trade fall apart.

What real-world problem is this about?

Imagine you are setting a price or a rule for a deal you are offering. You think through what the average person would do, and you set your offer accordingly. But here is the catch: once people hear your offer, the ones who accept it are not the average anymore—they are the ones for whom your offer looked like a good choice. That means the people who show up are a filtered group, not the typical one you planned for.

Why does this problem exist?

Screening and adverse selection happen when one side of a deal knows something important—their true quality, how much it will cost to serve them, their health risk, or whether the deal is actually good for them—and the other side does not know it. The uninformed side (the one making the offer) can only set terms and watch who accepts. Those terms are like a sieve: different types of people experience them differently. A person who knows they are a bad risk finds a certain price much more attractive than a person who knows they are low-risk. The sieve filters the group, changing who shows up.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A belief—your guess about something you do not know—is a list of possible situations (called states or types) with a probability for each one. If you do not know the type but you guess the odds, the expected payoff is found by multiplying each payoff by its probability and adding them up: `E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta).` In this formula, theta stands for the unknown fact (which type the person is); p(theta) is how likely you think that type is; u(a, theta) is what you get if you act with choice a and the type is theta. Expected payoff is a weighted average.

Lecture-Depth Walkthrough

The lecture starts by asking a puzzle: if both sides want to trade and gains exist, why does the bargaining model predict immediate agreement? Lecture 23 gives the answer: incomplete information breaks the model in two ways. First, the terms of a deal do not just divide surplus—they also sort types. An insurer cannot set a price based on the average person in the full population, because the price itself determines who comes forward. If you charge low, healthy people buy and you lose money. If you charge high, sick people buy and you still lose money. The population that shows up is not the average; it is the subset filtered by the price. Second, when someone accepts or rejects a deal, that choice reveals information about them. If a used car owner eagerly accepts your offer to buy their car, you learn something—usually something bad. The buyer now adjusts their belief and the negotiation changes direction. Together, these two forces—filtering and information revelation—mean that the payoff calculation must be redone after each move, using only the types who remain in play, not the types you started with. A concrete walkthrough: Start with all possible customers, who might be healthy or sick. You set a price thinking about the average person. Once the price is public, healthy people think it is too high and leave; sick people think it is low and buy. Your true average cost is now higher than expected. You lose money. You raise the price. Now even sicker people buy, because they value it more. You still lose money. The agreement unravels or never happens because the deal itself changed the population.

Mathematical Intuition

The core intuition is that a rule or offer selects from a population. Start with many types. Your offer is a cutoff: some types prefer accepting, some prefer rejecting. After you announce the offer, the types that remain are only those who accepted. That subset has a different average than the original population. The math forces you to condition on the subset. You cannot use the original distribution anymore because the rule itself changed who is in the game.

Why This Mathematical Object Has To Exist

Ordinary reasoning with an average lie in filtered populations. If you set a price and only certain types accept, the people who say yes are not representative of everyone you offered to. You need a math object—a cutoff rule, a participation condition, or a posterior belief—to track who joins after the rule is made known. That object has to capture the idea that some types prefer in and some prefer out, and the payoff depends only on the ones who accept.

Why is this concept important?

This concept matters because it explains why trade sometimes stops, delays, or falls through—even when both sides would be happier if the deal went through. Insurance people know they do not see the full picture when they set a price. Sellers of used goods worry that only the worst ones get bought. People bargaining over money or contracts sometimes give up because revealing information through offers is risky. In every case, hidden information and selective acceptance block deals that would be good for both sides if all the facts were on the table.

What breaks without it?

Without this concept, the course would predict that whenever a buyer wants something more than the seller wants to keep it, they would simply agree on a price and trade. That is true when both sides know all the facts. But the concept shows why it is false when information is hidden: an offer attracts certain types and repels others, and the change in who accepts can destroy the gains from trade. The course would miss entire categories of real behavior—refusals to insure certain groups, haggling that breaks down, quality spirals in used-goods markets.

Worked Mini-Example

An insurance company sets a price of 50 per month for health insurance. A person who knows they go to the doctor every month thinks: that is a bargain, I will buy. A person who never gets sick thinks: 50 is expensive for something I will not use, I will skip it. The company wanted to charge an average customer, but it attracted the expensive ones. When it computes how much it actually costs to serve the people who bought in, the true average is higher than the population average, so the price was too low.

Where Students Get Stuck

Students often slip into thinking of hidden information as just another source of randomness or uncertainty. But the tricky part is that the action—the price or offer—changes which types remain. The probability distribution after the action is not the same as before. Hidden information by itself is just hard to predict. But screening and adverse selection say: the prediction problem gets worse because your action filters out some types and leaves others.

Where The Idea Stops Working

The boundary of this concept is the source of the filtering. Not every case where people do not trade is adverse selection. The mechanism requires that one side has private information about their type—about value, cost, quality, or risk—and that the other side's offer filters these types differently. In the lecture's examples (insurance and bargaining), information about health or outside options truly is private, and the price truly does filter by type. Broader claims about real-world insurance markets, legal disputes, or labor markets need evidence that the information is actually hidden, that offers actually filter by type, and that this filtering actually explains what you observe.

Common Misunderstanding

A common mistake is to think only about how many people buy less when the price goes up. But in adverse selection, the composition of buyers shifts too. Raising the price of insurance might bring in even sicker people (who value it more). Lowering the price might attract healthier people (who now find it cheaper). The real puzzle is not just quantity; it is that the people who show up are not the people you expected.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for situations where an offer, price, contract term, or test makes people sort themselves based on what they know about themselves. If changing the offer changes who accepts it, and if the people who accept have different hidden types from the people who reject, then screening and adverse selection are operating. Watch for cases where a rule meant to apply to an average instead pulls in a selected population.

Bottom-Line Diagnostic

Bottom-line diagnostic: whenever a price, offer, or contract changes who participates, recompute the pool. The old average may be irrelevant after selection. The concept is active when hidden types sort themselves through the rule. Participation is evidence, not just a count of customers.

Connected Concepts

Screening and adverse selection sit at the center of incomplete-information games. They connect to beliefs and types—how you model uncertainty about who the other person is; to bargaining—how you make offers and why they might fail; to signaling—how the person who knows the secret proves it to someone who doubts; to auctions—how rules can make truthful bidding the best move; and to perfect Bayesian equilibrium—the way to predict what rational players do when information is hidden and revealed over time.

Setup: An insurer sets one price for people with different hidden health risks.

Walkthrough: At a low price, both low-risk and high-risk buyers may buy. At a higher price, low-risk buyers may leave because the policy is not worth it to them. The remaining pool can become riskier, so raising price can worsen the seller's problem.

Lesson: Adverse selection appears when participation reveals hidden type and changes the group being served.

Trap: Do not assume a higher price simply means more revenue per customer; it can change who the customers are.

Model move: The modeling move is to connect price or contract terms to the composition of participants. The action of joining or leaving reveals information about hidden risk or value.

Reader action: After changing a price or contract, recompute who participates. Do not reuse the original population average, because the people who remain may be exactly the hidden type the old average concealed. Then ask whether a menu of contracts could separate types more cleanly than one pooled offer.

Numbers / table check: Before the price change, list low-risk and high-risk buyers in the pool. After the price increase, cross out anyone who leaves. Recompute average cost using only the remaining rows. If mostly high-risk buyers remain, revenue per customer rose but pool quality worsened.

Boundary check: The example changes when sellers can screen with multiple contracts, buyers have multidimensional private information, or participation is driven by factors outside risk.

Screening And Adverse Selection As Rules Changing Who Shows Up

Start From Hidden Quality Or Cost

Screening and adverse selection begin when one side knows something important that the other side cannot see. A seller knows product quality, a worker knows effort cost, an insured person knows risk, or a bargaining partner knows value. The uninformed side sets a price, contract, or rule. That rule does not just divide surplus. It changes which hidden types choose to participate.

Why The Idea Has To Exist

Trade can fail even when trade would create value under full information. If a buyer offers an average price for used goods, high-quality sellers may leave, lowering the average quality of those who remain. Insurance prices can push low-risk people out, leaving a riskier pool. The concept exists because selection changes the group being analyzed. The average after people respond is not the same as the average before the rule.

The Common Mistake

The mistake is to say there are gains from trade, so agreement should happen. Hidden information can block that simple logic. Another mistake is to ignore participation. A contract that looks profitable against the whole population may attract only the types that make it unprofitable. Screening tries to design choices that separate types, but it must give each type a reason to choose the intended option.

Why It Matters Beyond Market Examples

Screening and adverse selection matter in insurance, credit, hiring, college admissions, platform trust, medical markets, and regulation. They also connect to topology and equilibrium because participation rules can change the feasible set and the belief distribution. A stable outcome may exist, but the important question is which types remain in the market and which valuable trades disappear. The everyday warning is that the rule does not meet a fixed crowd; it helps create the crowd it will later face. This is why participation is part of the model rather than an afterthought; the people who accept the rule are selected by the rule itself.

Where This Shows Up Outside The Course

Insurance

A high premium may drive low-risk people away, leaving a riskier pool. The price changes who buys the contract.

Hiring

A test or credential can screen applicants, but only if different worker types face different costs or benefits from completing it.
