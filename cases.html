Case study

Entry Threat: when a scary announcement is not a credible future action

Real-world setup: A large firm says it will start a price war if a smaller rival enters. The entrant hears the threat before deciding whether to enter, but the price war would also hurt the large firm after entry has already happened.

First-principles question: Is the threat a real constraint on entry, or is it an announcement that will stop being attractive once the future decision point arrives?

Modeling path: Start by drawing the timing. The entrant moves first, then the incumbent reacts. That makes this a tree, not only a payoff table. Solve the incumbent's later choice first, then use that continuation to decide what the entrant should believe at the start.

Mathematical spine: The spine is backward recursion plus mutual consistency. A continuation action must be best for the player who takes it at that later node, and the full prediction must survive those later checks.

Worked walkthrough: Suppose entry gives the incumbent payoff -1 if it fights and 2 if it accommodates. The later node selects accommodate. The entrant should not treat fight as the continuation merely because the incumbent said it earlier. If entry is profitable against accommodation, the threat does not block entry in the model.

Where the simple story breaks: The simple story says a powerful firm can threaten anything. The model breaks that story by asking whether carrying out the threat would still be rational later.

What to check in transcript: Look for the course's movement from representation of games into backward induction and subgame perfection. The evidence should support timing, later incentives, and credibility, not just the word threat.

Case study

Tacit Cartel: when cooperation depends on the weight of the future

Real-world setup: Several sellers keep prices high without signing an explicit agreement. Each seller can earn more this week by secretly undercutting, but rivals may punish future cheating with lower prices.

First-principles question: Can repeated interaction make restraint individually sensible, or does the one-time gain from cheating overwhelm the future punishment?

Modeling path: Start with the one-shot temptation. Then add the repeated relationship and compare today's deviation gain with the discounted stream of future losses. The model only supports cooperation when the future is heavy enough.

Mathematical spine: The spine is a discounted payoff comparison plus incentive compatibility. The intended cooperative action must give at least as much total discounted payoff as cheating once future punishment is included.

Worked walkthrough: If cheating gives an extra 5 today but causes a future loss of 2 each period, the result depends on patience. A very impatient seller treats the future loss as small and cheats. A patient seller may prefer preserving the high-price path.

Where the simple story breaks: The simple story says repeated contact creates cooperation. The model says repetition only helps when monitoring, punishment, and patience make the no-cheating constraint pass.

What to check in transcript: Look for the repeated-game lectures connecting finite endings, infinite horizons, folk-theorem logic, and cartel applications. The evidence should show future punishment as a mechanism rather than a slogan.

Case study

Warranty Signal: when an action reveals hidden type only if imitation is costly

Real-world setup: A used-car seller can offer a warranty. Reliable sellers expect few repairs, while unreliable sellers expect many repairs. Buyers see the warranty but not the car's true quality.

First-principles question: Does the warranty reveal quality, or would every seller imitate the same signal if buyers rewarded it?

Modeling path: Start by naming the hidden types. Then ask what each type would pay to send the signal and how buyers should update after seeing it. A signal separates types only when the cost and payoff differences make imitation unattractive for the wrong type.

Mathematical spine: The spine is belief updating plus incentive compatibility. Buyers use observed behavior to update beliefs, while the seller's action must be optimal for the type assigned to it in the proposed equilibrium.

Worked walkthrough: If a reliable seller expects warranty costs of 1 and an unreliable seller expects costs of 8, a price premium of 4 can make the warranty attractive to reliable sellers but unattractive to unreliable sellers. Then the action can carry information.

Where the simple story breaks: The simple story says a warranty means quality. The model asks why a low-quality seller would not mimic that signal, and the answer must come from payoffs, not trust.

What to check in transcript: Look for Bayesian games, type spaces, perfect Bayesian equilibrium, and signaling. The evidence should support both belief movement and the incentive constraint that keeps messages meaningful.

Case study

Ad Auction Design: when rules turn private values into bids and rankings

Real-world setup: A search platform sells ad slots. Advertisers differ in value per click and ad quality. The platform chooses a rule for ranking ads and charging winners.

First-principles question: What behavior does the rule make individually sensible, and does that behavior produce the allocation the platform wants?

Modeling path: Start with bidders' private values and the allocation rule. Separate the chance of winning, the payment if winning, and the value created by clicks. Then ask whether the rule rewards truthful or strategically shaded behavior.

Mathematical spine: The spine is expected payment and incentive compatibility. A bidder compares value from winning against expected payment, while the platform designs the ranking and price rule that shapes those comparisons.

Worked walkthrough: If raising a bid increases the probability of winning from 20 percent to 70 percent while also raising payment if the ad wins, the bidder must compare both effects. In ad auctions, quality scores add another channel because better ads can change rank and expected value.

Where the simple story breaks: The simple story says the highest bid wins. The course logic shows that auction format, payment rule, quality weighting, and beliefs about rivals all change the strategic incentives.

What to check in transcript: Look for the auctions, revenue equivalence, and ad-auction lectures. The evidence should support the separation between allocation, payment, bid shading, and rule design.

Case study

Public Announcement: when the same fact changes behavior by becoming shared

Real-world setup: Many traders privately hear that a policy change is coming. Before the public announcement, each trader is unsure whether others heard the same information. After the announcement, everyone knows everyone heard it.

First-principles question: Why can a public announcement matter if it does not add a new fact to some traders' private information?

Modeling path: Start by distinguishing truth from shared awareness. Private knowledge may not support coordination because each player is unsure about others' knowledge. A public event changes the levels of reasoning about what others know.

Mathematical spine: The spine is knowledge recursion. The model moves from the event itself, to everyone knowing the event, to everyone knowing everyone knows, and so on.

Worked walkthrough: If each trader privately heard the rumor, selling early may still feel risky because others may not act. A public announcement can make coordinated selling more likely because the fact becomes a shared anchor for expectations.

Where the simple story breaks: The simple story says information is information. The model says publicness changes strategic meaning because action depends on beliefs about other people's beliefs.

What to check in transcript: Look for common knowledge and communication evidence. The evidence should distinguish private belief, public announcement, cheap talk, and equilibrium coordination.
