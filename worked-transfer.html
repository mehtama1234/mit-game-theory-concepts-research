Worked Transfer Examples

Full worked examples for applying the course outside the lecture setting. Each card starts with an unfamiliar situation, builds the model, states the mathematical move, audits assumptions, and ties the reasoning back to transcript evidence.

Worked transfer example

Platform Ban: when a rule threat only works if later enforcement is worth it

New situation: A marketplace tells sellers that any seller caught fake-reviewing will be banned for a year. Sellers earn more from fake reviews this month, but the platform also earns fees from sellers it bans.

Model construction: Model the seller and platform separately. The seller chooses honest or fake-review behavior now. The platform later chooses enforce or forgive after observing a signal. This is a dynamic game because the enforcement choice happens after the seller's behavior and after platform revenue is at stake.

First-principles solution: Do not begin by trusting the rule announcement. Move to the later enforcement point and ask whether banning is still better for the platform than forgiving. If enforcement is costly and the platform wants the seller's fees, the ban may not be credible unless reputation, legal rules, or automated enforcement changes the later payoff.

Math move: Use backward induction. Compare platform payoff from ban versus forgive at the later node, then feed that continuation value back into the seller's current choice. The seller is deterred only if fake-review gain today is smaller than the expected future loss from credible enforcement.

Assumption check: The key assumptions are observation and commitment. Can the platform detect fake reviews with enough confidence, and will it actually enforce after detection? If either fails, the published rule is not enough to prove deterrence.

Evidence bridge: The extensive-form and backward-induction records support reading the situation as a tree. The subgame-perfect record supports the later-node credibility check, and the repeated-game records explain when future punishment can discipline present temptation.

Transfer lesson: In new papers, treat a policy threat as a strategic object, not as a sentence. The threat works only when the future actor's later incentives and information make enforcement believable.

Lecture pattern mapped: The lecture pattern is the entry-deterrence tree: an early threat is tested by a later best-response check. In the platform version, the incumbent becomes the platform, the entrant becomes the seller deciding whether to violate, and the costly price war becomes costly enforcement. The same primitive travels because both cases ask whether a future punishment is still attractive after the triggering action has already happened.

Step-by-step transfer: First draw the timing: seller acts, signal arrives, platform chooses enforcement. Second write the platform's later payoff from ban and forgive, including fees, legal risk, user trust, and future deterrence. Third convert that later choice into the seller's expected future loss. Only then compare the seller's fake-review gain against the credible expected punishment.

Where transfer breaks: The transfer breaks if enforcement is automatic, if regulation forces the platform to ban, if fake reviews are never observed, or if mistaken signals are common enough that enforcement punishes honest sellers. It also breaks if sellers care more about short-run exit than future access. In those cases the simple credibility tree needs monitoring, error, and repeated-market terms.

Worked transfer example

AI Pricing Bots: when a stable price may be equilibrium, collusion, or a missing action

New situation: Two firms use pricing algorithms that observe each other's prices daily. Prices rise and remain high even though neither firm signs an explicit agreement.

Model construction: Start by separating the firms, the algorithms, and the repeated price choices. If the algorithms are fixed tools, the firms may be the players; if algorithms update strategically, the model must say how their rules choose prices after histories.

First-principles solution: The high price is not explained by saying algorithms collude. Ask whether each firm would gain from undercutting today, whether the undercut would be observed, and whether future pricing would punish it strongly enough. Stability comes from the comparison between today's temptation and tomorrow's discipline.

Math move: Use a repeated-game incentive inequality. Cooperation survives when the discounted value of continued high prices is at least as large as the payoff from undercutting once plus the discounted value after punishment.

Assumption check: The dangerous assumptions are monitoring, action set, and payoff completeness. Can rivals detect undercutting, can firms choose alternative algorithm rules, and do payoffs include regulatory penalties or reputation costs?

Evidence bridge: The implicit-cartel, folk-theorem, and one-shot-deviation records support the repeated-incentive reading. The proof-sketch and assumption-audit cards explain why repetition itself is not enough.

Transfer lesson: When a paper reports stable high prices, ask what mechanism turns future behavior into a current incentive. Without monitoring and credible punishment, high prices are a pattern to explain, not a solved equilibrium claim.

Lecture pattern mapped: The lecture pattern is repeated cooperation under a temptation to deviate. In the pricing-bot version, cooperation is the high-price path, deviation is an undercut, and punishment is the algorithmic response after the undercut is detected. The mathematical primitive travels because the present action is disciplined by a future continuation value, not because algorithms have intentions.

Step-by-step transfer: First decide who the players are: firms, algorithms, or designers of algorithms. Second list the actual price-setting moves available, including changing the algorithm itself. Third write the one-period gain from undercutting and the future loss after punishment. Fourth check whether the undercut is observed and whether the punishment strategy remains a best response for the firms that carry it out. Fifth separate tacit coordination from explicit communication, because the legal and strategic interpretations differ.

Where transfer breaks: The transfer breaks if prices rise because of shared costs, demand shocks, capacity limits, or a platform rule rather than strategic punishment. It also breaks if algorithms cannot observe rivals, if firms can secretly change algorithm parameters, or if antitrust penalties dominate the pricing payoff. In those cases stable prices need a different model before being called repeated-game discipline. A serious transfer should explain what evidence distinguishes punishment from parallel response to the same market conditions.

Worked transfer example

Creator Badge: when a costly badge separates quality from imitation

New situation: A creator platform sells a verified badge. High-quality creators gain repeat subscribers from the badge, while low-quality creators gain a short-term boost but face refunds and poor reviews.

Model construction: Model hidden creator type, badge purchase, viewer belief, and viewer subscription choice. The badge is not proof by itself; it is an observed action whose meaning depends on which type wants to buy it.

First-principles solution: Ask whether high-quality and low-quality creators have different incentives to buy the badge. If the badge is cheap for everyone, it pools types and teaches viewers little. If the future benefit is high for good creators and refund cost is high for bad creators, the badge can separate types.

Math move: Use Bayes rule for viewer belief after observing the badge and incentive constraints for creator choices. A separating outcome needs the high type to prefer buying and the low type to prefer not imitating.

Assumption check: The important assumptions are belief source, payoff completeness, and publicness of the signal. Viewers must observe the badge, believe it is linked to type behavior, and the payoff model must include future reviews or refunds that make imitation costly.

Evidence bridge: The Bayesian-games, types-and-beliefs, perfect-Bayesian-equilibrium, and signaling records support the hidden-type reading. The proof sketch explains why evidence changes beliefs only through type likelihoods.

Transfer lesson: When a paper treats a badge, certificate, warranty, or credential as information, ask who can imitate it and why imitation is unattractive for the wrong type.

Lecture pattern mapped: The lecture pattern is signaling with hidden type. In the badge version, car quality becomes creator quality, the warranty becomes the verified badge, and repair cost becomes refund, churn, or review cost. The pattern transfers because the visible action has meaning only if different hidden types face different payoff consequences from taking that action.

Step-by-step transfer: First name the types and what viewers cannot directly observe. Second compute why each type would or would not buy the badge. Third ask what viewers believe after seeing the badge and after not seeing it. Fourth check that the viewer response makes badge purchase worthwhile for the high-quality type while leaving imitation unattractive for the low-quality type. Fifth ask whether the platform's own badge-selling incentive weakens the signal.

Where transfer breaks: The transfer breaks if the platform sells the badge cheaply to everyone, if viewers ignore it, if low-quality creators can collect the short-term boost and leave, or if refunds and poor reviews do not arrive quickly enough to matter. It also breaks if the badge certifies payment rather than quality, because then the signal reveals willingness to pay, not creator type. If the platform profits from selling badges to all types, the certifier's incentive must be modeled too.

Cloud Spot Auction: when format labels hide allocation and payment rules

New situation: A cloud provider sells spare compute through a spot market. Users submit bids, but the provider also considers reliability scores and may charge a price different from the winning bid.

Model construction: Separate the provider's design problem from each user's bidding problem. The provider chooses allocation and payment rules. Each user compares the payoff from bidding truthfully, shading, or changing demand under uncertainty about competitors.

First-principles solution: Do not compare the mechanism by name. Ask who gets compute, what they pay, how reliability scores enter, and whether a user can improve by changing the report. The core question is whether the rule makes the desired report or behavior personally attractive.

Math move: Use expected utility and incentive constraints. Expected payment combines chance of winning with price paid if winning. Truthful bidding requires that the payoff from the truthful report is at least as high as the payoff from each useful misreport.

Assumption check: Check the auction environment: private versus common values, risk attitudes, budget constraints, bidder asymmetry, and whether reliability scores are manipulable. Revenue-equivalence style reasoning only travels when its assumptions travel.

Evidence bridge: The auction, revenue-equivalence, and ad-auction evidence records support separating allocation from payment. The auction assumption audit explains why theorem labels fail when the environment changes.

Transfer lesson: When reading market-design papers, translate the format into allocation probability, payment rule, and deviation check before trusting claims about truthfulness, revenue, or efficiency.

Lecture pattern mapped: The lecture pattern is auction design as allocation plus payment plus incentive checks. In the cloud version, the object is compute capacity, the bidder's value is job value or deadline value, and reliability scores are extra rule inputs. The pattern transfers because the user still compares reports by expected payoff, even when the market label no longer matches a textbook auction exactly.

Step-by-step transfer: First identify what counts as winning: receiving capacity, priority, duration, or reliability. Second write what the user pays and when payment occurs. Third list the useful deviations: lower bid, inflated reliability, split demand, delayed job, or exit to another provider. Fourth ask whether the intended report beats each deviation under the actual uncertainty users face. Fifth check whether the provider can revise allocation after bids arrive, because discretion changes the mechanism.

Where transfer breaks: The transfer breaks if reliability scores are manipulable, if users have hard budgets, if values are common because outages affect everyone, or if the provider can ration outside the announced rule. Revenue-equivalence reasoning also breaks if users are risk averse or asymmetric. The mechanism then needs its own incentive proof instead of borrowing the auction theorem label. The transfer also weakens when users care about deadline failure more than average price.

Worked transfer example

Safety Announcement: when publicness changes behavior without changing the fact

New situation: Engineers on several teams privately learn that a deployment may be unsafe. Later, leadership posts the same warning publicly in a channel everyone can see.

Model construction: Model the teams as decision makers who choose whether to pause, continue, or wait for others. The public post is a change in information structure, not necessarily a change in the underlying safety fact.

First-principles solution: The public post can matter because each team now knows that the others saw the warning too. If pausing is costly unless others also pause, private knowledge may not coordinate behavior. Public knowledge can create the shared confidence needed for aligned action.

Math move: Use knowledge recursion. The issue is not the probability of the safety problem alone; it is belief about other teams' beliefs. Common knowledge represents the repeated layers: everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, and so on.

Assumption check: Check whether the announcement is truly public, whether teams interpret it the same way, and whether incentives are aligned. If leadership has incentives to exaggerate or hide information, cheap-talk concerns enter.

Evidence bridge: The common-knowledge, cheap-talk, rationalizability, and Nash records support the shared-awareness reading. The publicness assumption audit separates public signals from private messages and cheap talk.

Transfer lesson: When a paper says information was disclosed, ask whether disclosure created private awareness, public awareness, credible communication, or all three. Those are different strategic objects because each one changes a different part of the reasoning chain: what I know, what I think you know, and whether I expect you to act on it.

Lecture pattern mapped: The lecture pattern is common knowledge rather than private information. In the safety version, the public policy fact becomes the deployment warning, and the coordinated action becomes pausing or escalating. The pattern transfers because each team may need confidence that other teams also saw the warning before bearing the cost of slowing deployment.

Step-by-step transfer: First distinguish the safety fact from the communication event. Second ask what each team knew privately before the post. Third ask what the public channel lets each team infer about everyone else's knowledge. Fourth check incentives: if leadership benefits from exaggeration or suppression, the public post may create shared awareness without creating credible content. Fifth name the coordinated action, such as pausing rollout, escalating review, or refusing approval.

Where transfer breaks: The transfer breaks if the post is not seen by all teams, if some teams read it as advisory while others read it as mandatory, or if pausing has team-specific costs that the message does not reveal. It also breaks if leadership's incentives are misaligned, because cheap talk can be public without being trusted. A strong transfer should say whether the message changes beliefs, incentives, or both.

Security Randomization: when unpredictability is the stable plan

New situation: A security team can inspect systems at predictable times or random times. Attackers choose when to attempt intrusion after studying the inspection pattern, so a schedule that looks efficient internally may become a map for the opponent.

Model construction: Model security and attacker as strategic players. Security chooses an inspection pattern; attackers choose attack timing. If predictable inspection creates an exploitable gap, the relevant model may need mixed strategies rather than one fixed action, because the choice being protected is the opponent's expectation.

First-principles solution: Randomization is not indecision. It is a way to prevent the attacker from exploiting a known pattern. The inspection probabilities should make the attacker unable to gain by moving attacks to one obvious window, so the attacker faces no clean weak point to chase.

Math move: Use mixed-strategy logic: choose probabilities that equalize the attacker's relevant expected payoffs across attack times. Security's probability is determined by the attacker's comparison because security is shaping the attacker's incentives, not expressing a private mood about which time feels likely.

Assumption check: Check the action set and payoff mapping. Are all attack times included, are inspection costs modeled, and does the attacker actually observe enough history to exploit predictability? If the attacker cannot learn the pattern, mixing may be unnecessary; if some attacks are missing, the equalization is solving the wrong problem.

Evidence bridge: The mixed-strategy, minimax, expected-utility, and Nash evidence records support the randomization-as-incentive reading. The decoder warns against confusing mixing with personal uncertainty, and the Nash material keeps the focus on a stable pair of responses rather than a one-sided security rule.

Transfer lesson: When a paper uses randomization, ask whose behavior the randomness is meant to discipline. Often the probability is chosen to change the other side's best-response calculation, which means the real claim is about incentives under observation, not about randomness as decoration.

Lecture pattern mapped: The lecture pattern is mixed strategy as an anti-exploitation device. In the security version, pure actions are inspection times, the opponent's responses are attack windows, and the payoff from a schedule depends on whether the attacker can predict it. The pattern transfers because the defender's probability is chosen to shape the attacker's payoff comparison.

Step-by-step transfer: First list the inspection windows and attack windows. Second write the attacker's expected payoff from each attack time under a candidate inspection mix. Third choose inspection probabilities that remove the attacker's obvious best target, if feasible. Fourth check the defender's own costs and whether the random schedule can actually be committed to and hidden from attackers.

Where transfer breaks: The transfer breaks if attackers cannot observe patterns, because randomization may be unnecessary, or if attackers observe the random draw before acting, because randomization no longer protects uncertainty. It also breaks if some attack windows were omitted, if inspection costs vary sharply, or if the defender cannot tolerate the risk created by deliberately low-inspection periods.
