Mathematical Primitives

Preference

A way to say which outcome a person would rather have.

Everyday Setup

Imagine choosing between two concrete outcomes: more money with more risk, less money with less risk, agreement now, or a chance of a better agreement later. Preference is the ordered list hiding behind that choice.

Plain-Language Principle

Preference is the course’s way of saying that a person can compare outcomes before any strategic machinery starts. The primitive matters because a game cannot explain choice unless there is some stable sense in which one outcome is better for that player than another.

Symbol By Symbol

x and y are possible outcomes; u assigns each outcome a number; the larger number records the option the person ranks higher.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is that choice has to happen before strategy can be analyzed. If the model cannot say which outcome a player would rather have, then later words like payoff, incentive, or deviation have no anchor. Preference is the smallest object that turns desire into an inspectable ordering.

Worked Micro-Case

Suppose a bidder would rather win an item at a low price than win it at a high price, and would rather lose than win while paying above value. Those rankings are the primitive. The auction model can later attach numbers, but the ordering is what tells the model what counts as better.

Where It Appears

Preferences appear first in the opening choice lecture, then reappear whenever payoffs, utilities, outside options, bidder values, or expected utilities are used as inputs to strategic reasoning.

Why This Primitive Matters

It makes later payoff comparisons meaningful without forcing the course to measure inner happiness directly. The math only needs the ranking behavior that is relevant for choice.

Transfer Test

When meeting a new model, ask what outcomes the actor is being asked to rank. If the model jumps straight to a payoff number, translate it back into the ranking it is supposed to preserve. If that ranking is unclear or unstable, the rest of the strategic analysis is already fragile.

Concept Pages Using This Primitive

PreferencesUtilityExpected Utility

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The common misuse is treating the utility representation as a literal psychological ruler. In this course, different numerical representations can describe the same ordering unless uncertainty or expected utility requires stronger comparisons.

Payoff Mapping

A table that says what each person gets from each combination of choices.

Everyday Setup

A firm’s profit depends on its own price and a rival’s price; a bargainer’s payoff depends on both the offer and the response; an auction payoff depends on the bid, rivals’ bids, value, and payment rule.

Plain-Language Principle

Payoff mapping says that one person’s result is produced by the whole situation, not by that person’s action alone. A game table or tree is a compact machine that turns everyone’s choices into each player’s consequence.

Symbol By Symbol

i is one player; a_i is that player's action; a_-i means everyone else's actions; u_i is the payoff assigned to the whole action profile.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is interaction. In ordinary decision-making, an action can be judged by its own consequence. In a game, the consequence is produced by several choices at once, so the model needs a map from the whole situation to each person's payoff.

Worked Micro-Case

If two firms both choose prices, one firm's profit from a high price depends on whether the rival also stays high or undercuts. The same action can be profitable in one cell and damaging in another. The payoff map keeps those cell-by-cell consequences visible.

Where It Appears

Payoff mapping appears in strategic-form games, extensive-form games, bargaining, ad auctions, and any lecture that asks whether a deviation changes a player’s payoff.

Why This Primitive Matters

It prevents the analyst from evaluating actions as isolated moves. Once the payoff map is written down, best responses, dominance, equilibrium, and mechanism design have something precise to inspect.

Transfer Test

When reading a strategic model, locate the sentence or table that says how outcomes are produced from everyone’s actions. Then ask what has been omitted: timing, information, outside options, future punishments, or costs. Missing pieces in the payoff map become hidden assumptions in every later result.

Concept Pages Using This Primitive

Strategic Form GamesBargainingImperfect CompetitionAd Auctions

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

A payoff map can smuggle in the conclusion if the modeler omits costs, outside options, information, or future consequences. The table is only as honest as the situation it represents.

Best Response

The choice that works best given what someone believes others will do.

Everyday Setup

If a rival sets a high price, one response may be best; if the rival cuts price, another response may be best. The action is not universally good; it is good against a specified situation.

Plain-Language Principle

Best response is the one-player optimization step inside game theory, but in everyday words it means: given what I think the others are doing, what should I do?

Symbol By Symbol

argmax means choose the action a that makes player i's payoff as large as possible when others choose a_-i.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is conditional choice. A move is not good in empty space; it is good after the player forms an expectation about the environment. Best response exists because the model must separate the action chosen from the condition under which it is chosen.

Worked Micro-Case

A store may cut price if the rival keeps price high, but keep price high if both firms are avoiding a price war. Neither action is simply best. Each is best against a different rival behavior, which is why the condition belongs inside the primitive.

Where It Appears

Best response appears in rationalizability, Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, minimax reasoning, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, and the deviation checks behind dynamic concepts.

Why This Primitive Matters

It is the local test behind nearly every solution concept. Equilibrium is built by making many best-response checks hold at the same time.

Transfer Test

Whenever a paper says an action is optimal, ask: optimal against what? Name the rival action, belief, type, or history being held fixed. If the condition changes and the action no longer wins, the result is a conditional best response, not a universal recommendation. A good transfer reading should also identify the choice set being searched; if some actions are omitted, the best-response claim is only relative to that restricted menu.

Concept Pages Using This Primitive

Best ResponseMinimaxImperfect Competition

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is naming a best response without naming the belief, opponent action, or information condition. That hides the actual assumption doing the work.

Mutual Consistency

A situation where each person's plan makes sense given everyone else's plan.

Everyday Setup

A traffic merge, price competition, or bidding situation can sound stable until one participant notices a better move. Mutual consistency means that no participant has that private reason to switch.

Plain-Language Principle

Mutual consistency turns a collection of individual choices into a stable strategic prediction. Everyone’s plan has to make sense given everyone else’s plan.

Symbol By Symbol

For each player i, their chosen action a_i must belong to the set of best responses against the other players' actions.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is circular dependence. My best move depends on yours, and yours depends on mine. A prediction has to close that loop without leaving one player wishing to revise after seeing the proposed outcome.

Worked Micro-Case

In a prisoner’s dilemma, mutual defection can be stable even though both players would prefer mutual cooperation. Each player audits the outcome from their own unilateral-deviation point of view. The bad social result survives because switching alone is worse under the modeled payoffs. This is why equilibrium can explain a stuck situation without praising it.

Where It Appears

Mutual consistency appears in Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, zero-sum games, subgame perfection, infinitely repeated games, folk theorem logic, and Bayesian Nash equilibrium.

Why This Primitive Matters

It is the reason equilibrium is a disciplined prediction rather than a story. The concept asks every player to audit the proposed outcome from their own incentive point of view.

Transfer Test

When a model claims equilibrium, perform the one-person switch test. Hold everyone else fixed and ask whether a player can improve. Then repeat for every player or type. If the answer is no, the profile has mutual consistency, even if it is inefficient or unattractive. If the model is dynamic or has hidden information, ask whether the same consistency test is being refined rather than abandoned.

Concept Pages Using This Primitive

Nash EquilibriumMixed StrategiesZero-Sum GamesSubgame-Perfect Nash EquilibriumFolk TheoremBayesian Nash Equilibrium

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is reading equilibrium as good, fair, optimal for society, or morally desirable. It only says that unilateral deviation is not profitable under the modeled conditions.

Pruning

Remove choices that are never worth taking, then reason with the smaller problem.

Everyday Setup

If one route is slower no matter what traffic does, or one bid is worse no matter how rivals bid, a careful decision-maker should not keep treating it as live.

Plain-Language Principle

Pruning removes actions that cannot be justified before solving the whole game. It is the mathematical version of clearing away choices that are never worth taking.

Symbol By Symbol

The inequality checks every possible action by others; dominance means one strategy is never worse and sometimes better.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is attention. A game can list many actions, but some actions have no defensible reason to remain once payoffs are compared across contingencies. Pruning exists to remove choices that cannot survive even before a full prediction is built.

Worked Micro-Case

If a bidder strategy pays less than another strategy whether rivals bid low, medium, or high, keeping it alive only clutters the analysis. But if it wins under one rival case, deletion is no longer dominance; the analyst has moved into beliefs and best responses.

Where It Appears

Pruning appears in dominance, iterated deletion, rationalizability, and as background for equilibrium reasoning because eliminated strategies cannot be serious best responses.

Why This Primitive Matters

It reduces strategic clutter and teaches students that not every listed action deserves equal attention. It also shows how rationality assumptions can have observable consequences before equilibrium is imposed.

Transfer Test

Before deleting an action, check every relevant opponent action or state. If the action loses everywhere, prune it. If it loses only under the scenario you expect, keep it and say what belief makes it unattractive. That distinction prevents a hunch from masquerading as proof. Also ask whether the deletion is strict or weak; weak deletion can change the remaining problem in ways that strict deletion usually does not.

Concept Pages Using This Primitive

DominanceIterated Deletion

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is deleting too aggressively, especially with weak dominance or when beliefs matter. A deletion rule can change the remaining game, so the strength and order of the rule must be explicit.

Backward Recursion

Solve the future first, then use that answer to decide what should happen earlier.

Everyday Setup

A threat to punish later is persuasive only if punishment will still be worth doing later. A buyer’s current offer depends on what both sides expect at the next bargaining step.

Plain-Language Principle

Backward recursion says that later incentives determine earlier meaning. To understand a promise or threat now, solve what the player will want when the promised moment arrives.

Symbol By Symbol

V is continuation value; at each decision point the player chooses the next branch with the best continuation value.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is time. Earlier choices are shaped by what later selves or later players will actually want to do. Backward recursion exists because future incentives determine whether present threats, promises, and expectations are credible.

Worked Micro-Case

A seller may threaten to reject a low offer, but if rejection later leaves the seller with nothing, the threat may not survive the later decision node. Solving the end first tells the buyer whether the early threat changes the current bargaining problem. The same logic appears in finite repetition: the last round can decide what earlier rounds mean.

Where It Appears

Backward recursion appears in extensive-form games, backward induction, subgame perfection, one-shot deviation, bargaining, finitely repeated games, and perfect Bayesian equilibrium.

Why This Primitive Matters

It gives sequential games their discipline. Instead of accepting announced plans, the course tests each continuation point and pulls those continuation values back to the start.

Transfer Test

When a dynamic model includes a threat or promise, jump to the moment when it must be carried out. Ask what the player wants at that later point, ignoring earlier words. If the action is not best there, the model needs commitment, reputation, or a different tree. Then check whether the player observes enough history to know which later node they are actually in.

Concept Pages Using This Primitive

Extensive Form GamesBackward InductionSubgame-Perfect Nash EquilibriumOne-Shot Deviation PrincipleFinitely Repeated GamesPerfect Bayesian Equilibrium

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is solving backward through a tree that leaves out information, uncertainty, or renegotiation. Backward logic is powerful only after the tree is an honest representation.

Discounting

Future rewards count, but usually count less than rewards right now.

Everyday Setup

A firm may avoid undercutting today if it values future high prices enough. A bargainer may accept less now if waiting is costly or reject now if future surplus is valuable.

Plain-Language Principle

Discounting is the course’s way to compare now with later. Future payoffs matter, but the discount factor says how much weight the future has relative to the present.

Symbol By Symbol

u_t is payoff in period t; delta between 0 and 1 controls how strongly the future matters.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is distance in time. A payoff tomorrow can discipline behavior today only if the player cares enough about tomorrow. Discounting exists to turn patience into a number that can be compared against present temptation.

Worked Micro-Case

A cartel member can cut price today and gain immediately, but may trigger future price punishment. Cooperation survives only if the discounted future loss is larger than the one-time gain. The discount factor is what decides whether the future is heavy enough. If customers, rivals, or regulators make punishment slow or uncertain, the future becomes lighter in the current calculation.

Where It Appears

Discounting appears in bargaining, infinitely repeated games, folk theorem arguments, implicit cartels, and the repeated-game parts of cooperation.

Why This Primitive Matters

It turns patience into a mathematical object. The survival of cooperation often depends on whether discounted future punishment is larger than immediate temptation.

Transfer Test

For any repeated-game claim, write the immediate gain from deviation and the future loss from punishment. Then apply the discount factor. If the discounted future loss is too small, repetition is not doing the work the author says it is doing. Also check monitoring: a future punishment cannot discipline present behavior if players cannot tell that a deviation occurred. Finally, check whether the end date is known, because a known last period can strip future punishment of force.

Concept Pages Using This Primitive

BargainingInfinitely Repeated GamesFolk TheoremImplicit Cartels

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is treating future discipline as automatic. If players are impatient, cannot observe cheating, or know the game ends, the discounting inequality can fail.

Belief

A probability story about what kind of situation or opponent you are facing.

Everyday Setup

A bidder estimates rivals’ values, a receiver estimates a sender’s type, and a player in a Bayesian game estimates which payoff situation they are facing.

Plain-Language Principle

Belief is a probability story about hidden facts. It lets a player choose while admitting that they do not know the opponent’s type, value, history, or information.

Symbol By Symbol

theta is the unknown type or state; p(theta) is its probability; expected utility averages payoffs across possibilities.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is hidden structure. Players often choose without knowing values, costs, types, histories, or motives. Belief exists because uncertainty has to be inside the model, not left as a vague background cloud.

Worked Micro-Case

A buyer sees a seller reject an offer. That rejection may suggest high cost, strong bargaining position, or strategic posturing. A belief assigns weights to these possibilities, and later equilibrium reasoning asks whether those weights are consistent with the behavior that produced the signal. The action is therefore both a move in the game and evidence about hidden structure.

Where It Appears

Belief appears in expected utility, rationalizability, mixed strategies, Bayesian games, types and beliefs, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, auctions, PBE, signaling, and cheap talk.

Why This Primitive Matters

It keeps uncertainty inside the model instead of pretending it away. Once beliefs are explicit, the course can ask whether actions are optimal under those beliefs and whether observations should update them.

Transfer Test

When a model invokes belief, ask what the belief is over, where it came from, and how observations update it. If the belief can be changed freely to rescue any conclusion, it is no longer evidence discipline; it is decoration. In dynamic settings, also ask whether the observation is on the predicted path, because off-path beliefs need special care. A strong belief argument should say what evidence would move the belief and what evidence would leave it mostly unchanged.

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is using beliefs as arbitrary story glue. In dynamic games especially, beliefs must be tied to information, Bayes rule where possible, and the incentives that produced observed behavior.

Incentive Compatibility

Design rules so people want to reveal or do what the rule needs them to reveal or do.

Everyday Setup

A bidder may shade a bid, a sender may lie, a firm may cheat on a cartel, and a platform participant may respond to ranking rules. The question is whether the rules make the intended action the best move.

Plain-Language Principle

Incentive compatibility asks whether the rule makes desired behavior privately worthwhile. A mechanism cannot simply request honesty, cooperation, or truthful bidding; it must make those actions attractive.

Symbol By Symbol

The inequality says a person should not gain by replacing the truthful action with another report.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is private advantage. A rule may want people to reveal information, keep cooperating, bid honestly, or send useful messages, but participants compare the requested behavior against profitable alternatives. Incentive compatibility exists because a rule must survive self-interest.

Worked Micro-Case

In an auction, a platform may want bidders to reveal values, but a bidder may gain by shading the bid. The mechanism has to make the desired report or action at least as good as the manipulation. Otherwise the rule describes a wish, not a strategic outcome. The same check applies to cartel punishment, cheap talk, and signaling: desired behavior must be privately worth choosing.

Where It Appears

Incentive compatibility appears in implicit cartels, auctions, revenue equivalence, ad auctions, signaling, and cheap talk, with related logic in folk-theorem punishment constraints.

Why This Primitive Matters

It is the design version of game theory. Instead of assuming people do what the designer wants, the course checks whether strategic self-interest leads there.

Transfer Test

When reading a mechanism, identify the intended behavior and then list the tempting lies, deviations, or manipulations. Compare payoffs for each. If any alternative pays more, the design is not incentive compatible under the stated assumptions. Then ask whether the information needed to enforce the rule is actually observed by the participants or platform.

Implicit CartelsScreening And Adverse SelectionAuctionsRevenue EquivalenceAd AuctionsSignalingCheap Talk

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is designing by intention rather than incentives. If misreporting, deviation, or manipulation pays more, the mechanism fails no matter how elegant the rule looks.

Knowledge Recursion

Not only knowing a fact, but knowing that others know it, and so on.

Everyday Setup

A public announcement can coordinate people because each person knows that everyone else heard it. A private message may give the same fact to one person without creating that shared confidence.

Plain-Language Principle

Knowledge recursion captures the difference between a fact being true, people knowing it, and people knowing that others know it. Coordination often needs the higher levels, not just the first one.

Symbol By Symbol

E is the event; K means known by the players; repeated K's represent higher-order knowledge.

First-Principles Pressure

The pressure is coordinated confidence. Sometimes knowing a fact is not enough; a player also needs confidence that others know it, and confidence that others know that everyone knows it. Knowledge recursion exists because coordination can depend on those higher layers.

Worked Micro-Case

Two investors may both receive similar private warnings, but each may wonder whether the other received them too. A public announcement can change behavior even if it adds little new factual content, because it creates shared awareness that the warning is shared. The informational payload is not only the fact; it is the publicness of the fact.

Where It Appears

Knowledge recursion appears most directly in common knowledge, and indirectly in iterated deletion, rationalizability, equilibrium selection, and communication because players reason about others’ reasoning.

Why This Primitive Matters

It explains why publicness can be strategically powerful. Some outcomes require not only information but shared awareness that the information is shared.

Transfer Test

When a coordination model uses information, ask whether the fact is private, mutual, public, or common knowledge. If action requires confidence about others' confidence, a private signal may be too weak even when every player individually knows the same fact. Then ask whether the model needs one level of knowledge or an unbounded recursive chain. In a new paper, look for public announcements, shared protocols, or visible histories; those are often attempts to create the recursive confidence the result needs.

Concept Pages Using This Primitive

Common Knowledge

Why Misuse Breaks The Model

The misuse is treating widespread knowledge as common knowledge. If players are unsure whether others saw the same fact, the recursive chain can break and coordination may fail.

Derivation Cards

Core equations unpacked as plain-language operations.

pruning

Dominance Inequality

A player is comparing two possible moves before making any guess about what the others will do. If move s does at least as well as move t in every relevant case, and does better in at least one case, move t has no independent reason to stay in the analysis.

u_i(s, a_-i) >= u_i(t, a_-i) for every a_-i, and > for at least one a_-i

Derivation In Plain English

  1. Choose two strategies for the same player: the candidate keeper s and the candidate loser t.
  2. Hold one possible set of opponent actions fixed and compare the payoffs from s and t.
  3. Repeat that comparison for every possible opponent action covered by the game.
  4. If s is never worse and sometimes strictly better, t is dominated and can be pruned before solving the rest of the game.

Symbol By Symbol

u_i is player i's payoff. s and t are two strategies available to that same player. a_-i means the actions chosen by everyone else. The phrase for every a_-i is the important part: the comparison must survive all opponent actions, not only the one that seems likely.

Why This Relation Matters

This relation explains why dominance is stronger than a hunch. It lets the course delete a strategy without first solving beliefs or equilibrium, because no belief about the opponent can rescue a strategy that loses across the whole comparison.

Common Misread

The common mistake is deleting a strategy because it performs badly in the most likely case. That is not dominance. Dominance requires the inequality to hold across every case named by the model.

belief

Expected Utility

A player chooses before knowing which state of the world is true. Instead of pretending one state is certain, the model weighs each possible payoff by how likely that state seems.

E[u(a, theta)] = sum_theta p(theta) u(a, theta)

Derivation In Plain English

  1. List the possible hidden states, such as low value and high value.
  2. Attach a probability to each state. These probabilities must add up to one.
  3. Compute the payoff from the action in each state.
  4. Multiply each payoff by its probability and add the pieces.

Symbol By Symbol

a is the action being evaluated. theta is the unknown state or type. p(theta) is the probability of that state. u(a, theta) is the payoff if action a is chosen and theta is true. The summation adds the probability-weighted payoffs.

Why This Relation Matters

This is the bridge from uncertainty to choice. Bayesian games, auctions, and screening problems all need a way to compare actions before hidden information is revealed.

Common Misread

The formula is not saying the average state actually happens. It is a decision rule for choosing under uncertainty.

best response

Best Response

A move is not good by itself. It is good against some belief or opponent action. Best response asks what player i should do after holding the rest of the situation fixed.

BR_i(a_-i) = argmax_a u_i(a, a_-i)

Derivation In Plain English

  1. Fix what the other players are doing.
  2. Try each available action for player i.
  3. Compute player i's payoff for each candidate action.
  4. Keep the action or actions with the highest payoff.

Symbol By Symbol

BR_i is player i's best-response set. a_-i means the actions of everyone except player i. argmax means the action that makes the payoff as large as possible. u_i is player i's payoff function.

Why This Relation Matters

Best response is the local engine behind Nash equilibrium, rationalizability, mixed strategies, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, and many deviation checks.

Common Misread

A best response is always conditional. If the opponent action or belief changes, the best response may change too.

mutual consistency

Nash Equilibrium

A proposed prediction must survive every player asking whether they alone would rather switch. Nash equilibrium is the point where all those one-person checks pass at once.

a_i in BR_i(a_-i) for every player i

Derivation In Plain English

  1. Start with a proposed action profile.
  2. For each player, hold the other players' actions fixed.
  3. Check whether the player's assigned action is a best response.
  4. If every player passes the check, the profile is a Nash equilibrium.

Symbol By Symbol

a_i is player i's chosen action. a_-i is everyone else's action. BR_i(a_-i) is the set of best responses against those other actions. The phrase for every player means no one is exempt from the deviation check.

Why This Relation Matters

It turns strategic prediction into a mutual consistency test. The result can be stable even when it is inefficient, unfair, or socially bad.

Common Misread

Equilibrium does not mean the best overall outcome. It means no single player wants to move alone under the modeled payoffs.

backward recursion

Backward Induction

A threat or promise matters only if it will still be attractive later. Backward induction solves later choices first and uses them to interpret earlier choices.

V(node) = max_action V(next node)

Derivation In Plain English

  1. Go to the final decision points in the game tree.
  2. At each final decision, choose the branch with the best payoff for the player moving there.
  3. Replace that decision point with the value of the chosen continuation.
  4. Move one step earlier and repeat until the start of the tree is reached.

Symbol By Symbol

V(node) is the value of arriving at a decision point. max_action means choose the action with the highest continuation value. next node is the future decision point or outcome reached by that action.

Why This Relation Matters

It separates credible future behavior from empty threats. Dynamic games, bargaining, finite repetition, and subgame perfection all depend on this recursive logic.

Common Misread

Solving backward is only as good as the tree. If the tree leaves out information, renegotiation, or hidden types, the backward calculation can be precise but misleading.

discounting

Discounted Payoff Sum

Repeated interaction asks whether future consequences can discipline today's temptation. The discount factor says how strongly the future counts today.

U = u_0 + delta u_1 + delta^2 u_2 + ...

Derivation In Plain English

  1. Write the payoff from the current period.
  2. Write the payoff from each future period.
  3. Multiply each future payoff by an extra power of delta for each step into the future.
  4. Add current and discounted future payoffs into one present-value comparison.

Symbol By Symbol

U is total discounted payoff. u_0 is payoff now. u_1 is next period's payoff. delta is between zero and one and measures patience. delta squared means a payoff two periods away receives two rounds of discounting.

Why This Relation Matters

Cooperation, cartel discipline, and bargaining delay all depend on whether discounted future gains or losses outweigh current temptation.

Common Misread

The formula does not make future punishment automatically powerful. If delta is small, future consequences may barely matter.

belief

Bayes Rule

After observing an action or signal, a player revises what they believe about hidden type. Bayes rule is the bookkeeping that turns prior belief plus observed evidence into posterior belief.

P(type | signal) = P(signal | type) P(type) / P(signal)

Derivation In Plain English

  1. Start with the prior probability of the type.
  2. Ask how likely the observed signal would be if that type were true.
  3. Multiply prior probability by signal likelihood.
  4. Normalize by the total probability of seeing that signal across all possible types.

Symbol By Symbol

P(type | signal) is the updated belief after seeing the signal. P(signal | type) is how likely that signal is for that type. P(type) is the prior belief. P(signal) rescales all type weights so the updated probabilities add to one.

Why This Relation Matters

PBE, signaling, screening, and Bayesian games need beliefs that respond to observations rather than floating freely.

Common Misread

Bayes rule does not tell you off-path beliefs when the observed signal has zero probability under the proposed strategy. That is why dynamic information games need careful belief discipline.

incentive compatibility

Auction Expected Payment

A bidder does not only ask what they pay if they win. They also ask how likely the bid is to win. The expected payment combines those two pieces.

expected payment = payment if win x probability of winning

Derivation In Plain English

  1. Pick a bid.
  2. Find what the bidder would pay if that bid wins under the auction rule.
  3. Find the probability that this bid wins against rival bids.
  4. Multiply the payment-if-win by the probability of winning.

Symbol By Symbol

The payment-if-win comes from the auction format. The probability of winning comes from beliefs about rival bids or values. Their product is the average payment before knowing whether this bid wins.

Why This Relation Matters

Revenue equivalence, bid shading, first-price auctions, and ad auctions all require separating the price paid from the chance of paying it.

Common Misread

A higher bid can raise both the chance of winning and the payment made when winning. The bidder's incentive depends on both effects, not one alone.

knowledge recursion

Common Knowledge Recursion

Coordination can require more than everyone privately knowing the same fact. Players may need to know that others know, and know that others know that others know.

E, KE, KKE, KKKE, ...

Derivation In Plain English

  1. Start with an event E.
  2. Add the statement that everyone knows E.
  3. Add the statement that everyone knows that everyone knows E.
  4. Continue the chain without stopping at any finite level.

Symbol By Symbol

E is the event. K is the knowledge operator. KE means everyone knows E. KKE means everyone knows that everyone knows E. The dots mean the chain continues indefinitely.

Why This Relation Matters

Public announcements, coordination, rationalizability, and equilibrium reasoning often depend on shared awareness rather than private information alone.

Common Misread

A fact being true or widely known is not enough. Common knowledge requires the recursive publicness that lets players reason about other players' reasoning.
