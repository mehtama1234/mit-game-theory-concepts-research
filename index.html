Start With Concepts

choice and payoff

Preferences

A rule that lets you write down which outcomes you like more than others — a scoreboard for how much you value each possible result.

Problem
Before anyone starts thinking strategically, we need a way to ask: which outcome does this person actually want? The catch is that outcomes are tangled up. One choice gives you more money but more risk. Another gives you less money but more free time. Before you can ask whether someone makes sense or does best for themselves, you need a way to say how they rank all these mixed-up options.
Math
A preference is a comparison rule over outcomes. You list each outcome and mark which ones rank above others. A utility function u(x) is a shortcut: instead of writing out all the rankings, you assign each outcome a number, and higher numbers mean ranks you like more. If outcome x gets a higher number than y, that means you prefer x to y — the numbers are just a way to write down the ranking.

choice and payoff

Utility

A number attached to each outcome that records how much someone wants it.

Problem
When several people each chase what is best for them, you need to know whether one person will prefer outcome A or outcome B. But you cannot write down a math formula using the words 'I like this more' — math works with numbers. So you have to turn 'A is better than B' into numbers you can actually use in a calculation.
Math
A preference is a ranking — 'I like pizza more than salad.' A utility function is a way to turn that ranking into numbers so math can handle it. If u is a utility function, then u(pizza) = 10 and u(salad) = 5 means 'pizza ranks higher.' The only rule is: a higher number must mean a higher ranking. The actual numbers are up to you — you could use 100 and 20 instead, and it works the same way for ordinary choice.

  • ev-utility-02: Lecture 2: Representation of Games

Expected Utility

When you face an uncertain choice, expected utility is the score you calculate by adding up (outcome score × its probability) for every possible result.

Problem
You often must decide before you know what will happen: will a bid win or lose? Will a risky investment pay off or lose money? Will someone turn out to be honest or dishonest? You need a way to compare choices when the future is uncertain.
Math
Your belief about the unknown is shown as a probability distribution—a list of possible states or types with a number (the probability) for each. The calculation is E[u(a, θ)] = Σ_θ p(θ) u(a, θ). In plain terms: θ is the unknown state or type; p(θ) is how likely you think it is; multiply each payoff by its probability and add them all up.

  • ev-expected_utility-02: Lecture 16: Bayesian Games

strategic dependence

Strategic Form Games

A table where you write down all the players, all the moves each one can make, and the score each player gets from every possible combination of moves.

Problem
Imagine you and your friend each need to choose something at the exact same moment, without knowing what the other chose. But once you both decide, what you get depends on both choices together. How do you show what's really going on? You need a table that captures the fact that nobody gets to see the other's choice before picking their own.
Math
A payoff function u_i(a_i, a_-i) assigns a score to player i. Here is what each symbol means: i is one player; a_i is that player's action; a_-i means all the other players' actions taken together; u_i is the number (payoff) that player i receives. The full action profile is (a_i, a_-i) — everyone's action at once — and that determines what score player i walks away with.

  • ev-strategic_form_games-02: Lecture 1: Introduction to Individual Decision-Making

time and credibility

Extensive Form Games

A game drawn as a tree diagram instead of a table, because the timing of moves and what each player knows at each moment matter.

Problem
In real conflicts, people move one at a time, not all at once. A choice made later can depend on what happened earlier. And sometimes you know more than the other person — or they know more than you. A flat table misses all of that.
Math
Backward induction starts at the end. At the final decision point, a player chooses the branch that gives the best score. Then move back one step: knowing what the later player will do, what should the earlier player choose? Keep going backward to the start. The formula is `V(node) = max_action V(next node)`, which means: the value of where you stand is the highest value you can reach by picking one of your moves.
  • ev-extensive_form_games-01: Lecture 2: Representation of Games
  • ev-extensive_form_games-02: Lecture 8: Backward Induction

strategic dependence

Dominance

A test to cross off moves that are worse than some other move in every possible situation your opponent faces you with.

Problem
In any game, some moves are just worse. They lose to another move no matter what the other person tries. But if you list all possible moves, the bad ones can hide the real choices that matter. Dominance lets you cross off the hopeless ones first.
Math
The math compares two moves by looking at what each one gives you against every possible opponent move. Write it as: move A dominates move B if your score from A is always at least as high as your score from B, and sometimes strictly higher. The inequality holds for every opponent choice; that is what makes it dominance.
