Jargon Decoder

A plain-language bridge for terms that appear in lectures, problem sets, and papers. Each card translates the term family into the everyday problem, the mathematical object, a reading test, and the confusion to avoid.

Jargon decoder

Utility, payoff, reward, objective, loss, welfare

Where the reader sees it: A paper says an agent maximizes utility, a firm has a payoff, a platform optimizes welfare, or a model minimizes loss.

Plain translation: Someone's wants have been turned into a score. The score is not the person; it is a measuring stick the model uses so different outcomes can be compared.

First-principles pressure: Choice needs comparison. If the actor cannot say which outcome is better, equal, or worse, there is no way to say which action is sensible. Numbers let the model compare outcomes, average risky futures, and check whether a different action would be preferred.

Mathematical object: A function such as u(outcome), payoff(action pair), or E[u(outcome)]. The function takes a possible result and returns a number; the expectation averages those numbers using probabilities before uncertainty is resolved.

Reading test: Ask whose score this is, what outcome features enter the score, and whether the score ranks outcomes or also measures meaningful size differences. Then ask whether the actor controls the score directly or only chooses an action that changes which outcome happens.

Common confusion: The common mistake is treating utility as happiness or moral value. In this course it is usually a compact representation of choice, and the model can be wrong if the chosen score leaves out a real pressure.

How to unpack it in a paper: Translate the score into three everyday pieces: who is choosing, what outcomes are being compared, and what the number is allowed to mean. Then ask whether the paper is using the score as a ranking, an expected value under uncertainty, a welfare target for a designer, or a loss that an algorithm reduces.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs vague talk about wanting, preferring, and improving. Instead of saying an actor wants a good outcome, the model specifies the scoreboard used to compare outcomes and then checks actions against that scoreboard.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when readers assume one score captures every value in the real setting. Welfare, utility, payoff, and loss can point to different actors and different purposes, so the paper’s objective may not be the social objective a reader cares about.

Why the word has to exist: The word exists because ordinary language says people want things, but a model needs a sharper object before it can compare choices. Utility and payoff are the bookkeeping device that lets the analyst ask whether changing an action changes the actor's score.

Math in plain sight: When you see u, payoff, reward, or loss, read it as a scoreboard. When you see an expectation, read it as averaging possible scoreboards before the uncertainty is revealed. The math matters because it separates ranking outcomes from predicting which outcome will actually occur.

Jargon decoder

Action, strategy, plan, policy, contingent plan

Where the reader sees it: A paper lists actions in a matrix, strategies in a game tree, or policies that tell an agent what to do after different histories.

Plain translation: An action is a move now. A strategy is a full plan for what the actor would do whenever the model says the actor might have to choose.

First-principles pressure: Other people need to reason about what you would do even at moments that may never arrive. A dynamic game cannot judge a threat, promise, or response by looking only at the path that actually happens; it needs the whole plan so future incentives are explicit.

Mathematical object: In a strategic-form game a strategy may be one row or column. In an extensive-form game it is a rule that chooses an action at each decision point belonging to the player. The object is larger than a single observed move.

Reading test: Ask whether the paper is modeling one immediate choice or a plan over many possible situations. If the claim depends on what happens after a surprise move, you need the strategy at off-path decision points too.

Common confusion: The trap is calling the observed action the whole strategy. That hides the counterfactual parts of the plan, which are exactly where credibility and subgame-perfect reasoning often live.

How to unpack it in a paper: Separate what happened from what would have happened. In a table, the strategy may be a single listed choice. In a tree or repeated setting, it is a rule for many possible decision points, including histories that the predicted play never reaches.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs the mistake of judging a dynamic model only by the visible path. A plan can look sensible on the path while containing unbelievable responses after surprises, so the full strategy is needed for credibility checks.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks if the model cannot defend what the actor observes at each decision point. A contingent plan is meaningful only after the information available at those contingencies has been specified.

Why the word has to exist: The word strategy exists because a one-time move is too small for many games. Other players need to reason about your response after surprises, refusals, delays, or new information. The full plan makes those hidden branches visible enough to test.

Math in plain sight: When a paper writes a strategy as a function from histories or information sets to actions, read it as an if-this-happens-then-do-that rule. The notation matters because it makes the off-path parts of a plan part of the object being judged. Always ask which histories share the same information, because a plan cannot condition on details the actor does not observe.

Jargon decoder

Best response, deviation, unilateral improvement

Where the reader sees it: A paper checks whether a player wants to deviate, computes best-response curves, or says no unilateral deviation is profitable.

Plain translation: Hold everyone else's behavior fixed and ask what this one actor would most like to do. It is a local test, not a statement about what is best for everyone.

First-principles pressure: In a strategic situation, an action is not good in isolation. Its value depends on what the other actors do. Best response exists because each participant faces a different local problem once the rest of the environment is treated as fixed.

Mathematical object: BR_i(a_-i) is the set of player i's best actions against the other players' actions. The subscript i names the actor, and a_-i means the actions of everyone except that actor.

Reading test: Ask what has been held fixed. If the answer changes when other players change their behavior, then the paper is using a best-response idea rather than a universal ranking of actions.

Common confusion: The common mistake is treating best response as a globally best action. It is only best against a specified outside behavior, and the same action can stop being best when the outside behavior changes.

How to unpack it in a paper: Find the fixed object before reading the optimization. The fixed object might be another player’s action, a belief about types, a price rule, or a continuation history. Then read the best response as conditional on that object.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs the phrase rational action. It forces the reader to ask rational against what, and it exposes the assumption that turns one action into the local winner.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when the paper quietly changes what is being held fixed. If the belief, rival move, information set, or action menu changes, the old best-response claim may no longer apply. It also breaks when the author calls a population average optimal; a best response belongs to a particular actor with a particular information condition.

Why the word has to exist: Best response exists because strategic choice is conditional. The same move can be smart against one rival action and foolish against another. The term forces the author to name the environment around the actor before calling a choice rational.

Math in plain sight: When you see BR_i(a_-i), read it as player i's best available replies after everyone else's actions are frozen. The subscript names whose local problem is being solved; the minus sign reminds you that the comparison depends on the rest of the players. If the paper changes beliefs, prices, or histories, rerun the local comparison instead of carrying over the old answer.

Jargon decoder

Equilibrium, fixed point, stable outcome, self-consistency

Where the reader sees it: A paper says an outcome is stable, an equilibrium exists, a fixed point is reached, or strategies are mutually consistent.

Plain translation: The proposed behavior survives everyone checking whether they personally want to move away from it. It is a no-one-wants-to-change test inside the model.

First-principles pressure: Strategic reasoning is circular: my best move depends on yours, and yours depends on mine. Equilibrium is the mathematical way to stop that circle at a pattern where all local best-response checks fit together.

Mathematical object: A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where each player's strategy is a best response to the others. A fixed point appears because the behavior predicted by the best-response rule matches the behavior being checked.

Reading test: Ask which deviations are allowed, what information each player has, and whether the equilibrium condition is checked only at the start or after every possible history.

Common confusion: Equilibrium does not mean good, fair, efficient, inevitable, or learned by real people. It means the modeled participants have no profitable unilateral deviation under the stated rules.

How to unpack it in a paper: Read equilibrium as a checklist, not a mood. Identify the candidate behavior, the allowed deviations, what each player knows, and whether the check is static, dynamic, Bayesian, or sequential. Then ask whether every player or type passes the relevant deviation test. If there are multiple equilibria, look for the extra selection story rather than pretending the word equilibrium selected one outcome by itself.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs loose claims of stability. A pattern is not stable because it sounds balanced; it is stable because the model cannot find a profitable allowed move away from it for any participant covered by the solution concept.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when the reader imports welfare or inevitability. Equilibrium can be inefficient, fragile, one of many, or behaviorally unrealistic if learning, mistakes, institutions, or omitted deviations matter. A careful reader should ask what empirical or institutional story would make this equilibrium the relevant one.

Why the word has to exist: Equilibrium exists because individual best-response checks can chase one another in a circle. The word names a point where the circle closes: each person is choosing well given the others, and the others are also choosing well given that person.

Math in plain sight: When a paper says a profile is an equilibrium, read the formula as many best-response tests stacked together. The fixed-point language means the behavior produced by the response rule is the same behavior that was put into the rule for checking. If there are several closing points, the math has not yet explained which one the world should select.

Jargon decoder

Dominance, dominated strategy, deletion, elimination, pruning

Where the reader sees it: A paper removes actions, says one option is dominated, iteratively deletes strategies, or narrows the possible set before solving for equilibrium.

Plain translation: Some option is never worth keeping because another option does at least as well in every relevant case and better in some case.

First-principles pressure: Reasoning can explode when each person has many possible actions and many beliefs about others. Dominance is a safe deletion rule: it removes choices that cannot be defended no matter what the other side does.

Mathematical object: A strategy is dominated when another strategy gives a payoff at least as high against every opponent action and strictly higher somewhere. Iterated deletion repeats that comparison after earlier deletions shrink the game.

Reading test: Ask whether the paper has checked all relevant opponent actions or only the action that seems likely. Dominance needs the stronger all-cases comparison; otherwise the paper is making a belief-based argument, not a dominance argument.

Common confusion: The common mistake is deleting a strategy because it seems unlikely or unattractive in one scenario. That is not dominance. Safe pruning requires the strategy to lose the comparison across the whole stated choice environment.

How to unpack it in a paper: Look for the quantifier. Dominance needs a comparison across every relevant opponent action or state, not just the scenario the author expects. If the claim uses iteration, check what was removed in each round and whether the rule is strict or weak.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs overlarge action spaces. It gives the analyst a justified way to remove choices before full equilibrium analysis, while keeping deletion separate from prediction.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when deletion depends on a belief, an omitted action, or weak dominance treated casually. In those cases the pruning may be order-sensitive or may require a different concept such as rationalizability. It also breaks if the payoff map is incomplete, because a missing contingency can be exactly where the supposedly dominated action is useful.

Why the word has to exist: Dominance exists because some choices are indefensible before beliefs about others even enter. If one option loses to another across every relevant case, keeping it only clutters the reasoning. The term marks a rare situation where deletion does not require predicting the opponent.

Math in plain sight: When you see a dominance inequality, read every opponent action as a separate column in a checklist. The candidate survivor must do at least as well in each column and better somewhere. The phrase for all is the heart of the math, not decoration. If a paper deletes choices round by round, track the order because weak deletion can depend on earlier pruning.

Jargon decoder

Mixed strategy, randomization, probability distribution, indifference

Where the reader sees it: A paper says players mix, randomize, use a probability distribution over actions, or make opponents indifferent.

Plain translation: The actor is not choosing one move for sure. The actor is choosing a lottery over moves, often because being predictable would be exploitable.

First-principles pressure: Some conflicts have no stable pure move. If each side can exploit a predictable choice, stability may require controlled unpredictability. The probabilities are chosen so the other side cannot gain by switching its own action.

Mathematical object: A mixed strategy assigns probabilities to actions. In many two-player examples, the mixing probability is found by making the opponent's relevant choices have equal expected payoff, so the opponent has no strict reason to prefer one over the other.

Reading test: Ask who is being made indifferent. If the paper chooses player A's mixing probability, that probability often comes from equalizing player B's payoffs, because A's randomization shapes B's incentives.

Common confusion: The trap is thinking the player is personally unsure or does not care. Mixing is a strategic commitment to probabilities; indifference is often a condition created in the opponent's payoff comparison.

How to unpack it in a paper: Ask whose probabilities are being chosen and whose payoffs are being equalized. In many mixed-equilibrium calculations, one player’s mixing rate is solved by making the other player indifferent among the actions they use. Then check whether unused actions earn no more than the used actions, because an omitted profitable pure action would break the proposed mixture.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs the assumption that stable behavior must be deterministic. In some games, any predictable pure action invites exploitation, so randomness becomes the disciplined object rather than noise.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when observed frequencies are treated as proof of intentional mixing. Data may reflect learning, mistakes, heterogeneity, or changing environments rather than an equilibrium distribution chosen by a player. The term also breaks if players cannot actually commit to or implement the probability distribution the model assigns.

Why the word has to exist: Mixed strategy exists because some games punish predictability. If every fixed move can be exploited, the stable object cannot be a single move. It has to be a pattern of probabilities that denies the other side an easy target.

Math in plain sight: When you see a probability on each action, read it as controlled unpredictability. When the paper solves by indifference, remember whose payoff is being equalized: often my randomization is chosen to remove your strict reason to switch actions. Also check unused actions; they must not secretly earn more than the actions receiving positive probability.

Jargon decoder

Subgame, credible threat, backward induction, sequential rationality

Where the reader sees it: A paper discusses future histories, threats, promises, subgames, dynamic consistency, sequential rationality, backward induction, or whether a plan remains believable after an unexpected move.

Plain translation: A plan must still make sense when the future decision point actually arrives. A threat is not credible just because it was announced earlier.

First-principles pressure: Time creates a gap between what someone wants others to believe now and what that person will want to do later. Dynamic game theory exists because future incentives can undo present claims.

Mathematical object: Backward induction solves later decision nodes first and carries their values back to earlier choices. Subgame perfection requires equilibrium behavior not only on the predicted path but also inside each proper continuation game.

Reading test: Find the future moment where the promised action would be taken. If the actor would rather do something else at that moment, the threat or promise needs commitment, reputation, or changed payoffs to become credible.

Common confusion: The common mistake is evaluating the whole plan only from the beginning. Credibility is tested at the later node, after earlier moves have already happened.

How to unpack it in a paper: Jump to the future history where the promised action would be taken. Ask what the actor observes there, what actions remain available, and whether carrying out the earlier statement is still best at that later moment. If the paper uses subgame perfection, verify that the history begins a proper subgame; if beliefs matter there, the paper may need a belief-based refinement instead.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs empty threats and promises. It prevents a model from counting speech at the beginning as credible unless the later incentives make the speech worth honoring.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when the tree omits hidden information, renegotiation, commitment devices, or reputation. Backward logic is only as reliable as the histories and information structure being solved. A precise backward solution can still be the wrong lesson if the represented future is not the future the players face.

Why the word has to exist: Subgame and credibility language exists because early words can be cheap. A plan that sounds intimidating at the beginning may become unattractive after the future history arrives. The vocabulary forces the reader to test the later self, not only the earlier announcement.

Math in plain sight: When you see backward induction, read the tree from the end toward the start. Each later node sends a value back to the earlier node. When you see subgame perfection, read it as equilibrium checked again inside every proper continuation problem. If a history is not a proper subgame because information is pooled, the paper needs a belief-based tool instead.

Jargon decoder

Discount factor, patience, continuation value, present value

Where the reader sees it: A paper uses delta, patience, long-run payoff, continuation value, repeated interaction, future punishment, or relational contracting.

Plain translation: Future consequences are being converted into today's decision. A high discount factor means the future weighs heavily; a low one means tomorrow barely disciplines today.

First-principles pressure: Repeated interaction changes incentives only if future gains and losses matter enough now. A short-term temptation can be resisted when the future relationship is valuable, but the same temptation wins when the future is weak or unlikely.

Mathematical object: A repeated value often looks like v0 + delta v1 + delta^2 v2 + ... . Delta is the weight on one-period-ahead value, and powers of delta make farther futures count less today.

Reading test: Compare the one-time gain from deviating with the discounted stream lost after punishment. If the future loss is smaller than the immediate gain, the proposed cooperation is not held together by repetition.

Common confusion: The trap is saying the future matters without checking how much. Repetition is not magic; it works through a numerical comparison between present temptation and future discipline.

How to unpack it in a paper: Rewrite the claim as a comparison between deviation today and continuation tomorrow. Identify the one-time gain, the future punishment or lost surplus, the discount factor, and whether the deviation is observed soon enough to trigger consequences.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs vague talk about relationships and patience. It turns future discipline into a present-value calculation that can succeed or fail depending on monitoring, horizon, and how strongly players value tomorrow.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when the future is treated as automatically powerful. A low discount factor, noisy monitoring, a known final period, or weak punishment can make repeated interaction strategically close to a one-shot problem. It also breaks when continuation value is named but not computed; the term must eventually cash out as a comparison against present temptation. Watch for papers that cite patience while leaving observability or punishment credibility implicit.

Why the word has to exist: Discounting exists because tomorrow is not the same as today in a present choice. People may die, leave, forget, lose trust, or care less about delayed payoffs. The term turns the vague shadow of the future into a weight.

Math in plain sight: When you see delta, read it as the current weight on next period. Delta squared is the weight on the period after that. The series matters because many small future losses can beat one tempting gain only if the weights stay large enough. The useful paper check is to isolate the one-period temptation before summing the promised future discipline.

Jargon decoder

Type, prior, posterior, belief, signal, information set

Where the reader sees it: A paper describes hidden types, prior distributions, posterior beliefs, observed signals, Bayesian updating, or private information.

Plain translation: The model is keeping track of what someone does not know. A type is a hidden kind of actor or state; a belief is a probability over which hidden case is true.

First-principles pressure: People often choose while missing information that another person has. The action they observe may be both a move and a clue, so the model needs a way to update beliefs after evidence appears.

Mathematical object: A prior belief is the probability before the signal. A posterior belief is the probability after the signal. Bayes rule updates by asking how likely the observed evidence would be under each hidden type.

Reading test: Ask what is hidden, who knows it, what is observed, and how the observation changes beliefs. Then ask whether the strategy and beliefs fit together in equilibrium.

Common confusion: The common mistake is treating beliefs as opinions floating outside the model. In Bayesian games and signaling models, beliefs are formal objects that change payoffs and determine which actions are sensible.

How to unpack it in a paper: Name the hidden variable first: type, value, cost, signal, or history. Then identify the prior, the observed evidence, the posterior, and the action that becomes sensible under that posterior. If the paper says Bayesian, look for the update rule or the reason updating is constrained.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs the habit of treating uncertainty as background noise. It puts missing information inside the strategic model so actions can depend on what players believe rather than on what the analyst knows afterward.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when beliefs are chosen only to support a desired conclusion. Off-path observations, unjustified priors, and type spaces that omit important cases can make a belief-based explanation too flexible. It also breaks when the paper forgets that different types may choose different actions, so one observed move may select the population being inferred from.

Why the word has to exist: Type and belief language exists because the analyst may know more than the modeled players know. The model needs a separate object for each player's uncertainty, otherwise it accidentally lets everyone act as if hidden information were already public.

Math in plain sight: When you see a prior, read it as the guess before the clue. When you see a posterior, read it as the revised guess after the clue. Bayes rule matters because it prevents the paper from updating beliefs in a way that ignores how likely the clue was under each hidden case. If the observation was chosen strategically, ask which types would have produced it on purpose.

Jargon decoder

Incentive compatible, truthful, no profitable misreport, self-selection

Where the reader sees it: A paper says a mechanism is truthful, incentive compatible, strategy-proof, self-selecting, or robust to misreporting.

Plain translation: The rule is designed so the actor gets the best result by doing the behavior the designer wants, such as telling the truth or choosing the contract meant for their type.

First-principles pressure: A designer cannot simply demand honesty when participants have private information and their own goals. The rule has to make the desired behavior personally attractive compared with lying, hiding, or imitating someone else.

Mathematical object: An incentive constraint compares the payoff from the intended action with the payoff from a deviation. Truthfulness means truthful reporting gives at least as much payoff as every relevant false report.

Reading test: Find the deviation the actor might prefer. If the paper does not compare the intended behavior against that deviation, the mechanism claim is incomplete even if the rule is clearly described.

Common confusion: The trap is reading incentive compatibility as a statement about character. It is not saying people are honest; it is saying the rule makes honesty or self-selection the best move inside the model.

How to unpack it in a paper: Write the intended behavior beside the best tempting deviation. For truthfulness, compare honest reporting against each false report. For self-selection, compare the contract meant for a type against the contracts that type might imitate. Then ask whether the inequality must hold for every type, every report, or only for the subset of agents the mechanism actually serves.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs design by wishful thinking. It forces the mechanism designer to prove that participants prefer the intended behavior using their own payoff comparison, not the designer’s hope.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when important deviations are omitted or when the rule assumes information the designer cannot observe. Incentive compatibility is always relative to a stated action menu, information structure, and payoff model. A mechanism that blocks one lie may still fail if another unmodeled manipulation remains profitable.

Why the word has to exist: Incentive compatibility exists because rules do not enforce themselves. A designer may want truth, effort, or self-selection, but participants compare consequences from their own point of view. The word names the requirement that the rule make the intended behavior personally best.

Math in plain sight: When you see an incentive constraint, read it as intended payoff at least as large as deviation payoff. The inequality matters because the designer's preferred behavior must survive the participant's private comparison, not just appear in the instructions. Strong papers name the deviation set; weak ones prove honesty only against the easiest lie.

Jargon decoder

Common knowledge, public signal, higher-order belief, shared awareness

Where the reader sees it: A paper talks about public announcements, common knowledge, higher-order beliefs, shared signals, coordination, or communication protocols.

Plain translation: It is not only that people know a fact. They know that others know it, know that others know that they know it, and so on enough for coordinated reasoning.

First-principles pressure: Coordinated action can fail when everyone privately knows the same fact but cannot rely on everyone else knowing it too. Public information changes behavior by changing the layers of mutual awareness, not necessarily by changing the physical fact.

Mathematical object: The object is recursive belief: I know X, I know you know X, I know you know I know X, and the chain continues. Common knowledge is the idealized endpoint of that recursion.

Reading test: Ask whether the action requires private information or shared confidence. If people must coordinate on what others will infer, then the paper is about higher-order belief, not just data transmission.

Common confusion: The common mistake is treating public and private signals as equivalent because they contain the same fact. In strategic settings, who knows that others know can matter as much as the fact itself.

How to unpack it in a paper: Track the level of shared awareness the result needs. First ask who knows the fact. Then ask who knows that others know it, and whether the protocol or public event keeps that chain going far enough for the coordination claim.

What this term repairs: This vocabulary repairs the idea that information is only about facts. In coordination problems, the publicness of a fact can be the strategic ingredient because it lets players rely on others relying on the same fact. It also explains why a message can matter even when it repeats information everyone privately suspected.

Where the translation breaks: The translation breaks when a paper treats private, mutual, public, and common knowledge as interchangeable. If coordinated action requires higher-order confidence, identical private messages may not substitute for a public announcement.

Why the word has to exist: Common knowledge exists because coordination can require confidence about confidence. It may not be enough that everyone has the fact; each person may need to know that others can rely on the same fact when choosing.

Math in plain sight: When you see higher-order belief, read each layer as one more sentence about what someone knows that others know. The recursion matters because coordinated action can depend on the chain, while ordinary information only records who received the fact. The practical test is whether a private duplicate message would produce the same reasoning; if not, publicness is doing the work. Also ask whether people interpret the signal the same way, because shared receipt without shared meaning may still fail to coordinate behavior.
