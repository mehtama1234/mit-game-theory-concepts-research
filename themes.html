Themes And Subthemes

Choice Under Tradeoffs

The course begins before strategic conflict: a person has to be able to rank outcomes before a model can ask what they will choose. This theme is about turning vague wants into a disciplined object that can survive calculation without pretending the number is literal happiness.

Why This Theme Matters

Without this layer, every later game table is decoration. Payoffs, expected payoffs, bidding values, outside options, and bargaining surplus all assume that the model has already said what each participant is trying to protect or gain.

Cross-Course Argument

The first lectures use preferences and utility to build the measuring stick used everywhere else. Later lectures quietly reuse the same stick in mixed strategies, Bayesian games, auctions, and bargaining: once risk, hidden values, or future rounds enter, the course still needs a way to compare alternatives before the player knows which world will arrive.

Lecture Evidence Chain

The opening preference and utility evidence anchors the construction of rankings and numerical payoffs. The expected-utility evidence then shows why probabilities must be folded into payoff comparisons when the outcome is uncertain. Those records support the theme directly; later uses in auctions and Bayesian games are cross-theme synthesis.

Subthemes

  • Ranking Outcomes: Preferences, Utility, Expected Utility

Subtheme · Choice Under Tradeoffs

Ranking Outcomes

Everyday problem: Before anyone can solve a game, the model has to know what counts as better or worse for each person. The everyday version is simple: if a student chooses between two schedules, or a bidder chooses between money and an object, the model needs a consistent ranking of those outcomes before strategy can enter.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is representability. Preferences are qualitative rankings, but game theory often needs a number that preserves those rankings so the course can compare actions, lotteries, and expected payoffs.

Mathematical lever: The lever is a utility representation and, under uncertainty, an expected-utility average. The number is useful because it lets later concepts ask whether one action gives a higher payoff than another under the same strategic conditions.

Why it matters: This subtheme prevents a common mistake: treating payoffs as arbitrary table decorations. They are the compressed statement of what each player is trying to achieve, and every equilibrium or auction result depends on them.

First-principles walkthrough: Start with ordinary preference: which outcome would the person choose if both were available? Then ask whether the ranking is consistent enough to be represented by a utility number. If chance enters, average the utility of each possible outcome by its probability rather than pretending uncertainty has disappeared.

Cross-links and limits: The same payoff ranking reappears in best response, bargaining, auctions, and Bayesian games. The limit is that ordinal preference and expected utility do different jobs; risk comparisons require more structure than a simple ranked list.

Strategic Dependence

This is the point where ordinary decision-making becomes game theory. A move can be sensible or foolish only after the other players choices are included, so the course needs representations that keep everyone’s actions in the same picture.

Why This Theme Matters

Strategic dependence is the reason a payoff matrix, a game tree, dominance, deletion, and rationalizability exist. They are all ways to avoid judging an action in isolation when its consequence is created jointly with other actions.

Cross-Course Argument

The strategic-form lecture makes dependence visible in a table. The extensive-form lecture shows that timing can be part of the dependence. Dominance and iterated deletion then ask whether some actions can be removed before equilibrium reasoning begins, while rationalizability asks what choices can still make sense under some belief about others.

Lecture Evidence Chain

The strategic-form and extensive-form records supply the representation evidence. Dominance, iterated deletion, and rationalizability records support the pruning and belief-based parts of the theme. The theme argument is the bridge between those lecture spans.

Subthemes

  • Tables, Trees, And Strategic Dependence: Strategic Form Games, Extensive Form Games
  • Eliminating Bad Moves: Dominance, Iterated Deletion, Rationalizability

Subtheme · Strategic Dependence

Tables, Trees, And Strategic Dependence

Everyday problem: A social situation can be confusing because everyone’s outcome is created by several people at once. The course therefore needs pictures that keep the players, actions, timing, and payoffs visible.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is representation discipline. A payoff table is good when timing can be ignored; a tree is needed when the order of moves or information at a decision point changes what a player can condition on.

Mathematical lever: The lever is the mapping from action profiles or histories to payoffs. Strategic form compresses simultaneous choices into profiles; extensive form expands the situation into nodes, branches, histories, and information sets.

Why it matters: The representation often decides which solution concept is even meaningful. Dominance in a matrix, backward induction in a tree, and PBE in an information structure are not interchangeable visual styles.

First-principles walkthrough: Ask whether players choose once without seeing each other, or whether someone observes, waits, or moves later. If timing has no strategic content, use the table. If later choices depend on earlier histories, draw the tree and preserve what each player knows at each node.

Cross-links and limits: Tables connect to dominance and Nash equilibrium. Trees connect to backward induction, subgame perfection, bargaining, and PBE. The analogy breaks when a table hides a threat or a tree pretends players know histories they cannot observe.

Subtheme · Strategic Dependence

Eliminating Bad Moves

Everyday problem: Some games are cluttered with actions that no careful player would use. The everyday problem is how to remove obviously bad possibilities without already knowing exactly what everyone else will do.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is belief-free versus belief-based pruning. Dominance removes a move because another move beats it across opponent actions; rationalizability keeps a move if some defensible belief could make it optimal.

Mathematical lever: The lever is comparison across opponent actions. Strict dominance checks an inequality in every relevant column or contingency, while iterated deletion repeats that comparison after earlier deletions shrink the game.

Why it matters: This subtheme teaches a lighter form of reasoning before full equilibrium. It shows that some behavior can be ruled out by internal inconsistency, but it also warns that deletion rules can depend on how strong the dominance notion is.

First-principles walkthrough: List one player’s possible actions. Compare two of them while holding each possible opponent action fixed. If one action is never worse and sometimes better, the inferior action has no strategic defense. After removing it, repeat the question because the strategic environment has changed.

Cross-links and limits: Pruning connects backward to payoff tables and forward to Nash equilibrium. It also connects to common knowledge because repeated deletion assumes players understand rationality and understand that others understand it. Weak dominance is the limit case where deletion order and interpretation can matter.

Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency

Equilibrium reasoning asks for a prediction that does not immediately fall apart when each player looks at their own incentives. It is less a claim that the outcome is good than a consistency check on everyone’s planned behavior.

Why This Theme Matters

Many stories about strategic behavior sound plausible until one player can profitably change. Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, zero-sum value, and minimax reasoning give the course tests for whether a proposed outcome survives individual deviation.

Cross-Course Argument

Best response supplies the one-player test. Nash makes all players pass that test at once. Mixed strategies show that stability may require deliberate randomness. Zero-sum and minimax sharpen the same idea under pure conflict, where protecting yourself against the opponent’s best attack becomes the prediction logic.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Best-response and Nash records anchor the general stability test. Mixed-strategy records support intentional randomization. Zero-sum and minimax records support the special case where conflict turns stability into security-level reasoning.

Subthemes

  • Stable Predictions: Best Response, Nash Equilibrium, Mixed Strategies
  • Conflict And Security: Zero-Sum Games, Minimax

Subtheme · Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency

Stable Predictions

Everyday problem: A strategic prediction is fragile if one person can look at it and say, given what everyone else is doing, I should switch. The everyday problem is finding a forecast that survives that private check.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is mutual consistency. Each player’s action must be understandable as a best response to the others, so the prediction is held together by everyone’s incentives at once.

Mathematical lever: The lever is the best-response condition. Nash equilibrium applies it to all players simultaneously; mixed strategies add probability weights when no fixed pure action can be stable.

Why it matters: This subtheme is the central bridge from story to prediction. It lets the course reject outcomes that sound plausible but contain a profitable one-person deviation.

First-principles walkthrough: First freeze what everyone else is doing. Ask what one player would choose. Do the same for each player. If the proposed profile passes every one of those checks, it is stable in the Nash sense; if not, the story has not earned prediction status.

Cross-links and limits: Stable prediction connects to dominance because dominated moves cannot be best responses. It connects to subgame perfection and Bayesian Nash when timing or hidden information changes what best response means. Stability still does not imply efficiency or fairness.

Subtheme · Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency

Conflict And Security

Everyday problem: In pure conflict, doing well means preparing for an opponent who benefits from your loss. The everyday problem is choosing a plan when the other side is actively trying to exploit it.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is security under hostile response. A player should evaluate a move by the worst consequence an opponent can force, not by a friendly or average response.

Mathematical lever: The lever is maximin/minimax comparison. One side chooses the action or mixture with the best worst-case payoff, while the other side chooses the response that makes that payoff as small as possible.

Why it matters: This subtheme clarifies a special but powerful case of equilibrium. In zero-sum games, strategic stability, value, and protection against exploitation line up more cleanly than in general games.

First-principles walkthrough: Imagine announcing a plan to an adversary. For each plan, mark the worst payoff the adversary can force on you. Then choose the plan whose worst case is least damaging. Mixed strategies enter when predictability itself becomes exploitable.

Cross-links and limits: Conflict-and-security connects to mixed strategies and best response. The limit is that many economic games are not zero-sum: cooperation, side payments, future interaction, or market design can make the security logic incomplete.

Time, Threats, And Credibility

When actions unfold over time, a plan is not credible merely because someone announces it early. The course’s time-based concepts ask whether a later action will still be worth taking when the later moment actually arrives.

Why This Theme Matters

Threats, promises, offers, refusals, and continuation plans are common in real strategic settings. Without a credibility test, a model may predict behavior based on statements that no rational player would carry out later.

Cross-Course Argument

Game trees represent timing. Backward induction solves from the end because the end constrains the beginning. Subgame perfection removes noncredible threats. One-shot deviation gives a local test for dynamic optimality. Bargaining uses the same time pressure when delay changes the value of agreement.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Extensive-form evidence anchors trees and histories. Backward-induction, subgame-perfection, one-shot-deviation, and bargaining evidence records provide the credibility checks that make this theme more than a timing label.

Subthemes

  • Credible Time Paths: Backward Induction, Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium, One-Shot Deviation Principle, Bargaining

Subtheme · Time, Threats, And Credibility

Credible Time Paths

Everyday problem: Plans over time can fail because the person who promised a later action may not want to carry it out later. The everyday problem is separating real threats and promises from empty announcements.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is sequential rationality. A plan must make sense not only at the start, but after every history where a player has to act.

Mathematical lever: The lever is backward recursion through a game tree. Terminal outcomes give values to final nodes; those values are pulled backward so earlier choices reflect credible later behavior.

Why it matters: This subtheme explains why timing changes game theory. A Nash equilibrium can rest on a threat that would never be used, while subgame perfection and one-shot deviation tests remove those unstable time paths.

First-principles walkthrough: Draw the last real decision first. Ask what the player would do there. Replace that part of the tree with its value, then move one step earlier. Continue until the start of the game inherits only choices that later players would actually want to carry out.

Cross-links and limits: Credible time paths connect to bargaining because delay changes outside options and surplus division. They connect to repeated games because punishment must remain credible. They break when beliefs, hidden information, or renegotiation change the continuation values.

Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Repeated interaction changes the meaning of a current move because today’s gain can affect tomorrow’s treatment. The theme asks when the future is strong enough to discipline short-run temptation.

Why This Theme Matters

Many relationships, markets, and organizations do not happen once. Cooperation, punishment, reputation, and tacit collusion become possible only when players expect their current action to be remembered or answered later.

Cross-Course Argument

Finite repetition shows the known-last-round problem: if nothing follows the final period, cooperation can unravel backward. Infinite repetition removes the fixed endpoint and lets trigger strategies work. The folk theorem shows how large the set of sustainable outcomes can become, while cartel applications translate the abstract logic into market behavior.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Finite and infinite repeated-game records support the horizon contrast. Folk theorem evidence supports the broad sustainability result. Cartel evidence supplies the applied interpretation in pricing and market competition.

Subthemes

  • The Future As Discipline: Finitely Repeated Games, Infinitely Repeated Games, Folk Theorem, Implicit Cartels

Subtheme · Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

The Future As Discipline

Everyday problem: People may want to cooperate, but a one-time temptation can be stronger than goodwill. The everyday problem is when tomorrow’s consequences can make today’s cooperation rational.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is intertemporal incentive compatibility. Cooperation survives only if the discounted value of keeping the relationship outweighs the current gain from cheating.

Mathematical lever: The lever is a comparison between deviation gain and discounted punishment loss. Finite horizons often unravel because the final round has no future discipline; infinite or uncertain horizons can support trigger strategies.

Why it matters: This subtheme explains how repeated play changes the set of possible outcomes. It is the course’s bridge from prisoner-dilemma-style failure to sustained cooperation, collusion, and folk-theorem multiplicity.

First-principles walkthrough: Find the one-period gain from cheating. Then compute what the player loses if future partners punish that cheating. If the future loss, after discounting, is larger than the immediate gain, cooperation can be self-enforcing.

Cross-links and limits: The future-as-discipline connects to credibility, because punishments must be worth carrying out, and to mechanisms, because repeated markets can generate informal rules. It breaks with impatience, poor monitoring, known final periods, or weak punishment capacity.

Private Information And Beliefs

Strategic choices often happen when one person knows something another person does not: a value, cost, type, signal, or history. This theme is about making hidden information explicit enough to reason about.

Why This Theme Matters

If private information is ignored, the model treats different kinds of players as if they were the same. Bayesian games, types, beliefs, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, and perfect Bayesian equilibrium are tools for keeping those hidden differences inside the analysis.

Cross-Course Argument

Types and beliefs give names to unknown facts and probability stories. Bayesian Nash asks each type to choose optimally before all hidden information is revealed. Perfect Bayesian equilibrium adds time and updating, requiring beliefs and future actions to fit the observed path.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Bayesian-game and type evidence records support the representation of hidden information. Bayesian Nash records support type-contingent best response. PBE records support the dynamic link between beliefs, observed behavior, and sequential rationality.

Subthemes

  • Hidden Types And Beliefs: Bayesian Games, Types And Beliefs, Bayesian Nash Equilibrium, Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium, Screening And Adverse Selection

Subtheme · Private Information And Beliefs

Hidden Types And Beliefs

Everyday problem: One side often knows something the other side cares about. A bidder knows her value, a worker knows ability, a sender knows type, or a player knows a private signal. The problem is how to reason before those facts are publicly known.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is conditioning behavior on possible worlds. The model must say what types might exist, what each player believes about them, and what each type would choose.

Mathematical lever: The lever is expected payoff over types, plus Bayes-consistent updating in dynamic settings. A strategy is no longer one action; it can be a plan that tells each type what to do.

Why it matters: This subtheme is what prevents private-information games from collapsing into complete-information fiction. It lets the course analyze auctions, signaling, reputation, and dynamic belief revision.

First-principles walkthrough: List the hidden facts. Assign probabilities to them. For each type, ask which action gives the best expected payoff given beliefs about others. If actions are observed later, update beliefs when possible and check whether future behavior remains rational.

Cross-links and limits: Hidden types connect to auctions through private values, to signaling through type-dependent messages, and to PBE through updated beliefs. The limit is that off-path beliefs and assumed priors can carry heavy modeling weight.

Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Mechanism lectures reverse the usual question. Instead of only predicting behavior under fixed rules, they ask how rules should be built when participants will respond strategically to those rules.

Why This Theme Matters

Auctions, online ads, and market institutions are engineered strategic environments. A rule that looks good mechanically can fail if bidders shade bids, hide values, exploit ranking formulas, or change behavior once the rule is known.

Cross-Course Argument

Auction lectures show how bidding rules translate private values into allocations and payments. Revenue equivalence shows that different rules can agree under strong assumptions. Ad auctions add platform constraints such as clicks, positions, and quality scores. Cartel material shows the darker side: firms may also design informal punishment rules for themselves.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Auction, revenue-equivalence, and ad-auction records support the formal market-design side. Cartel evidence links rules and incentives to repeated market behavior rather than centralized design.

Subthemes

  • Rules For Bidding: Imperfect Competition, Auctions, Revenue Equivalence, Ad Auctions

Subtheme · Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Rules For Bidding

Everyday problem: A seller, platform, or institution has to allocate scarce objects when participants know their own values and can shade what they reveal. The everyday problem is designing rules that still work when people act strategically.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is rule-induced incentives. A bid is not just a number; it is a response to the payment rule, allocation rule, beliefs about rivals, and sometimes quality or position effects.

Mathematical lever: The lever is incentive compatibility and expected payment. Auction models compare how rules map bids and values into allocation probabilities and payments, then ask whether truthful or shaded bidding is optimal.

Why it matters: This subtheme turns game theory into market engineering. It explains why small rule changes can alter bidding behavior, seller revenue, efficiency, and platform outcomes.

First-principles walkthrough: Write the object being allocated, each bidder’s private value, the allocation rule, and the payment rule. Then stand in one bidder’s shoes and ask what bid maximizes payoff given beliefs about others and the consequences of winning.

Cross-links and limits: Rules for bidding connect to hidden types because values are private, and to stable prediction because bidding strategies must be equilibrium behavior. Revenue equivalence breaks when its assumptions fail; ad auctions add platform-specific constraints beyond textbook single-object auctions.

Communication And Knowledge

Communication changes games only when it changes what people believe others know or intend. This theme separates empty words from messages and public facts that can actually coordinate behavior.

Why This Theme Matters

Real strategic life is full of announcements, signals, advice, disclosures, threats, and public information. The course needs tools for asking when these things persuade, reveal, mislead, or coordinate.

Cross-Course Argument

Signaling studies costly or informative actions by privately informed senders. Cheap talk asks when costless words can still matter. Common knowledge pushes beyond private awareness into higher-order awareness: not just knowing a fact, but knowing that others know it too.

Lecture Evidence Chain

Signaling and cheap-talk evidence records support the incentive side of communication. Common-knowledge records support the higher-order knowledge side, where public facts become strategic only through shared awareness.

Subthemes

  • Messages And Shared Awareness: Signaling, Cheap Talk, Common Knowledge

Subtheme · Communication And Knowledge

Messages And Shared Awareness

Everyday problem: People talk, signal, announce, and observe public facts, but not every message should be believed and not every public fact coordinates action. The everyday problem is knowing when information actually changes behavior.

Hidden principle: The hidden principle is belief movement under incentives. Communication matters when it changes what receivers believe, and credibility depends on whether senders of different types would want to send different messages.

Mathematical lever: The lever is a belief system plus incentive constraints. Signaling uses costly or type-dependent actions; cheap talk uses payoff alignment; common knowledge uses repeated knowledge operators rather than ordinary private awareness.

Why it matters: This subtheme explains the strategic life of information. It covers why education can signal ability, why advice may or may not persuade, and why coordination can require everyone to know that everyone else knows.

First-principles walkthrough: Ask what the sender knows, what the receiver sees, what each type would prefer the receiver to believe, and whether the message or action is costly enough to separate types. For public facts, ask whether the fact is merely observed or mutually and repeatedly known.

Cross-links and limits: Messages connect to hidden types and mechanisms through incentive compatibility. Common knowledge connects back to equilibrium because players may need shared awareness to coordinate on a strategy. The analogy breaks if costless talk is treated like costly signaling.
