Model-Building Workbook

A from-scratch checklist for turning an ordinary strategic situation into a usable game model. Each step names the everyday question, the formal slot, the math check, the failure mode, and transcript-backed links.

Model-building workbook

Name the decision makers before naming the game

Ordinary question: Who can make a choice that changes what happens? Start with people, firms, platforms, regulators, bidders, sellers, buyers, or committees before writing down any formal object.

Formal slot: Players. A player is not necessarily a person; it is any decision-making unit whose action the model treats as chosen rather than automatic.

Why this slot exists: Strategic dependence only exists when more than one chooser matters. If a character in the story cannot affect outcomes, they may be context rather than a player. If a hidden actor can change payoffs, leaving them out makes the model explain the wrong pressure.

Construction move: List every candidate actor, then delete actors whose behavior is fixed by the setup. For each remaining actor, write one sentence beginning with: this actor chooses whether to...

Math check: A payoff function needs a player index, such as u_i, because each actor can rank the same outcome differently. The subscript is the reminder that the model is tracking separate points of view.

Failure if skipped: The analysis starts optimizing a blended story. You may accidentally use the platform's objective to explain bidder behavior, or use social welfare to explain a firm's price choice.

Worked prompt: In an ad auction, the platform designs the rule, advertisers bid, and users react to ad quality. Decide which of those are active players in the model you need for the question being asked.

Naive shortcut: The shortcut is to name every person or institution in the story as a player. That makes the model look complete but usually hides the actual strategic pressure. A user may affect platform value without being modeled as a chooser, while the platform may be a designer in one question and background infrastructure in another.

Step-by-step use: First write the real question in one sentence. Then list candidate actors and mark which ones choose something inside that question. For each active player, write the action verb they control. Finally check whether any omitted actor can change payoffs or information; if yes, either add them as a player or state why their behavior is fixed.

Transfer check: In a paper, compare the named players with the actors mentioned in the motivation. If an actor appears in the story but not in the player set, ask whether they are fixed background, folded into payoffs, or accidentally omitted. This check often reveals whether the model is about firms, consumers, platforms, regulators, or a designer.

Model audit protocol: Before trusting the player list, run a role audit. For each actor, ask: do they choose, do they only react mechanically, do they design the rules, or do they merely receive consequences? If the same institution appears in two roles, split the roles or state which role the model studies. A clean player list should make the later payoff subscripts feel necessary rather than decorative.

Model-building workbook

Separate what can be chosen from what merely happens

Ordinary question: What choices are actually available at the moment of decision? Do not include hopes, outcomes, labels, or states of the world as actions unless the actor can directly choose them.

Formal slot: Actions or strategies. In a one-shot table this may be one move; in a game tree it may be a complete plan for many possible future decision points.

Why this slot exists: A model can only test deviations among choices that were available. If the choice set is too wide, the model invents impossible behavior. If it is too narrow, the model hides the deviation that would break the proposed answer.

Construction move: For each player, write a short menu of available moves. Then ask whether the player chooses once, chooses after observing something, or chooses a plan for several possible histories.

Math check: Best-response reasoning compares payoffs across feasible actions. The word feasible matters: BR_i(a_-i) is chosen from player i's action set, not from every imaginable thing the player might want.

Failure if skipped: You can call an outcome stable even though a missing feasible deviation would overturn it. You can also blame a player for not choosing an option that the model never actually allowed.

Worked prompt: In an entry-threat story, the entrant chooses enter or stay out first. The incumbent chooses fight or accommodate only if entry occurs. That timing means a tree is clearer than a single flat list.

Naive shortcut: The shortcut is to list outcomes as actions: profit, cooperation, trust, winning, or high welfare. Those are results the actor wants, not necessarily buttons the actor can press. The model becomes misleading when it lets a player choose a consequence rather than a feasible move that may or may not produce that consequence.

Step-by-step use: For each player, write the action menu using verbs: bid, enter, reject, wait, disclose, punish, cut price. Then remove anything the player cannot directly choose. Next ask whether the action is a one-time move or a complete strategy that must specify behavior after several possible histories. Only after that should payoffs be attached.

Transfer check: When reading a model, underline every action set. If the paper later discusses a deviation that is not in the set, the model is missing a choice. If the paper assumes away an action that would be natural in the setting, look for a sentence justifying why that action is infeasible, illegal, too costly, or outside the question.

Model audit protocol: Before solving, run a deviation audit. Take the proposed answer and write the most tempting alternative move for each player in ordinary language. Then check whether that move appears in the action set. If it is absent, either the model has deliberately ruled it out or the stability claim is too strong. This catches models that look formal while hiding the real escape route.

Model-building workbook

Choose table or tree by asking when choices are seen

Ordinary question: Do people choose at the same time, or does someone move after seeing an earlier action? The answer decides whether the model needs simultaneous reasoning or backward reasoning.

Formal slot: Game form. A strategic-form table compresses choices into one simultaneous comparison. An extensive-form tree shows the order of moves, information available at each node, and later decision points.

Why this slot exists: Time changes incentives. A threat made before entry may not be attractive after entry. A promise made before bargaining may not survive when the last offer is on the table. The representation has to expose the moment where incentives are tested.

Construction move: Draw the calendar before solving. Mark who moves first, what later players observe, and which histories would never be reached if everyone followed the predicted path.

Math check: Backward induction reads a tree from the end toward the beginning. Subgame perfection then asks whether the proposed strategy remains an equilibrium inside every continuation game.

Failure if skipped: The model treats cheap threats as if they were commitments. It may predict behavior from the opening announcement while ignoring the later payoff comparison that makes the announcement unbelievable.

Worked prompt: For a platform ban threat, ask whether the platform can precommit to the ban or will choose later after seeing seller behavior and current revenue. Those are different games.

Naive shortcut: The shortcut is to flatten time because tables are easier to solve. That can erase the very reason the problem is strategic. A threat, promise, counteroffer, or punishment has meaning only because it is made now and possibly carried out later under different incentives.

Step-by-step use: Draw a timeline before writing equations. Mark each decision point, who moves there, what they have observed, and what remains possible. Then decide whether simultaneous form is an honest compression or whether a tree is needed. If a later choice changes the credibility of an earlier statement, keep the timing visible.

Transfer check: In a paper, look for words such as after, before, observes, commits, threatens, announces, or history. Those are signs that timing may be doing work. If the formal model is a simultaneous table despite those words, ask whether the timing has been deliberately compressed or accidentally lost. Then check whether the claimed result depends on a later action being credible; if it does, the representation must preserve that later decision point.

Model audit protocol: Before accepting the representation, run a clock audit. Rewrite the model as a plain timeline with observation points marked. If two choices happen at different times but the formal object treats them as simultaneous, write what assumption makes that compression harmless. If no such assumption exists, the model may be solving the wrong game because it erased the moment where credibility is tested.

Model-building workbook

Write down who knows what at the moment of choice

Ordinary question: What does each decision maker know, what do they only guess, and what can they observe before acting?

Formal slot: Information structure. This includes hidden types, beliefs, signals, information sets, prior probabilities, posterior probabilities, and public or private announcements.

Why this slot exists: People cannot condition their choices on facts they do not know. When information is hidden, actions become evidence, and beliefs become part of the strategic environment rather than background commentary.

Construction move: For every decision point, write: this actor knows..., this actor does not know..., this actor observes..., and this actor believes... . Then ask whether the observed behavior should update those beliefs.

Math check: Bayes rule updates beliefs after observed evidence. Perfect Bayesian equilibrium adds the requirement that strategies are sensible given beliefs and beliefs are consistent with strategies wherever the path provides evidence.

Failure if skipped: The model either gives actors impossible knowledge or forgets that observed actions can reveal hidden types. Both errors can make signaling, screening, and communication claims look easier than they are.

Worked prompt: In a warranty model, buyers do not directly observe product quality. They observe the warranty and ask which seller type would find that action attractive enough to choose.

Naive shortcut: The shortcut is to let the analyst's knowledge leak into the players' minds. Once the analyst knows the seller is high quality, the model may accidentally let the buyer act as if they know it too. That destroys the reason signaling, screening, and Bayesian reasoning are needed.

Step-by-step use: At each decision point, write three lists: known facts, unknown facts, and observed signals. Then write the belief that converts unknown facts into probabilities. After any observed action, ask whether the belief should move. This makes information a working part of the model rather than a paragraph of background. If a player cannot distinguish two histories, put those histories in the same information set or explain the equivalent belief restriction.

Transfer check: In a paper, separate hidden state from observed action. If an action is being interpreted as evidence, ask which types would want to take that action and which types would avoid it. If all types behave the same way, the observation may not carry the informational meaning the prose assigns to it. Also ask whether the signal is public or private, because belief updating and coordination are different problems.

Model audit protocol: Before using beliefs, run an information audit at every decision point. Ask what the player knows directly, what they infer, and what they merely wish were true. Then ask whether two histories look identical to that player. If they do, the model should not let the player choose differently across those histories. This catches accidental omniscience, the most common hidden-information modeling error.

Model-building workbook

Translate consequences into separate payoff comparisons

Ordinary question: What does each actor gain or lose from each possible outcome, including money, delay, risk, reputation, future punishment, and missed outside options?

Formal slot: Payoff mapping. The model maps an action profile, history, or outcome into a payoff number for each player.

Why this slot exists: Game theory cannot infer behavior from labels like cooperate, defect, honest, aggressive, or fair. It needs to know what those choices cost and benefit each actor. The same visible action can mean different incentives under different payoff mappings.

Construction move: Write each outcome in ordinary language, then write what each player cares about in that outcome. Only after that should you assign numbers or inequalities.

Math check: A payoff vector lists one payoff for each player. Expected utility averages payoff numbers across uncertain outcomes. Repeated games add continuation values, where future payoffs are discounted before being compared with present temptation.

Failure if skipped: The model borrows moral or narrative meaning as if it were a payoff. That can make cooperation seem automatic, threats seem powerful, or truth telling seem natural without checking the actual incentives.

Worked prompt: In a repeated cartel, the payoff from undercutting today must be compared with the future payoff loss after punishment. High current profit alone does not settle the model.

Naive shortcut: The shortcut is to treat labels as payoffs: cooperation is good, cheating is bad, truth is desirable, punishment is costly. Game theory needs each actor's private comparison, not the moral label attached by the analyst. A choice that sounds bad socially may still be privately attractive.

Step-by-step use: For every terminal outcome or relevant history, write what each player receives in ordinary terms. Include money, delay, outside options, risk, reputation, and future consequences. Then convert those consequences into numbers or inequalities only after the ingredients are visible. If there is uncertainty, write the states and probabilities before averaging.

Transfer check: In a paper, find the payoff definition and ask what real pressures are missing. If the result depends on cooperation, truth telling, deterrence, or revenue, check whether the payoff includes the temptation that would undermine it. Missing payoffs often explain why a formal result feels too easy. Also check whether payoffs are one-period, expected, or discounted continuation values, because those versions answer different questions. If the payoff is normalized, verify that the normalization did not erase the comparison being claimed.

Model audit protocol: Before trusting a payoff table, run a pressure audit. For each player, name the force pushing toward the claimed behavior and the force pulling away from it. Both forces should appear somewhere in the payoff comparison. If only the desired behavior has a payoff and the temptation is verbal, the model has not earned its conclusion. If future consequences matter, check that they enter as continuation values rather than as vague discipline.

Model-building workbook

Pick the solution test after the model shape is clear

Ordinary question: What kind of stability or rationality question is appropriate for this model: deletion, best response, Nash equilibrium, subgame perfection, Bayesian equilibrium, or incentive compatibility?

Formal slot: Solution concept. This is the rule for deciding which behavior patterns count as internally stable or defensible in the model.

Why this slot exists: Different model shapes create different failure modes. Simultaneous interaction needs mutual best-response checks. Dynamic interaction needs credibility after later histories. Hidden information needs beliefs. Designed rules need incentive constraints.

Construction move: Do not start by saying Nash. First ask whether the game has timing, hidden information, repeated future incentives, or rule design. Then choose the weakest test that answers the actual question and state what deviations it checks.

Math check: Nash checks unilateral deviations at the strategy profile. Subgame perfection checks continuation games. Bayesian equilibrium checks strategies against beliefs. Incentive compatibility checks intended behavior against misreports or off-menu deviations.

Failure if skipped: A paper or explanation can sound formal while using the wrong stability test. The most common error is applying a simultaneous equilibrium label to a dynamic or information problem that needs extra consistency checks.

Worked prompt: A seller-signal problem usually cannot stop at Nash equilibrium because buyers' beliefs after observed behavior matter. The solution concept has to include both optimal actions and belief consistency.

Naive shortcut: The shortcut is to choose the most familiar solution concept first and force the model into it. That can produce formal language without the right test. Nash is not wrong because it is simple; it is wrong when the problem's actual failure mode involves time, beliefs, types, commitment, or mechanism incentives that Nash alone does not inspect.

Step-by-step use: After players, actions, timing, information, and payoffs are written, ask what kind of error would invalidate a proposed answer. If one player can deviate in a simultaneous table, use a best-response or Nash-style check. If a later threat is not credible, use a dynamic refinement. If hidden information matters, include beliefs. If a rule asks for truthful behavior, write incentive constraints.

Transfer check: In a paper, identify what the solution concept is ruling out. Does it rule out one-person deviations, noncredible continuation behavior, type-specific deviations, inconsistent beliefs, or profitable misreports? If the real concern is not in that list, the chosen concept may be too weak for the claim.

Model audit protocol: Before naming the solution, run a failure-mode audit. Write the sentence: this proposed outcome would fail if... . Complete it with the actual danger: a unilateral deviation, a noncredible future action, a type-specific deviation, a belief update problem, or a misreport. The solution concept should inspect that danger directly. If it inspects a different danger, the proof may be formally correct but aimed at the wrong target.

Model-building workbook

State the boundary where the model stops earning trust

Ordinary question: What real detail has been simplified away, and would the answer survive if that detail returned?

Formal slot: Model boundary. This includes missing actors, omitted actions, simplified payoffs, assumed beliefs, monitoring assumptions, commitment assumptions, and distributional assumptions.

Why this slot exists: A model is a controlled simplification. It earns trust by making the right pressure visible, not by pretending every real-world detail is inside the equations. The boundary statement tells the reader which omitted detail would most likely change the answer.

Construction move: After solving, write one sentence of the form: this answer depends on... . Then name the assumption whose failure would most directly change the result, such as monitoring, commitment, private information, risk neutrality, or the set of feasible deviations.

Math check: Every inequality, best-response condition, or belief update has premises. Change the action set, payoff numbers, information structure, or discount factor, and the sign of the comparison can change.

Failure if skipped: The reader exports a classroom conclusion as a universal law. Cooperation, truth telling, deterrence, or revenue equivalence may disappear once monitoring, commitment, risk, or hidden information changes. Without the boundary, the site teaches vocabulary but not judgment.

Worked prompt: Revenue equivalence depends on assumptions about bidder values and auction environment. If those assumptions change, the theorem may stop applying even though the auction vocabulary stays familiar.

Naive shortcut: The shortcut is to treat the solved model as the world. A model earns trust by isolating a pressure, not by swallowing every detail. The boundary is not an apology; it is the instruction manual for when the answer should and should not travel.

Step-by-step use: After solving, name the load-bearing assumptions. Ask which assumption, if changed, would most directly flip an inequality, alter a belief update, add a deviation, or change a payoff comparison. Then write the boundary in plain language before exporting the result to a new setting.

Transfer check: In a paper, look for robustness checks, assumption paragraphs, or model extensions. If the conclusion is presented broadly but the assumptions are narrow, mark the claim as local. Then ask which real-world detail would be the first one to test before trusting the result outside the model. A good boundary note should name not only what is omitted, but which part of the solution would change if the omission returned.

Model audit protocol: Before exporting the result, run a boundary audit. Pick the strongest conclusion sentence and attach the phrase 'as long as...' to it. The words that follow should name the load-bearing assumption, not a vague caution. Then change that assumption in one direction and ask which inequality, belief, action set, or payoff comparison moves first. If nothing specific moves, the boundary statement is too ceremonial to guide judgment.
