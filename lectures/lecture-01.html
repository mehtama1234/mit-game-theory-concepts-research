Lecture 1 · Choice Under Tradeoffs, Strategic Dependence, Mechanisms, Auctions, And Markets

Lecture 1: Introduction to Individual Decision-Making

Builds the individual-choice foundation: preferences, utility, beliefs, lotteries, and the idea that choices need a payoff language before strategy can begin.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: can we just say what people choose and move on? The lecture slows that down because choice is not usable in a model until the alternatives can be compared in a stable way.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture starts before game theory: a player cannot reason strategically until the model says what that player prefers. It builds from ranked outcomes to utility numbers and then to risky choices, so later payoff tables are not arbitrary decoration.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as representation. A preference ordering becomes a utility function, and uncertain outcomes become expected utility. The point is not to measure happiness; it is to make comparisons precise enough for later strategic checks.

The mathematical object has to exist because natural language can hide contradictions. You can say you like many things, but a model needs to know whether those likings fit together into one consistent ranking. A preference relation is the smallest formal rule that lets the course check whether your choices are complete and consistent enough to build strategic analysis on top of. Tables, graphs, and equations need numbers to work. You cannot plug words into a formula. Utility is the translator from 'I want this' to a number, so the course can calculate what rational behavior looks like. It is the handle that moves from 'prefer' to 'has the larger payoff.' Uncertainty creates a bundle of possible outcomes. You choose now and find out later which one happens. You cannot optimize separately for each future state—you need one score for the whole risky action. Expected utility is that one score, built by summing (payoff × probability) over all possibilities you assign a belief to. You cannot avoid this representation. Once every payoff depends on every player's action at once — on the full profile — you need a thing that holds all those dependencies in one place and makes them visible. Words scatter the information across a story. A table collects it so you can ask a clear question: if player 1 changes their move from A to B while player 2 stays the same, does player 1 do better or worse? The table lets you check this without confusion. Because plausibility depends on belief. A move can look odd from outside and still be rational for someone who expects opponents to behave differently. Normal language tends to lump surprising, wrong, irrational, and off-the-equilibrium-path into one bucket. Rationalizability separates them. The math must exist because the question—does there exist a probability distribution over surviving opponent strategies such that this action maximizes expected payoff—requires checking an infinite space of possible beliefs. Ordinary reasoning cannot track this precisely. The math asks for existence: there must be at least one coherent belief that supports the move. The belief does not have to be the analyst's best guess or shared by everyone. It just has to be coherent enough to make the action optimal. Allocation and payment are linked. If I bid high, I am more likely to win but I also might pay more. My payoff depends on both. I cannot analyze my choice unless I model: the values everyone has, the bid space, the allocation rule, the payment rule, my beliefs about others, and how payoffs work. Without all of these pieces, you cannot tell if someone bid their truth, shaded their value, or distorted a bid to manipulate what they believe others will do.

Worked Mini-Example

If someone chooses between a sure payment and a lottery, the model needs more than a list of outcomes. It needs a rule for comparing risk, so the lecture turns probability-weighted consequences into a choice object.

Mistakes To Avoid

The easy mistake is to treat utility as a psychological substance. In this course, utility is a compact ranking device that lets later game models compute what a player would rather do.

The tempting shortcut is to treat payoff numbers as direct measurements of pleasure, money, or moral value. That shortcut fails because the later strategic model only needs a disciplined ranking that survives comparisons, lotteries, and counterfactual choices.

Students often get stuck by asking: is this preference realistic for a real human in a real situation? The course is not trying to explain human psychology. It is asking a modeling question: in this problem, can we treat the player's choices as coming from a stable ranking? If yes, we can use that ranking to predict what they will do. Students often think utility is money or happiness — something real that you can add up. Then they get confused when a utility of 100 does not mean 'twice as happy' as 50, or when one person's utility of 50 has nothing to do with another person's. The cure is to remember: utility is a number you pick to represent a ranking and make it computable. Two common traps: students sometimes average dollar amounts when they should average payoff (utility) amounts, because payoff measures what you care about, not the raw dollars. Also, students forget that probabilities come from your belief before you know the answer—they are not frequencies from looking back at what already happened. Students often think a row of the table has one value and one meaning. That is wrong. A row shows what happens to player 1 across all of player 2's possible moves. Player 1's payoff in that row changes depending on which column you are in — which move player 2 picked. So a move cannot be judged as good or bad until you ask: good or bad against which opponent move? The table has multiple values per row because the opponent has choices. Students often get stuck because rationalizability feels permissive—like anything goes. It is permissive on purpose: it widens the net before you lock down predictions. But it is not empty. A strategy still has to be a best response to some belief. You cannot just claim a move is rational because someone somewhere might pick it. It has to be optimal under that belief, which means it must yield a higher expected payoff than the alternative moves. A trap is thinking of bids as if they are confessions of value. They are not. Bids are actions that respond to incentives. The payment rule, the belief about what others will do, and the risk of losing all shape the bid. The same bidder will bid differently under different rules.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that game theory starts with a language for individual tradeoffs before it talks about strategic conflict. Preferences say what is better than what; utility compresses those rankings into numbers; expected utility adds uncertainty without pretending the numbers are a happiness meter.

Reader Work

While reading this lecture, build a small preference table before writing any utility numbers. Mark which comparisons are rankings, which comparisons involve uncertainty, and which claims would change if the utility scale were stretched. The work is to separate choice order from psychological interpretation.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: explain why two different utility scales can represent the same preference ordering, then give one situation where the spacing between utility numbers would start to matter. If you cannot separate ranking from scale, the later payoff tables will feel more objective than they are.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to representation of games: once each player has a payoff language, the next problem is to describe who moves, what they can do, what they know, and how everyone's choices jointly create outcomes.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for the place where an economic or strategic model has to say what an actor counts as better or worse before any reasoning about their moves begins. Watch for any time a paper or problem turns a goal, payoff, preference, or outcome into a number that can be compared, added up, or optimized. That is utility at work. Look for private information, uncertain types, lotteries, or risk where the model must add up payoffs across multiple possible worlds weighted by beliefs. When you see a problem where two or more agents choose at the same time (or nearly so), where each agent's payoff depends on everyone's choice, and where the exact order of moves or who knows what information is not the main point, you are looking at a situation that fits strategic form. Look for payoff matrices, simultaneous moves, or models where timing is compressed so that strategic dependence — the fact that my payoff hangs on your choice — becomes the main thing to understand. Watch for models that separate clearly impossible behavior—strategies that are never best responses to any belief—from behavior that is one of several plausible responses under different expectations. Any analysis that asks whether a strategy could be justified by some coherent belief is invoking rationalizability. When you see a system where someone has private information and a rule converts their choice into an outcome and a payment, you are seeing an auction.

Concepts In This Lecture

Preferences

Problem: Before anyone starts thinking strategically, we need a way to ask: which outcome does this person actually want? The catch is that outcomes are tangled up. One choice gives you more money but more risk. Another gives you less money but more free time. Before you can ask whether someone makes sense or does best for themselves, you need a way to say how they rank all these mixed-up options.

Mathematical handle: The object is a preference relation: a rule that compares any two outcomes and says which one ranks higher (or if they tie). For this rule to be useful in a model, two things have to be true. First, completeness: you must be able to compare any two outcomes in the problem — no cases where you say I do not know. Second, transitivity: if you rank A above B and B above C, then you must rank A above C — no loops.

What breaks without it: Without a clear preference ranking, the numbers in a payoff table have no real meaning. Someone could assign them however they want. There is no way to check whether the payoff numbers actually match what the player is trying to do or whether later calculations about their best move are trustworthy.

Utility

Problem: When several people each chase what is best for them, you need to know whether one person will prefer outcome A or outcome B. But you cannot write down a math formula using the words 'I like this more' — math works with numbers. So you have to turn 'A is better than B' into numbers you can actually use in a calculation.

Mathematical handle: A utility function u is a machine that takes an outcome (like 'get pizza') and outputs a number. That number is higher when the outcome ranks higher in someone's preference list. The job of u is to preserve the order — nothing more, nothing less.

What breaks without it: Without numbers, you cannot write a payoff table, calculate expected payoffs when outcomes are uncertain, or find best responses in a game. The course would be stuck describing outcomes without predicting behavior.

Open concept page

Expected Utility

Problem: You often must decide before you know what will happen: will a bid win or lose? Will a risky investment pay off or lose money? Will someone turn out to be honest or dishonest? You need a way to compare choices when the future is uncertain.

Mathematical handle: Expected utility is the number you get when you weight each possible payoff by its probability and sum them. It squeezes a risky situation down to one score so you can compare choices.

What breaks without it: Without expected utility, games where players have hidden information—like auctions or contract negotiations—would have no clear way to score an action. You could not reason about what a rational player would do when they do not know the full picture.

Strategic Form Games

Problem: Imagine you and your friend each need to choose something at the exact same moment, without knowing what the other chose. But once you both decide, what you get depends on both choices together. How do you show what's really going on? You need a table that captures the fact that nobody gets to see the other's choice before picking their own.

Mathematical handle: The thing you build is called a normal-form game. It collects: a list of all players, the set of moves each player can pick, and a payoff function that assigns a score to each player for every possible profile (i.e., every combination of moves, one from each player).

What breaks without it: If you only describe a game in words — a story about two people who want different things — you can talk about what seems good in theory, but you lack the structure to check your claim. You cannot easily compare what happens when player 1 chooses A versus B, or see which payoff pairs are possible. A table lets you check these things row by row and cell by cell.

Open concept page

Rationalizability

Problem: Deciding which strategies make sense to consider without assuming everyone has reached one locked-in agreement about what will happen. You need a test that catches strategies people might reasonably choose before you check whether all their choices can fit together perfectly.

Mathematical handle: A set—a list—of strategies where each surviving strategy has the property that it can be a best response to some belief about which of the other surviving strategies the other players will use. You build this set step by step: keep only strategies that are best responses to some probability distribution over the remaining strategies.

What breaks without it: Without it, students jump straight from strategies that are never good to Nash equilibrium strategies—skipping the middle ground. They miss that a strategy can survive because one player could hold a coherent belief that makes it their best move, even if that belief turns out not to match everyone else's choices in the final prediction.

Open concept page

Auctions

Problem: Suppose you want to sell your bicycle. Buyers know their own willingness to pay, but you do not. Also, each buyer does not know if someone else values the bike more. How do you run the sale so buyers bid honestly and you get a fair price?

Mathematical handle: An auction is described by four things: the values each bidder privately holds, the range of bids each bidder can make, the rule that decides who wins for each set of bids, and the rule that decides how much the winner pays.

What breaks without it: If we skip auctions, we miss the most direct example of how to take hidden information and turn it into visible bids through smart rule design. We also could not explain why bidders do not bid their true value in all auction types, or why different payment rules lead to different bids from the same person, or why some auction designs encourage honesty while others encourage bluffing.
