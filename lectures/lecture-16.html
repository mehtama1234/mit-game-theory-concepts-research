Lecture 16 · Choice Under Tradeoffs, Private Information And Beliefs

Lecture 16: Bayesian Games

Introduces hidden types and priors so strategy can be modeled when players do not share the same information.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture begins the incomplete-information part of the course. It introduces types and priors so hidden values, costs, beliefs, or payoff states become part of the game rather than vague uncertainty.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through type spaces and probability distributions. A strategy can become a rule from types to actions, and payoffs are evaluated in expectation over unknown types.

The mathematical object has to exist because uncertainty creates bundles of possible outcomes. A player choosing now cannot optimize separately in each future state; they need one comparison for the whole risky action. Expected utility is that comparison, built by weighting each consequence by the probability assigned before the outcome is known. The mathematical object has to exist because hidden information changes the identity of the decision problem. Types and priors let the model represent what each player knows privately and what others believe. Without them, private values and costs would be invisible to the equilibrium calculation. The mathematical object has to exist because uncertainty has two sides: the hidden state and someone’s probability over it. Types represent the state; beliefs represent the uncertainty. Keeping them separate lets the course analyze signaling, updating, and type-contingent strategy without confusing truth with perception. The mathematical object has to exist because ordinary average reasoning lies in selected populations. A cutoff, participation condition, or posterior distribution is needed to say who accepts, who rejects, and what the uninformed side should believe after observing that choice.

Worked Mini-Example

A bidder knows their own value but not a rival's value. The model represents the bidder's value as a type and the rival's unknown value through a prior distribution.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to say players are uncertain and leave it there. Bayesian games force the model to name what is hidden, who knows it, and what probabilities others assign.

Students often get stuck by averaging dollar outcomes when the model needs utility outcomes, or by forgetting that the probabilities are beliefs from the player’s point of view rather than facts known after the uncertainty resolves. Students often get stuck by treating uncertainty as noise outside the model. In a Bayesian game, uncertainty is inside the model and has named objects: types, beliefs, strategies, and payoffs. Students often get stuck by saying “the player believes the type” as if belief and type were the same thing. One is the hidden state; the other is someone’s uncertain view of it. Students often get stuck by treating hidden information as just another probability. The lecture point is sharper: the action or price changes which hidden types remain in the game, so the probability distribution after participation is not the original distribution.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for private information, lotteries, risk, or unknown types where the model has to average payoffs across possible worlds. Look for models with types, priors, private values, costs, signals, or asymmetric information. Look for any model where behavior depends on values, costs, quality, or information that only one side observes. Look for models where an offer, price, contract, or test makes different hidden types sort themselves. If changing the rule changes the pool of participants, the screening/adverse-selection primitive is active.

Concepts In This Lecture

Expected Utility

Problem: The problem is choosing when the outcome is uncertain: a bid might win or lose, a risky action might pay off or fail, and a type may be one of several possibilities.

Mathematical handle: The object is expected utility: utility in each possible state weighted by that state's probability. It compresses a risky situation into one comparison number.

What breaks without it: Without expected utility, Bayesian games and auctions would have no disciplined way to evaluate actions under unknown types or rival values. The model would either ignore risk or smuggle in an unspecified attitude toward it.

Open concept page

Bayesian Games

Problem: The problem is strategic choice when players do not know exactly who they are facing or what payoffs the other side has.

Mathematical handle: The object is a game with types, priors over types, actions for each type, and payoff functions that may depend on type.

What breaks without it: Without Bayesian games, the course would treat hidden values, costs, and motives as if everyone knew them. Auctions, signaling, and incomplete-information bargaining would collapse into misleading complete-information models.

Open concept page

Types And Beliefs

Problem: The problem is keeping track of hidden facts and what players think those facts might be.

Mathematical handle: The object is a type space plus probability beliefs over types or histories.

What breaks without it: Without types and beliefs, private-information models would not say who knows what or how uncertainty enters payoffs. The analyst could not distinguish a high-value bidder from a low-value bidder or a receiver belief from sender truth.

Open concept page

Screening And Adverse Selection

Problem: The problem is that a price or offer can change who shows up. A seller, insurer, or bargainer may set a rule thinking about an average customer, but the people who accept are not average; they are selected by the rule itself.

Mathematical handle: The object is an expected payoff under a cutoff rule: a price or offer is accepted only by types for whom accepting is better than rejecting, so the distribution of accepted types changes with the price.

What breaks without it: Without this concept, the course would predict trade whenever buyer value exceeds seller cost. It would miss the reason some trades fail: the terms that attract one type may drive away another or reveal information that changes future bargaining.
