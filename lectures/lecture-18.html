Lecture 18: Auctions

Turns private values into strategic bids under allocation and payment rules.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture turns private values into strategic bids. Allocation and payment rules shape whether bidders reveal, shade, or distort their values, so the rules themselves become the object of analysis.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through allocation rules, payment rules, values, bids, and expected payoffs. A bidder compares the chance of winning with the surplus left after payment.

The mathematical object has to exist because auction outcomes are created by rules. Values, bids, allocation, and payments have to be represented together so the course can ask how a bidder trades off winning more often against paying more when they win. The mathematical object has to exist because comparing auction formats requires more than comparing labels. Expected revenue depends on allocation probabilities, payment rules, and equilibrium bidding. Revenue equivalence formalizes the conditions under which those pieces line up across mechanisms.

Worked Mini-Example

In a first-price auction, bidding your full value may win but leaves no surplus. The bidder shades the bid because winning is useful only when the price is below value.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to think bids are transparent reports of value. A bid is a strategic action created by the auction format's payment consequences.

Students often get stuck by treating bids as direct value reports. In many auctions, bids are strategic actions distorted by payment rules, risk, competition, and beliefs about rivals. Students often get stuck by comparing the rules while holding bids fixed. Equilibrium bids change with the auction format, and those strategic adjustments are exactly what the theorem accounts for.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for allocation and payment rules that transform private values into strategic bids. Look for auction comparisons where allocation sameness and bidder payoff normalization drive payment conclusions.

Concepts In This Lecture

Auctions

Problem: The problem is allocating an object when people know their own values but the seller and rival bidders may not.

Mathematical handle: The object is an auction mechanism: bidder values, bid spaces, allocation rules, and payment rules.

What breaks without it: Without auction theory, the course would miss one of the clearest economic applications of incomplete-information games. It could not explain bid shading, truthful bidding in special formats, or why payment rules change behavior.

Open concept page

Revenue Equivalence

Problem: The problem is comparing auction formats that look different: first-price, second-price, English, and others may create different bidding behavior.

Mathematical handle: The object is a theorem linking allocation rules and expected payments under assumptions such as risk neutrality, independent private values, and symmetric bidders.

What breaks without it: Without revenue equivalence, every auction comparison would be treated as an isolated case. The course would miss the deeper structure that makes some formats equivalent and the assumptions that make equivalence fragile.
