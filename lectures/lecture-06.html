What This Lecture Teaches

Applies strategic reasoning to firm competition, where prices or quantities interact through demand and payoff consequences.

Where The Math Enters

The mathematical object has to exist because words like competition and market power are too loose. The model must say what firms choose, how choices determine price or demand, and how profit changes when rivals act. Best responses make the strategic dependence explicit.

Mistakes To Avoid

Students often get stuck by mixing up the market story with the strategic object. The story may be coffee shops, patents, or internet providers, but the object is the payoff map from firm choices to profits.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for a model where firms choose prices, quantities, entry, capacity, or product features while knowing that rivals' actions change the payoff. If the paper asks for a best response by a firm rather than a single optimal monopoly price, this concept is active.

Imperfect Competition

Concepts In This Lecture

Imperfect Competition

Problem: The problem is that many real markets sit between a street market with countless sellers and a single monopolist. A firm may be large enough that its price or quantity choice moves the market, but it still has rivals whose choices push back.

Mathematical handle: The object is a profit function for each firm, such as price minus cost times quantity, together with a best-response rule that says which quantity or price is most profitable against rival behavior.

What breaks without it: Without this concept, the course could not explain why a firm may charge above cost, why price competition can be brutal, or why changing the modeling choice from quantity to price can change the prediction. Market behavior would be described as if firms either had no power or total power.

Transcript teaching note: This anchor is useful because the lecture explicitly explains why the market application belongs in game theory. A firm with market power is not merely choosing against a fixed background. Its production or price decision can affect the market, and rival firms may also matter. That is the first-principles pressure behind the concept page: once the firm can move outcomes, profit maximization has to be written as a strategic payoff problem.

Evidence boundary: The evidence supports the market-power motivation for imperfect competition. It does not establish a specific model's equilibrium or show which real market has market power. Mathematical claim: A payoff function u_i(a_i, a_-i) maps everyone's actions to player i's payoff. The useful relation is `u_i(a_i, a_-i).`. In plain language: i is one player; a_i is that player's action; a_-i means everyone else's actions; u_i is the payoff assigned to the whole action profile.

In the case of imperfect competition where we have a few large firms, we normally think of what we say is that these firms have market power. What does market power mean? It's really the opposite of being a price taker.
