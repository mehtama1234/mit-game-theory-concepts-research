Lecture 19: Revenue Equivalence

Shows when different auction formats produce the same expected revenue because strategic bid adjustments offset rule differences.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: if auction formats feel different, must seller revenue be different? The lecture shows that under strong conditions, strategic adjustments can make expected payments line up.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture asks when different auction formats become equivalent from the seller's revenue perspective. It teaches that surface rules can differ while expected payments line up under strong assumptions.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through expected payment functions and allocation probabilities. Under the theorem's conditions, payment differences are pinned down by the same allocation rule and boundary condition.

Comparing auction formats is not a matter of opinion or intuition — it requires mathematics because the answer depends on how allocation, strategy, and payment timing interact. Revenue equivalence formalizes the conditions under which those pieces produce the same seller payoff across mechanisms. Without the theorem, you would have only case-by-case reasoning and no systematic principle.

Worked Mini-Example

A first-price auction and second-price auction feel different to bidders, but if the same type wins with the same probability and assumptions hold, expected revenue can match.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to remember only the equivalence. The theorem is also a list of conditions; changing risk attitudes, values, symmetry, or allocation rules can break it.

The tempting shortcut is to treat revenue equivalence as a universal law. That fails because the result depends on assumptions about values, risk, symmetry, allocation rules, and boundary payments.

Students often get stuck by comparing auction rules while pretending bidders use the same strategy in both formats. That is not how auctions work. Bidders change their bids when the rule changes. The theorem accounts for those strategic adjustments — it is not a comparison of fixed bids. Forgetting this leads to the false conclusion that different payment rules automatically create different revenues.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that the theorem is a conditions machine. It teaches when surface format differences wash out and, just as importantly, which changes make the equivalence disappear.

Reader Work

Before applying the theorem, write the assumptions as a checklist: allocation rule, lowest-type payoff, symmetry, independence, risk neutrality, and entry conditions. The work is to learn what makes equivalence fragile, not just what makes it elegant.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: take a proposed use of revenue equivalence and strike out one assumption, such as risk neutrality or symmetry. Then explain which step of the equivalence story breaks. The theorem is learned by testing its failure points. Do not call formats equivalent until the allocation rule also matches.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to ad auctions: real platform auctions modify the object being sold, the ranking rule, and the quality information, so the clean theorem becomes a reference point rather than the whole story.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for auction comparisons where the author concludes that two mechanisms produce the same revenue. Check whether allocation is identical across formats and whether bidder payoffs are normalized to be the same at the lowest types. If those conditions hold and the assumptions match (risk-neutral, independent private values, symmetric bidders), revenue equivalence is likely at work.

Concepts In This Lecture

Revenue Equivalence

Problem: You need to sell something. You could ask bidders to write down their best offer and pay what they bid (first-price). Or you could ask them to write down their best offer but then pay what the second-highest bidder bid (second-price). These rules look completely opposite. Do they produce different results, or can they actually be equivalent?

Mathematical handle: The object is a theorem that links the way objects are allocated to the payments bidders expect to make. It depends on three core assumptions: bidders are risk-neutral (they care only about the size of their payoff, not how uncertain it is), each bidder knows their own value privately and nobody else learns it, and all bidders are treated symmetrically by the auction rules. Under these assumptions, expected revenue can be the same across different auction formats.

What breaks without it: Without revenue equivalence, you would have to evaluate each auction type in isolation, comparing first-price to second-price to English to open-ascending one pair at a time with no systematic tool. You would miss the deeper structure that tells you which assumptions make formats equivalent and which breaks them. The course would become a memorization list instead of a set of principles.
