Lecture 22 · Private Information And Beliefs, Communication And Knowledge

Lecture 22: Signaling

Studies how privately informed senders can influence receivers through observable actions that may reveal type.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: if someone takes a visible action, does it automatically reveal who they are? The lecture says no, because other types may want to imitate the same action.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture studies how a privately informed sender can affect a receiver's beliefs through observable action. The action is informative only when types have different incentives to imitate it.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through sender types, signal choices, receiver beliefs, receiver actions, and incentive constraints that stop one type from profitably mimicking another.

The math exists because you need a language for both what players do and what they believe. Strategies (rules for action) and beliefs (probability assignments to unseen facts) have to be tied together in a way that ordinary game solving does not require. Bayes' rule is the glue: it says how beliefs update when you observe data. By writing out both pieces and connecting them with Bayes' rule, the math forces you to check whether a proposed state is actually consistent—whether moves make sense given beliefs, and beliefs make sense given moves. Why do we need equations? Because observation and truth are different. You see an action, but you don't directly see the hidden type. The model must represent three things: the cost or benefit of each action for each type, the sender's choice, and the receiver's belief and response. Only then can you ask: 'If I propose that high types send action A and low types send action B, and the receiver responds this way, do high types actually want to send A and low types actually want to send B?' Without setting up those constraints in the model, you can tell a story that sounds right but actually has no teeth. The math forces you to check whether the story survives incentives.

Worked Mini-Example

Education can signal ability if high-ability workers find education less costly or more worthwhile. If low-ability workers would copy the same education level, the signal pools rather than separates.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to think costly automatically means informative. A signal works only if the wrong type does not want to imitate it under the receiver's response.

The tempting shortcut is to say costly signals are always credible. That fails because credibility depends on relative incentives: the action must be unattractive enough for the type that should not mimic it.

Many students solve the strategies first, then add beliefs as an explanation. But if off-path beliefs—guesses about what happens if the game goes somewhere unexpected—are left vague or arbitrary, the student misses that those beliefs control what happens if the game does reach that fork. A complete answer pins down both the strategies players follow and the beliefs they hold at every possible fork, including the ones they do not expect to reach. The trap is thinking cost equals information. A cost by itself proves nothing. A high-ability worker and a low-ability worker could both pay $1000 for a degree and both think it's worth it, so both get the degree. If the receiver sees the degree, they learn nothing — both types sent it. The key is whether the cost or the benefit differs by type in a way that changes which types take the action.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that information can be carried by incentive differences. A signal works when the cost-benefit tradeoff differs across types so the receiver can rationally update after seeing it.

Reader Work

For each type, compare the payoff from sending the signal with the payoff from imitating another type's signal. Then update the receiver's belief from the behavior that survives. The work is to see information as an incentive pattern. If both types choose the same signal, label the result pooling before claiming anything was revealed.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: test whether the low type wants to imitate the high type's signal and whether the high type wants to separate. If both types prefer the same visible action, explain why the receiver should not infer separation.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to bargaining with incomplete information: hidden types can make offers and delays informative, so negotiation becomes both surplus division and information management.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for games that unfold over time and players have incomplete information—they do not know some fact about each other or the world. Watch for these signals: players observe some earlier move and must form a new belief about an unseen type or hidden state; later moves depend on those updated beliefs; the problem asks you to verify that both the strategy moves and the belief updates are correct. Common places: a sender can choose whether to signal their type (high quality picking expensive education, low quality picking cheap school); a buyer can make an opening offer but does not know the seller's reservation price (the minimum the seller will accept), so the offer revises the buyer's guess about that minimum; negotiators exchange offers over time, each one a signal about desperation or patience. If a model mentions beliefs alongside dynamic strategies, or asks you to check off-path beliefs, you are looking at this equilibrium concept. When reading new research or watching the world, look for actions that are only valuable because others infer something hidden about you from them. Someone gets a fancy degree, works at a brand-name company, or displays expensive art. The action itself might not be fun or productive. Its value comes from observers updating their beliefs about your quality, reliability, or type.

Concepts In This Lecture

Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium

Problem: When a game happens step by step and players have secret information: after you see someone's move, you must update your guess about what they know, then decide your own next move based on that updated guess. The puzzle is to find a state where everyone's guesses make sense with what they've seen, and everyone's actions are rational given their guesses.

Mathematical handle: A settled state is a pair: a strategy for each player (a complete plan for all moves) plus a belief system (the guesses each player holds after each possible sequence of events they might see). This pair must satisfy two conditions: given their beliefs, each player's moves are optimal—the best they can do; wherever the game path they actually follow gives enough history, their beliefs must update correctly by Bayes' rule.

What breaks without it: Without this frame, a game model might say 'this player sends a signal' without explaining what beliefs make the signal worth sending, or why the other player would answer that way. The moves look like they work, but the hidden guesses that justify them are never checked. You end up with advice that sounds good but has no foundation—the beliefs and moves do not actually support each other.

Signaling

Problem: Imagine you're good at your job but a stranger doesn't know it. How do you make them believe you? You can't just say so — they'd expect a bad worker to say the same thing. You need to do something that a bad worker wouldn't want to copy.

Mathematical handle: The model is a game between a sender and a receiver. The sender has private information about their own type. The sender chooses an action. The receiver sees the action but not the type. The receiver forms a new belief about what type the sender is. The receiver then picks a response based on that new belief. The sender's payoff depends on both their type and the receiver's response.

What breaks without it: If you skip signaling, you can't explain education, insurance, or investment. You'd treat all statements the same way — as if saying 'I'm good' contains the same information as actually being good. You'd lose the insight that people choose actions partly to change other people's minds, and that the action works only when doing it costs different amounts depending on your hidden type.
