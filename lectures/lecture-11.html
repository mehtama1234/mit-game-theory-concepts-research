Lecture 11: One-Shot Deviation Principle and Bargaining

Adds a practical dynamic-equilibrium check and returns to bargaining with sharper tools for deviations over time.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: must we compare every complete alternative strategy in a dynamic game? The lecture introduces conditions under which checking one local change at a time is enough.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture gives a practical way to check dynamic optimality and then applies it to bargaining. Instead of comparing every possible strategy change, it asks whether a single local deviation can improve the continuation payoff.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as a local deviation test under the theorem's conditions. If no profitable one-shot deviation exists at any history, the strategy can pass the dynamic optimality check.

The mathematical object has to exist because dynamic games create huge spaces of possible strategies. A person's strategy in a game lasting many turns says what to do after countless different things that might happen — that is a massive object to write down and compare. You need a theorem that reduces the verification problem: instead of comparing everything, compare only one-shot deviations at each history. Without this theorem, verifying optimality in dynamic games by hand would be impossible for anything but tiny examples. The theorem makes the course teachable. The mathematical model has to exist because you cannot guess the split from the size of the surplus alone. A huge surplus (say, 1 million dollars) can be split 999k-to-1k if one side has all the power. The split comes from outside options, impatience, timing, and information—not from how much value is available. To explain who gets what, you have to put these factors into a model and solve it. Without the math, you have only intuition, which misses how use (outside options, patience, timing) shapes the deal. Words like competition and market power are too slippery. The math object forces precision. It must say: What do firms choose? How do those choices determine price or demand? How does profit change when a rival acts? A best-response rule makes the strategic interdependence explicit and checkable. Without it, you are just telling a story.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to use the one-shot test without checking the environment. The shortcut is powerful because theorem conditions make local deviations informative, not because all strategy changes are always local.

The tempting shortcut is to treat the one-shot deviation test as a universal shortcut. That fails because the theorem depends on the structure of the game; without the right conditions, local checks can miss profitable longer deviations.

Students often think the principle is automatic — usable everywhere — and forget that it depends on the game's structure. They memorize the shortcut but skip verifying that the assumptions (finite horizon, or constant discounting) actually hold in the game at hand. Then they apply it to a game with externalities or state-dependent payoffs and get a wrong answer. The principle is not free; it is only good when the structure justifies it. The trap is thinking: the split ought to be fair, so that must be the answer. Game theory does not care about fair. It asks: given the outside options, the patience of each side, and the timing of offers, which split will actually happen and stay stable? A 30-70 split might be predicted if one side has all the power. Students have to let go of the idea that the answer should be nice or equal. Students get tangled up by mixing the story with the math. The story—coffee shops, tech firms, internet providers—matters for intuition. But the math object is abstract: a payoff map from firm choices to profits. The story is easy; the interlock of payoffs and choices is what matters. Students often want to solve a coffee-shop problem without setting up the payoff formulas first.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that theorems buy convenience by adding assumptions. When the conditions hold, dynamic optimality can be audited history by history with a manageable local test.

Reader Work

Choose a history and change exactly one current action while keeping the continuation plan fixed. Record the immediate gain and future loss. The work is to understand why a local deviation test is powerful only under the theorem's conditions.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: choose one history and compare the planned action with exactly one current deviation. Then say which theorem condition makes this local check informative. If you cannot name the condition, the shortcut is being used mechanically.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to finitely repeated games: the same dynamic tools now face a setting with a known last period, where backward reasoning can unravel cooperation.

How To Recognize This Later

When you see a proof that verifies a strategy in a dynamic game, look for whether it replaces a full strategy comparison with a finite list of one-shot deviations. If an author writes, 'Suppose player i deviates at history h by choosing action a' instead of a, holding the rest of the plan fixed, then...' and uses that to conclude the strategy is optimal, they are using this principle. It is a signature move in repeated games and dynamic settings. When you see negotiations over a division (salary talks, merger price, splitting inheritance, peace treaty terms, or any situation where both sides gain from a deal but fight over the split), you are seeing bargaining. Look for these clues: one side makes an offer, the other accepts or rejects; there may be multiple rounds; delay is costly; one side has a backup plan (outside option); one side can wait longer than the other. Look for a model where firms choose prices, quantities, entry decisions, capacity, product features, or some other action—knowing that rival choices will shift the payoff they get. If a paper asks for what one firm will do given rivals' choices—that is, a best response, not a single monopoly optimum—this concept is in play.

Concepts In This Lecture

One-Shot Deviation Principle

Problem: Imagine you wrote down a complete plan for how to behave in a game that plays out over many moments — what you'd do after every possible thing that could happen. Checking whether someone would actually want to stick to that plan sounds simple: does anyone want to cheat? But there are so many moments and so many ways to cheat that checking all of them seems impossible.

Mathematical handle: The object is a local change: you pick one decision point in the game, try a different action at that moment, and ask whether that one change (while everything else stays the same) improves the score. Formally: replace action a with action a' at history h, hold the rest of the plan fixed, and check if the new score is higher.

What breaks without it: If you do not have this shortcut, you face a choice: either skip checking whether people want to cheat (which is not safe), or write down every possible alternative plan and score them (which takes forever for games that last many turns). For a game where your choice today affects the scores available tomorrow, and tomorrow's scores affect the day after, the number of plans to check grows so fast that you cannot do it by hand.

Bargaining

Problem: When a seller and buyer meet on a used car, both would prefer a deal to no deal. But the seller wants the price high, the buyer wants it low. Each can walk away. Each can wait. Each can reject offers. Bargaining asks: what price will actually stick, and how long will it take to agree?

Mathematical handle: The bargaining game has several parts: who can make offers (and in what order), what happens if an offer is rejected (disagreement payoff or the next person's chance to offer), how much delay costs each side (discounting—waiting this round gives you less value later), and sometimes private information (you do not know if the other side values the good at 5,000 or 20,000). The math model puts all these pieces together so you can solve which offers will be accepted.

What breaks without it: Without bargaining theory, you might say agreement is efficient (everyone is better off than if they quit) but then shrug about division. The course would skip the hard part: who gets 60% and who gets 40%? Why? Bargaining theory explains that the split comes from each side's outside option (what they get if talks fail), how much delay costs each side, and who has the power to make an offer. Leave out that tool and you have no way to predict what deal actually happens.

Imperfect Competition

Problem: Many real markets fall between two extremes: a street market with hundreds of sellers who can't move price alone, and a single monopolist who has no rivals. In between are markets with a handful of firms—each one big enough to matter, but each still facing pushback from the others. A coffee shop can raise its price and lose customers; an airline can add flights and depress fares. The question is: how do you figure out what each firm will do when all of them are playing this game at once?

Mathematical handle: The object is a profit formula for each firm—typically something like (price minus cost) times the quantity sold, or a similar expression. Paired with that is a best-response rule—a statement of which quantity or price maximizes profit given what you expect a rival to do. The object is the interlock between payoff and choice.

What breaks without it: Without this idea, the course could not explain why a firm charges more than cost, why price wars happen, or why the representation matters—whether a firm chooses price or quantity. Everything would collapse into either a firm with zero power in perfect competition or absolute power in monopoly. The strategic layer would disappear.
