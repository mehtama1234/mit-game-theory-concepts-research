Lecture 11: One-Shot Deviation Principle and Bargaining

Adds a practical dynamic-equilibrium check and returns to bargaining with sharper tools for deviations over time.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture gives a practical way to check dynamic optimality and then applies it to bargaining. Instead of comparing every possible strategy change, it asks whether a single local deviation can improve the continuation payoff.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as a local deviation test under the theorem's conditions. If no profitable one-shot deviation exists at any history, the strategy can pass the dynamic optimality check.

The mathematical object has to exist because dynamic strategies are too large to compare naively. A local deviation test gives the course a finite, teachable way to verify optimality under the right assumptions. It turns the huge space of possible strategy changes into a sequence of one-history checks. The mathematical object has to exist because surplus division depends on strategic alternatives, not only on the size of the surplus. Offers, rejection payoffs, delay costs, and outside options must be represented together. Bargaining models create that object so division can be explained by incentives rather than asserted fairness. The mathematical object has to exist because words like competition and market power are too loose. The model must say what firms choose, how choices determine price or demand, and how profit changes when rivals act. Best responses make the strategic dependence explicit.

Worked Mini-Example

In an alternating-offer bargain, ask whether a player would gain by rejecting just this offer or changing just this proposal while leaving future behavior fixed.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to use the one-shot test without checking the environment. The shortcut is powerful because theorem conditions make local deviations informative, not because all strategy changes are always local.

Students often get stuck by treating the principle as universally available. It depends on the structure of the dynamic game, so the shortcut must travel with its assumptions. Students often get stuck by assuming the fair split is the predicted split. Game theory asks what division survives incentives, not what division sounds fair from outside the model. Students often get stuck by mixing up the market story with the strategic object. The story may be coffee shops, patents, or internet providers, but the object is the payoff map from firm choices to profits.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for dynamic incentive proofs that replace many possible deviations with a one-period deviation inequality. Look for surplus division, alternating offers, take-it-or-leave-it offers, or incomplete information about values. Look for a model where firms choose prices, quantities, entry, capacity, or product features while knowing that rivals' actions change the payoff. If the paper asks for a best response by a firm rather than a single optimal monopoly price, this concept is active.

Concepts In This Lecture

One-Shot Deviation Principle

Problem: The problem is checking a whole dynamic strategy can be exhausting because a strategy describes what to do after many histories.

Mathematical handle: The object is a local deviation test: change one action at one history while leaving the continuation strategy fixed.

What breaks without it: Without this principle, verifying repeated or dynamic equilibria can become intractable. Students may either skip deviation checks or drown in too many possible alternative histories.

Open concept page

Bargaining

Problem: The problem is dividing gains from agreement when each side would like more of the surplus and can sometimes wait, reject, or walk away.

Mathematical handle: The object is a bargaining game with offers, acceptance rules, disagreement payoffs, and sometimes discounting.

What breaks without it: Without bargaining theory, the course could say agreement is efficient but not explain who gets what or why delay occurs. It would miss how threat points and waiting costs shape the split.

Open concept page

Imperfect Competition

Problem: The problem is that many real markets sit between a street market with countless sellers and a single monopolist. A firm may be large enough that its price or quantity choice moves the market, but it still has rivals whose choices push back.

Mathematical handle: The object is a profit function for each firm, such as price minus cost times quantity, together with a best-response rule that says which quantity or price is most profitable against rival behavior.

What breaks without it: Without this concept, the course could not explain why a firm may charge above cost, why price competition can be brutal, or why changing the modeling choice from quantity to price can change the prediction. Market behavior would be described as if firms either had no power or total power.
