Lecture 13: Infinitely Repeated Games

Removes the known last round and turns future punishment into a possible enforcement mechanism, where ongoing relationships can change one-shot incentives.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: does no final round mean cooperation is guaranteed? The lecture says no; the future helps only if the loss from future punishment outweighs the immediate gain from cheating.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture removes the known final period and makes the future a possible enforcement device. Cooperation can survive when the discounted loss from punishment outweighs today's temptation.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through discounted payoff sums and incentive constraints. A player compares the one-period deviation gain against the present value of future punishment.

In an ongoing relationship, today's choice has consequences for your future payoffs. To say 'the future matters' is true but vague. To make it precise, you need the discount factor (how much today's score compared to tomorrow's) and the continuation payoff (what you earn after today). These mathematical objects let you write down an inequality: gain from cheating ≤ discounted punishment loss. Without that math, 'the future will punish me' remains just a slogan, not a reason you can verify. The math turns the vague idea into a check: does this strategy actually work given these payoffs?

Worked Mini-Example

A firm can cut price today and steal customers, but rivals may punish with low prices afterward. Cooperation works only if the firm values future profit enough.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to say indefinite repetition creates trust by itself. The lecture's claim is an inequality: patience and punishment must make deviation unattractive.

The tempting shortcut is to describe long relationships as trust and stop there. That fails because the model needs an incentive inequality: today's temptation must be smaller than the discounted value lost later.

A student hears 'infinite repetition' and thinks the problem is solved — cooperation will happen automatically. But that is wrong. Infinite repetition is necessary, but not enough. The cooperation strategy must also satisfy the payoff inequality. If the punishment payoff is too small, if the discount factor is too low (the other player only cares about right now), or if the one-time cheat gain is very large, then cheating still pays. The student must check the inequality, not just invoke repetition.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that patience can create self-enforcement. Players may cooperate without external contracts when future consequences are valuable enough and punishments are credible enough.

Reader Work

Write the cooperate path and the cheat-then-punish path side by side with discount factors attached. The work is to compare today's temptation with the present value of future consequences, not simply to say repetition creates trust.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: write the deviation gain on one side and the discounted punishment value on the other. Then lower the discount factor and say which side becomes harder to satisfy. This turns 'long relationship' into an incentive inequality.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to the folk theorem: once future incentives can sustain cooperation, the next question is how many payoff patterns can be supported by suitable reward and punishment schemes.

How To Recognize This Later

When you see a real situation, look for these signs: trigger strategies (someone says 'I will do X as long as you do Y; if you cheat, I will do Z forever'), continuation values (someone mentions what a relationship is worth over time), and payoff comparisons (someone weighs today's gain against tomorrow's loss). These are the fingerprints of infinite-repetition logic.

Concepts In This Lecture

Infinitely Repeated Games

Problem: When you and someone else interact over and over, each of you wants to cheat today to get an instant reward — but you're worried the other person will punish you tomorrow if you do.

Mathematical handle: The object is a game played round after round, where your score today plus your discounted score tomorrow plus your discounted-twice score the day after is your total payoff. The discount factor delta measures how much you care about your future self.

What breaks without it: Without infinite repetition, you'd only have the case where the last round is known — and in that round, punishment fails because it cannot happen afterward. You would lose the simplest model showing how ongoing relationships can make people behave decently toward each other. You would also miss why impatience (a low delta) is a real threat to cooperation.
