Lecture 2: Representation of Games

Turns social situations into formal objects: players, histories, strategies, strategy profiles, payoffs, and the difference between trees and compact strategic form.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: if we know the players and payoffs, is the game already specified? The lecture says no, because timing, information, and strategy spaces decide what the players are actually choosing.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture asks how a messy interaction becomes an object that can be solved. It separates players, actions, strategies, histories, information, and payoffs, then shows why tables and trees reveal different parts of the same strategic situation.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through the game form. A strategic-form table compresses simultaneous choices; an extensive-form tree exposes order, histories, and information. The representation chooses which constraints the later solution concept can see.

The mathematical object has to exist because natural language can hide contradictions. You can say you like many things, but a model needs to know whether those likings fit together into one consistent ranking. A preference relation is the smallest formal rule that lets the course check whether your choices are complete and consistent enough to build strategic analysis on top of. Tables, graphs, and equations need numbers to work. You cannot plug words into a formula. Utility is the translator from 'I want this' to a number, so the course can calculate what rational behavior looks like. It is the handle that moves from 'prefer' to 'has the larger payoff.' You cannot avoid this representation. Once every payoff depends on every player's action at once — on the full profile — you need a thing that holds all those dependencies in one place and makes them visible. Words scatter the information across a story. A table collects it so you can ask a clear question: if player 1 changes their move from A to B while player 2 stays the same, does player 1 do better or worse? The table lets you check this without confusion. A flat list of actions cannot encode time or information. It cannot say who moved first, what was observed, or which histories look identical to a player. The tree structure preserves those facts as explicit objects—nodes, edges, information sets, payoffs—so that later reasoning about credibility and belief updates has a home. The mathematical object has to exist because sequential choice creates a problem within a problem within a problem: the very last decision stands alone, but the second-to-last decision contains the last decision inside it, and the first decision contains all the rest. Without a notation for solved subtrees, you would have to carry every possible future story forward in your mind. A continuation value—a single number representing the best payoff from here onward—lets you replace an entire future subtree with one number. Without this object, the analyst would be stuck: you would have to write out every possible branch and every possible future to check whether a plan was credible. With continuation values, you just calculate once and reuse the answer. The mathematical machinery exists because private information changes what the decision problem is. If payoffs depend on a hidden type, you cannot solve the game by looking at one payoff table. You need types to name what is hidden, priors to say how likely each type is, and type-contingent strategies to specify what each type does. Without these objects, private values and costs would be invisible. The equilibrium calculation would be meaningless. The math exists because hidden types create hidden best-response conditions that simple reasoning can miss. A single action per player is not enough when players have types—each type needs its own strategy and its own best-response test. Bayesian Nash formalizes this: it writes down (1) a strategy function for each player (mapping that player's type to an action), (2) a belief distribution over types, and (3) a consistency condition: each type's action must be a best reply in expectation. Without the math, you can only guess whether a proposal works; with it, you can verify it rigorously.

Worked Mini-Example

A threat may look persuasive in a payoff table because the table hides timing. Draw the same situation as a tree and the later player may not want to carry out the threat after the history arrives.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to think representation is bookkeeping. It is a modeling claim. If the form hides timing or information, the equilibrium calculation can be exact and still answer the wrong question.

The tempting shortcut is to draw the most convenient table and assume the solution concept will fix the rest. That fails when the table hides order, commitment, or private information that a player would use in the real interaction.

Students often get stuck by asking: is this preference realistic for a real human in a real situation? The course is not trying to explain human psychology. It is asking a modeling question: in this problem, can we treat the player's choices as coming from a stable ranking? If yes, we can use that ranking to predict what they will do. Students often think utility is money or happiness — something real that you can add up. Then they get confused when a utility of 100 does not mean 'twice as happy' as 50, or when one person's utility of 50 has nothing to do with another person's. The cure is to remember: utility is a number you pick to represent a ranking and make it computable. Students often think a row of the table has one value and one meaning. That is wrong. A row shows what happens to player 1 across all of player 2's possible moves. Player 1's payoff in that row changes depending on which column you are in — which move player 2 picked. So a move cannot be judged as good or bad until you ask: good or bad against which opponent move? The table has multiple values per row because the opponent has choices. Students often assume that a tree automatically means every player saw every earlier move. That is wrong. Information sets are the remedy. An information set groups nodes together because a player at those nodes cannot tell them apart — they might be at node A or node B, but they do not know which. So they must choose the same move at both. Students often get stuck by trying to read the tree the way a player would actually experience it—forward in time. The player is born at the start and encounters choices one by one, so it feels natural to solve from the start forward. But the analyst solving the game cannot do that because the analyst would have to guess what the future players will do. Backward induction flips the order: you solve from the end backward because the end is the only place where there is no ambiguity. Later incentives determine which earlier expectations are credible. Once you know what someone will definitely do at the end, you can work backward with certainty. Students get stuck by thinking uncertainty is external—something that happens to the model. In a Bayesian game, uncertainty is internal and has structure. The opponent has a type. That type affects their payoff. You hold a prior—a probability over that type. Your strategy responds to your own type. Everyone is doing this at the same time. The model is not incomplete because of uncertainty; the uncertainty itself is part of what defines the model. A trap: students check only the average player—'on average, does anybody want to deviate?'—and call it done. Bayesian Nash is stricter. A proposed plan can fail because one hidden type wants to deviate, even if the pooled behavior or the average player looks stable. You must check every type separately, even if they are rare. One type's defection can unravel the whole equilibrium.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that a game form is an argument about what matters. A strategic-form matrix emphasizes complete plans chosen at once; an extensive-form tree exposes histories and later choices. Neither is neutral bookkeeping.

Reader Work

For each example, redraw the situation twice: once as a compact action table and once as a timeline. Then write what each representation hides. The work is to notice that representation is already an argument about timing, information, and available choices.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: take one example and say what would change if the move order were reversed or if one player could not observe an earlier action. If the answer changes, the representation is doing real modeling work rather than simply drawing the story.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to dominance: once the situation has been represented, the first question is whether any option can be rejected before making detailed beliefs about what everyone else will do.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for the place where an economic or strategic model has to say what an actor counts as better or worse before any reasoning about their moves begins. Watch for any time a paper or problem turns a goal, payoff, preference, or outcome into a number that can be compared, added up, or optimized. That is utility at work. When you see a problem where two or more agents choose at the same time (or nearly so), where each agent's payoff depends on everyone's choice, and where the exact order of moves or who knows what information is not the main point, you are looking at a situation that fits strategic form. Look for payoff matrices, simultaneous moves, or models where timing is compressed so that strategic dependence — the fact that my payoff hangs on your choice — becomes the main thing to understand. Look for any situation where the order of moves changes what a person would do, or where a player does not have full information about what happened before. Look for threats or promises that depend on what happens later. Look for a claim about strategy or a prediction that assumes simultaneous moves when the actual sequence was different. Look for finite sequential games—situations where people move one at a time for a known number of steps and then the game ends. If you can identify the very last move and solve it, and if the player moving earlier can predict the later move, backward induction applies. Any situation where you need to check whether a threat is credible by asking will the person actually do it when they face that choice is a place where backward induction helps you think. When you see a model or situation with hidden types, secret values, private costs, private information, asymmetric information, signals, or one player knowing something the others must guess at—that is Bayesian-game territory. Look for type-indexed strategies—rules that say 'if you are type X, do action Y'—and expected-payoff best-response conditions—statements that say 'this type, given its beliefs, maximizes its expected score by following this rule.' When you see a model where players are privately informed about something and you need to check if a proposed plan is stable, Bayesian Nash is the tool.

Concepts In This Lecture

Preferences

Problem: Before anyone starts thinking strategically, we need a way to ask: which outcome does this person actually want? The catch is that outcomes are tangled up. One choice gives you more money but more risk. Another gives you less money but more free time. Before you can ask whether someone makes sense or does best for themselves, you need a way to say how they rank all these mixed-up options.

Mathematical handle: The object is a preference relation: a rule that compares any two outcomes and says which one ranks higher (or if they tie). For this rule to be useful in a model, two things have to be true. First, completeness: you must be able to compare any two outcomes in the problem — no cases where you say I do not know. Second, transitivity: if you rank A above B and B above C, then you must rank A above C — no loops.

What breaks without it: Without a clear preference ranking, the numbers in a payoff table have no real meaning. Someone could assign them however they want. There is no way to check whether the payoff numbers actually match what the player is trying to do or whether later calculations about their best move are trustworthy.

Utility

Problem: When several people each chase what is best for them, you need to know whether one person will prefer outcome A or outcome B. But you cannot write down a math formula using the words 'I like this more' — math works with numbers. So you have to turn 'A is better than B' into numbers you can actually use in a calculation.

Mathematical handle: A utility function u is a machine that takes an outcome (like 'get pizza') and outputs a number. That number is higher when the outcome ranks higher in someone's preference list. The job of u is to preserve the order — nothing more, nothing less.

What breaks without it: Without numbers, you cannot write a payoff table, calculate expected payoffs when outcomes are uncertain, or find best responses in a game. The course would be stuck describing outcomes without predicting behavior.

Open concept page

Strategic Form Games

Problem: Imagine you and your friend each need to choose something at the exact same moment, without knowing what the other chose. But once you both decide, what you get depends on both choices together. How do you show what's really going on? You need a table that captures the fact that nobody gets to see the other's choice before picking their own.

Mathematical handle: The thing you build is called a normal-form game. It collects: a list of all players, the set of moves each player can pick, and a payoff function that assigns a score to each player for every possible profile (i.e., every combination of moves, one from each player).

What breaks without it: If you only describe a game in words — a story about two people who want different things — you can talk about what seems good in theory, but you lack the structure to check your claim. You cannot easily compare what happens when player 1 chooses A versus B, or see which payoff pairs are possible. A table lets you check these things row by row and cell by cell.

Extensive Form Games

Problem: In real conflicts, people move one at a time, not all at once. A choice made later can depend on what happened earlier. And sometimes you know more than the other person — or they know more than you. A flat table misses all of that.

Mathematical handle: The object is a tree: starting point, branches for each choice, nodes where decisions happen, a record of what happened so far, sets of nodes that a player cannot tell apart (because they do not know which one they are in), and numbers at the endpoints showing each player's score.

What breaks without it: Without a tree, you would write down all actions in rows and columns, like a table. But that table pretends everyone moved at the same time and saw nothing. It would hide whether a threat is really believable, whether later moves were made with information about earlier ones, and whether a player could distinguish between different situations.

Open concept page

Backward Induction

Problem: When one person moves today and another person moves tomorrow, today's person is trying to guess what tomorrow's person will do—but people often say they'll do something harsh later, then don't do it when the moment comes. You need a way to figure out which future threats are real and which are bluffing.

Mathematical handle: The mathematical object is a game tree: a diagram with nodes (decision points), branches (the choices available), and leaves (the final outcomes with scores). Backward induction assigns a strategic value to each node by starting at the leaves, figuring out what each player will choose at the nodes just before the leaves, and then replacing those pieces with their values, stepping back one layer at a time until the whole tree is solved.

What breaks without it: Without backward induction, a game tree—a picture showing every possible history and every choice that could be made—is just a picture. You can draw all the paths, but you have no way to tell which ones will actually happen and which are empty stories. You could watch a player write down a plan (I will do A now and then B later) and have no method to check whether that plan makes sense when the later moment arrives. You would accept threats that nobody would carry out.

Bayesian Games

Problem: When you bid in an auction, you might know what an item is worth to you, but you do not know how much it is worth to your rivals. You must decide how much to bid, facing uncertainty about what they value. This is the core: strategic choice when you lack full information about who you are playing and what the other side cares about.

Mathematical handle: The object has four pieces: a set of possible hidden types (for each player), a prior—a probability for each type, a set of actions each type can take, and payoff functions that depend on actions and types. All four must work together to define the game.

What breaks without it: If you model every game as if everyone knew everything, auctions would collapse. You would treat a bidder who knows the item is worth 50 to them the same as a bidder unsure whether it is worth 20 or 80—and the model would not make sense. Signaling (like a job applicant proving they are skilled), bargaining when both sides hide their reservation prices, and most real-world contracts would all disappear from the model. The course would be describing a world that does not exist.

Open concept page

Bayesian Nash Equilibrium

Problem: Imagine you are bidding for something, but sellers do not know if you will pay high or low, and you do not know if other bidders are rich or poor. Each person's best move depends on what they guess about the hidden information around the table. The riddle: whose plan holds together when no one can see the full picture?

Mathematical handle: The object is a full rulebook: for every type of every player, a move or distribution over moves that tells that type what to do. This rulebook is stable if every type, given its beliefs about what other players' types will do, and given its own payoff from the outcome, has no reason to deviate from its assigned move.

What breaks without it: Without a Bayesian Nash lens, you cannot tell if a type-based strategy actually works. You might write down: 'rich bidders bid high, poor bidders bid low,' but you'd have no way to check if rich bidders actually want to bid high once you do the math from their point of view. The strategy might fail for one hidden type even though it sounds sensible when you average everyone together.
