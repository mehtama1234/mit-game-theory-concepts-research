Lecture 4: Rationalizability

Adds beliefs to pruning. A strategy can remain plausible if some coherent belief about others makes it a best response.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture relaxes pure deletion and asks which strategies can be justified by some coherent belief about others. It turns the question from 'is this always bad?' to 'could this be optimal under a defensible belief?'

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through best responses to beliefs. A strategy survives if there is a belief over others' actions that makes it payoff-maximizing for the player.

The mathematical object has to exist because some choices can be rejected without forecasting the opponent. The dominance comparison formalizes “no matter what they do” as a set of inequalities across contingencies. That makes deletion a proof about payoffs rather than a guess about likely behavior. The mathematical object has to exist because rationality can be layered. Once one impossible action is removed, the remaining problem changes, and new impossibilities can appear. Iterated deletion is the formal procedure that keeps track of that changing action set instead of relying on informal rounds of intuition. The mathematical object has to exist because plausible behavior depends on beliefs. A strategy can be reasonable even when it is not part of a Nash equilibrium, provided some coherent belief makes it optimal. Rationalizability formalizes that support relation between beliefs and best responses. The mathematical object has to exist because “good strategy” is incomplete without a condition. The best-response set stores the actions that maximize payoff given a named opponent action or belief. It is the reusable local object behind equilibrium, auctions, and dynamic deviation checks. The mathematical object has to exist because coordination can depend on levels of knowledge, not only on the fact itself. Recursive knowledge operators let the course distinguish private knowledge from public assurance. That distinction explains why announcements can move behavior when private messages cannot.

Worked Mini-Example

A move that is not dominant may still be rationalizable if a player reasonably expects the rival to choose a particular action. The model asks whether some belief can make the move optimal.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to confuse rationalizable with predicted. Rationalizability says a move can be justified; equilibrium will later require everyone's justifications to fit together.

Students often get stuck by deleting strategies that are merely unattractive under their favorite prediction. That is not dominance. A strategy that is bad against one opponent move but useful against another is belief-dependent, not dominated. Students often get stuck by treating the order of deletion and the type of dominance as harmless details. With weak dominance especially, those details can change what remains, so the lecture distinction matters. Students often get stuck because rationalizability feels permissive. It is permissive on purpose, but not empty: a strategy still has to be a best response to some belief, not just a move someone might randomly choose. Students often get stuck by saying an action is rational without saying relative to what. The missing belief or opponent action is usually where the real assumption is hiding. Students often get stuck because the infinite chain sounds philosophical. The course uses it for concrete coordination: people act differently when a fact is publicly shared than when each person only privately receives it.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for arguments that rule out actions before equilibrium is computed because one action is never a best answer. Look for step-by-step elimination arguments where rationality of others is used more than once. Look for models that want to separate impossible behavior from behavior that is merely one of several plausible responses. Look for arrows, reaction functions, or optimization steps that start with given the other player does X. Look for coordination failures, public announcements, or arguments that depend on what everyone knows about everyone else's knowledge.

Concepts In This Lecture

Dominance

Problem: The problem is that some choices are plainly bad no matter what the other side does. Keeping them in the analysis can distract from the strategic choices that actually need thought.

Mathematical handle: The object is a dominance comparison between two strategies across all possible actions by others.

What breaks without it: Without dominance, every listed strategy would look equally alive even when some choices have no defensible reason to be played. The analysis would be noisier and less disciplined before equilibrium reasoning even begins.

Open concept page

Iterated Deletion

Problem: The problem is that once obviously bad moves are removed, new moves may become obviously bad in the smaller game.

Mathematical handle: The object is a reduced game created by repeated dominance pruning.

What breaks without it: Without iterated deletion, the course would miss how one round of reasoning changes the next. Some games reveal their strategic structure only after obviously dominated actions are removed and the remaining incentives are reconsidered.

Open concept page

Rationalizability

Problem: The problem is deciding which strategies can be defended as sensible without requiring everyone to land on one exact equilibrium.

Mathematical handle: The object is a set of strategies that survive because each can be a best response to some belief about the other players' surviving strategies.

What breaks without it: Without rationalizability, students may jump too quickly from dominance to Nash. They would miss the middle ground where a strategy survives because a player can hold a belief that makes it a best response.

Open concept page

Best Response

Problem: The problem is that a strategy is not good in the abstract. It is good against a particular opponent action or belief.

Mathematical handle: The object is the best-response set: all actions that maximize a player's payoff given the other side's action or belief.

What breaks without it: Without best response, equilibrium would be a name without a test. The course could not check Nash profiles, bidding functions, mixed strategies, or type-contingent plans because each requires a player-level optimality calculation.

Open concept page

Common Knowledge

Problem: The problem is that coordinated action may require more than everyone privately knowing a fact. People may also need to know that others know it, and know that others know that others know it.

Mathematical handle: The object is an infinite hierarchy: the event is known, everyone knows it is known, everyone knows everyone knows it is known, and so on.

What breaks without it: Without common knowledge, the course would miss higher-order uncertainty. Players might all know the same event, yet hesitate because they do not know whether others know, or whether others know that they know.
