Lecture 14: Folk Theorem

Explains how patient repeated games can sustain many feasible and individually rational payoff patterns.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture expands the set of sustainable outcomes in patient repeated games. It teaches possibility and multiplicity: many payoff patterns can be stable when punishments and patience are strong enough.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through feasible payoffs, individual rationality, discount factors, and punishment constructions. The theorem characterizes a set rather than one unique prediction.

The mathematical object has to exist because repeated games can support sets of payoffs rather than one obvious outcome. Feasibility, individual rationality, and patience define which payoff vectors can be sustained. The theorem needs that set-based object to explain both possibility and multiplicity.

Worked Mini-Example

If players can reward and punish across a long relationship, they may sustain many different divisions of surplus, not just the one-shot Nash outcome.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to treat the folk theorem as a prediction that cooperation happens. It often says many things can happen, which raises a new selection problem.

Students often get stuck by reading the folk theorem as “cooperation happens.” The deeper message is multiplicity: many outcomes can happen, so institutions and history may be needed to select one.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for repeated-game papers showing a large set of sustainable payoffs under patience and punishment.

Folk Theorem

Concepts In This Lecture

Folk Theorem

Problem: The problem is explaining why repeated interaction can support many outcomes, not just the one-shot equilibrium.

Mathematical handle: The object is a set of feasible and individually rational payoffs that can be sustained by equilibrium strategies when the discount factor is high enough.

What breaks without it: Without the folk theorem, students may think repeated games merely select one cooperative equilibrium. The theorem shows a much larger set of feasible, individually rational outcomes supported by punishment.
