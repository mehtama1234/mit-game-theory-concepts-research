Lecture 5 · Equilibrium As Mutual Consistency, Time, Threats, And Credibility

Lecture 5: Nash Equilibrium

Closes the best-response loop: every player must be choosing optimally given the others' choices.

Open YouTube lecture

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture closes the loop created by best responses. A proposed outcome must survive each player asking, given what the others do, whether changing alone would improve their payoff.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters as mutual best response. For every player, the chosen action or strategy must lie in that player's best-response set against the other players' choices.

The mathematical object has to exist because “good strategy” is incomplete without a condition. The best-response set stores the actions that maximize payoff given a named opponent action or belief. It is the reusable local object behind equilibrium, auctions, and dynamic deviation checks. The mathematical object has to exist because strategic prediction is circular. Each player’s best action depends on the others’ actions, so the model needs a profile where all those conditional choices fit together. Nash equilibrium is the formal closure of the best-response loop. The mathematical object has to exist because pure actions cannot always carry stable predictions. A probability distribution over actions lets the player control predictability itself. The distribution becomes the strategy because it determines opponents’ expected payoffs before the random action is realized. The mathematical object has to exist because exact conflict has special structure. When one player’s payoff is the negative of the other’s, the model can collapse two objectives into one value seen from opposite sides. That structure justifies security and minimax tools that would be too strong in general games. The mathematical object has to exist because dynamic strategies are too large to compare naively. A local deviation test gives the course a finite, teachable way to verify optimality under the right assumptions. It turns the huge space of possible strategy changes into a sequence of one-history checks.

Worked Mini-Example

Two firms may each like a different price in isolation. A Nash check asks whether either firm would cut or raise price after seeing the rival's chosen price held fixed.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to read equilibrium as good, fair, or socially desirable. Nash only says no single player has a profitable one-person deviation.

Students often get stuck by saying an action is rational without saying relative to what. The missing belief or opponent action is usually where the real assumption is hiding. Students often get stuck by importing the everyday meaning of equilibrium as balance or goodness. Nash equilibrium can be inefficient or ugly. Its claim is narrower: no single player wants to move alone. Students often get stuck by asking what the player “really” chooses. The answer is that the random device is part of the strategy; after it realizes an action, the distribution was still the planned commitment. Students often get stuck by calling any rivalry zero-sum. Many competitive economic settings still have joint gains, inefficiencies, or possibilities for collusion. Zero-sum is a payoff structure, not a mood. Students often get stuck by treating the principle as universally available. It depends on the structure of the dynamic game, so the shortcut must travel with its assumptions.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for arrows, reaction functions, or optimization steps that start with given the other player does X. Look for claims that a behavior pattern persists because no individual participant can gain by deviating alone. Look for indifference equations, randomized play, or models where predictable behavior would be punished. Look for adversarial models where maximizing my payoff is exactly minimizing yours. Look for dynamic incentive proofs that replace many possible deviations with a one-period deviation inequality.

Concepts In This Lecture

Best Response

Problem: The problem is that a strategy is not good in the abstract. It is good against a particular opponent action or belief.

Mathematical handle: The object is the best-response set: all actions that maximize a player's payoff given the other side's action or belief.

What breaks without it: Without best response, equilibrium would be a name without a test. The course could not check Nash profiles, bidding functions, mixed strategies, or type-contingent plans because each requires a player-level optimality calculation.

Open concept page

Nash Equilibrium

Problem: The problem is making a prediction that does not immediately fall apart when each participant asks, given what the others are doing, should I switch?

Mathematical handle: The object is an action or strategy profile where every player's choice is a best response to the others' choices.

What breaks without it: Without Nash equilibrium, payoff tables would produce stories but not disciplined predictions. Any outcome could be proposed even if one participant has an obvious profitable deviation once everyone else behavior is named.

Open concept page

Mixed Strategies

Problem: The problem is what to predict when choosing one pure action would make a player exploitable or when no pure-action equilibrium exists.

Mathematical handle: The object is a probability distribution over pure strategies, often chosen so the other player has no strict favorite response.

What breaks without it: Without mixed strategies, the course would falsely conclude that some games have no stable prediction simply because no pure action profile works. It would also miss why randomness can be chosen as discipline rather than noise.

Open concept page

Zero-Sum Games

Problem: The problem is pure conflict: one side's gain is the other side's loss, so cooperation or joint surplus is not the issue.

Mathematical handle: The object is a payoff function where player two's payoff is the negative of player one's payoff.

What breaks without it: Without the zero-sum distinction, students may apply adversarial security logic to games where cooperation or joint surplus exists. The course needs this special case precisely because its conclusions are sharper than in general games.

Open concept page

One-Shot Deviation Principle

Problem: The problem is checking a whole dynamic strategy can be exhausting because a strategy describes what to do after many histories.

Mathematical handle: The object is a local deviation test: change one action at one history while leaving the continuation strategy fixed.

What breaks without it: Without this principle, verifying repeated or dynamic equilibria can become intractable. Students may either skip deviation checks or drown in too many possible alternative histories.
