Lecture 12 · Repeated Interaction And Cooperation

Lecture 12: Finitely Repeated Games

Shows why a known last round can unravel cooperation even when the game is played many times.

Open YouTube lecture

Naive Starting Question

The naive question is: if people meet many times, should cooperation automatically become easier? The lecture shows that a known final round can remove the future pressure that cooperation needs.

What This Lecture Teaches

The lecture shows how a known endpoint can undermine cooperation. Even many repeated rounds may unravel if everyone knows the last round has no future punishment left.

Where The Math Enters

The math enters through backward induction over periods. The last stage is solved first; if cooperation fails there, the same logic can roll backward into earlier stages.

The mathematical object has to exist because a known horizon changes every period’s incentives. The model needs to preserve the number of remaining rounds so last-period logic can be pulled backward. A finite repeated game is not just many one-shot games; it is a time-indexed strategic object.

Backward Induction

Worked Mini-Example

Two firms might want to keep prices high for ten rounds, but in round ten there is no later punishment. If round ten unravels, round nine loses its punishment threat too.

Mistakes To Avoid

The mistake is to think repetition alone creates cooperation. The future must still have disciplinary force, and a known last round can remove that force.

The tempting shortcut is to count repetitions and assume trust accumulates mechanically. That fails because players look ahead; if the last round has no future punishment, the unraveling can move backward through the whole game.

Students often get stuck by counting repetitions rather than asking whether the relationship has a strategic future at each date. Ten known rounds and an ongoing relationship are different objects.

Durable Lesson

The durable lesson is that repetition only matters when the future can discipline the present. A finite horizon with a commonly known endpoint can make future punishment vanish exactly when it is needed.

Reader Work

Solve the final period first and write what future punishment is unavailable there. Then move one period earlier and repeat. The work is to see how a known endpoint can travel backward through incentives. If cooperation survives in your notes, name the extra force stopping the unraveling.

Reader Self-Test

Self-test: write the final-round incentive first, then explain how that final answer affects the previous round. If you still predict cooperation, name the extra ingredient that blocks backward unraveling, such as reputation outside the modeled game.

How This Lecture Hands Off

This hands off to infinitely repeated games: removing the known endpoint allows future punishment and reward to become a real enforcement device again.

How To Recognize This Later

Look for known horizons and unraveling arguments that start at the final period.

Concepts In This Lecture

Finitely Repeated Games

Problem: The problem is whether repeating a game for a known number of rounds changes behavior relative to playing once.

Mathematical handle: The object is a stage game repeated T times with a known final period.

What breaks without it: Without finite repetition, students might overgeneralize from ongoing relationships and assume history always disciplines behavior. The course needs the finite case to show why the endpoint is strategically powerful.
