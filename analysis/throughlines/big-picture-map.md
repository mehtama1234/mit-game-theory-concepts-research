# Big Picture Map

MIT 14.12 is about strategic dependence: choices are hard because other people react, hold private information, remember the past, send messages, and respond to rules.

The course starts with individual choice, moves to simultaneous games, then adds timing, repetition, private information, market rules, communication, and common knowledge.

## Deep Throughlines

- Equilibrium is mutual consistency, not goodness.
- Credibility means a future action remains optimal when the future arrives.
- Beliefs are mathematical containers for hidden information.
- Repetition changes incentives by making the future part of today's payoff.
- Mechanism design asks rules to survive strategic response.
- Communication matters only through incentives and beliefs.

## Mathematical Primitives

- **Preference**: A way to say which outcome a person would rather have.
- **Payoff Mapping**: A table that says what each person gets from each combination of choices.
- **Best Response**: The choice that works best given what someone believes others will do.
- **Mutual Consistency**: A situation where each person's plan makes sense given everyone else's plan.
- **Pruning**: Remove choices that are never worth taking, then reason with the smaller problem.
- **Backward Recursion**: Solve the future first, then use that answer to decide what should happen earlier.
- **Discounting**: Future rewards count, but usually count less than rewards right now.
- **Belief**: A probability story about what kind of situation or opponent you are facing.
- **Incentive Compatibility**: Design rules so people want to reveal or do what the rule needs them to reveal or do.
- **Knowledge Recursion**: Not only knowing a fact, but knowing that others know it, and so on.

## Method Families

- **Equilibrium Family**: Equilibrium methods turn strategic dependence into a stability test.
- **Information And Belief Family**: Information methods explain how private facts, beliefs, and messages affect strategy.
- **Mechanism And Auction Family**: Mechanism methods treat game theory as design for markets and institutions.
