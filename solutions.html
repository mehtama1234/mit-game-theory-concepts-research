Solve An Entry Threat Without Believing The Announcement Too Early

Problem: A small company can enter a local delivery market. The incumbent says it will slash prices if entry happens. Fighting would hurt both firms, but accommodation would leave the incumbent with a smaller yet positive profit.

Ordinary setup: The first temptation is to treat the announcement as a fact about the future. The better first step is to separate what was said before entry from what the incumbent would want after entry has already occurred.

Model choice: Use an extensive-form game because time matters. The entrant chooses enter or stay out; if entry occurs, the incumbent chooses fight or accommodate. The model must preserve that later decision point instead of flattening the threat into a payoff-table label.

Worked solution: Solve the last decision first. At the incumbent's later node, compare fight with accommodate after entry is already sunk. If accommodation gives the incumbent 2 and fighting gives -1, accommodation is the later best response. Then move back to the entrant's choice. If entry followed by accommodation gives the entrant a positive payoff, entry survives the threat. The announcement was strategically relevant as a message, but it was not credible inside the solved tree.

Math check: Backward induction is the mathematical move. It says: choose the best action at the final decision point, replace that whole continuation with its payoff, then solve the earlier decision using that replacement. The important relation is not a fancy formula; it is the order of comparison.

Assumption audit: Check whether the incumbent truly gets another choice after entry, whether fighting is costly as described, and whether the entrant observes the same payoff logic. If fighting creates a reputation benefit in other markets, the payoff table is missing a later reward and the solution can change.

Common wrong answer: The common wrong answer says the entrant should stay out because the incumbent threatened a price war. That answer confuses a spoken plan with a credible future action and skips the later incentive check.

Transfer rule: Whenever a new paper or business story uses a threat, first ask when the threatened action would be taken. If the threatened action is not optimal at that later moment, the model should not treat the threat as a hard constraint.

Reasoning trace: The solution has three moves. First, refuse to solve from the announcement because speech before entry is not the same as choice after entry. Second, preserve the later node where the incumbent can compare fight with accommodate. Third, replace that later branch with the incumbent's actual best response and let the entrant reason from the repaired continuation. That trace is what turns the story from a threat narrative into a backward-induction answer.

Overclaim audit: This answer proves noncredibility only inside the stated payoff tree. It does not prove that entry always happens, that incumbents never fight, or that threats are useless in markets. A reputation payoff, a legal commitment, a multi-market punishment, or hidden information about fighting cost could change the later comparison and make the original announcement more than cheap talk.

Delete A Product Feature Only After Every Rival Case Has Been Checked

Problem: A platform can keep or remove a costly feature. Keeping the feature helps if a rival launches a premium product, but hurts if the rival stays basic. A manager wants to remove the feature because it looks expensive in the most likely case.

Ordinary setup: This is a pruning problem, not a prediction problem. The question is not which rival action seems likely. The question is whether one action is worse no matter what the rival does.

Model choice: Use a strategic-form table because the actions are chosen without modeling a later response. Put platform actions on one side, rival actions on the other, and compare the platform's payoffs row by row against every possible rival action.

Worked solution: Do not delete the feature if there is any rival action where keeping it does better. Suppose removing gives payoff 4 when the rival stays basic and 1 when the rival goes premium. Keeping gives payoff 2 when the rival stays basic and 5 when the rival goes premium. Removing looks better in the basic case, but keeping wins in the premium case. Neither action dominates the other. To choose between them, the model needs beliefs or a different solution concept.

Math check: Dominance uses a universal comparison: action A dominates action B only if A gives at least as much payoff against every rival action and strictly more in at least one case. The word every is doing the mathematical work.

Assumption audit: Check that the action list is complete and that the payoff entries really represent the platform's objective. If the premium rival action was accidentally omitted, deletion could look valid only because the table hid the case that protects the feature.

Common wrong answer: The wrong answer deletes the feature because one case has the highest probability. That is expected-utility reasoning, not dominance. It may be a reasonable later calculation, but it is not a dominance deletion.

Transfer rule: When reading a model that removes actions before solving, look for the universal comparison. If the author only says an action is unlikely to pay off, they have not proved dominance.

Reasoning trace: The solution begins by separating deletion from prediction. First, write every rival case as a separate comparison. Second, check whether remove beats keep in all cases, or keep beats remove in all cases. Third, if each action wins somewhere, stop the dominance argument and ask what additional belief, probability, or equilibrium concept would be needed. The discipline is knowing when the pruning tool has run out.

Overclaim audit: This answer does not say the platform should keep the feature. It says dominance alone cannot justify deletion. A later expected-value calculation with credible probabilities might still favor removal, and a richer strategic model might make one rival action much more likely. The audit is to keep the conclusion narrow: no universal deletion, not no managerial choice.

Choose Random Inspection Probabilities By Making The Attacker Indifferent

Problem: A security team can inspect morning or evening. An attacker can attack morning or evening. If the attacker guesses an uninspected time, the attacker gains; if inspection meets attack, the attacker loses.

Ordinary setup: The everyday problem is predictability. A fixed inspection time may be easy to explain inside the organization, but it gives the attacker a target. The security team needs randomness because the opponent is adapting to the pattern.

Model choice: Use a mixed-strategy game. Security chooses a probability of morning inspection; the attacker chooses a probability of morning attack. The point is not that either side feels uncertain. The point is that each side may deliberately prevent the other from exploiting a known pure action.

Worked solution: Let security inspect morning with probability p. The attacker compares attacking morning with attacking evening. If attacking morning is much better, the attacker will always attack morning; if attacking evening is much better, the attacker will switch. Security chooses p so those two expected payoffs are equal, leaving no obvious attack time. Then the attacker's mixing probability is found from the security team's comparison between inspecting morning and evening.

Math check: The key equation has the form expected payoff from morning attack equals expected payoff from evening attack. Each side's probability appears in the other side's comparison because mixing is chosen to discipline the opponent's best response.

Assumption audit: Check whether the attacker can observe or learn patterns, whether both attack times are genuinely available, and whether the loss numbers are stable. If the attacker cannot learn the pattern, a pure schedule may be fine. If there are hidden attack times, the equalization solved the wrong action set.

Common wrong answer: The wrong answer picks fifty-fifty automatically. Equal probability is only correct in symmetric payoffs. When morning and evening have different stakes, the probability must be calculated from expected payoffs.

Transfer rule: When a paper uses randomization, ask whose incentive is being shaped. In strategic settings, probabilities are often chosen so the other side has no profitable predictable response.

Reasoning trace: The solution starts from the attacker, not from the security team's favorite schedule. First, write the attacker's payoff from attacking morning as a function of the inspection probability. Second, write the payoff from attacking evening. Third, choose the inspection probability that equalizes those attacker payoffs, because unequal payoffs invite a pure attack choice. Then repeat the mirror calculation for the attacker's randomization if the security team is also mixing.

Overclaim audit: This answer proves a mixed-strategy logic only under the listed action set and payoff numbers. It does not prove that fifty-fifty is right, that randomness is always necessary, or that attackers cannot learn patterns over time. If inspections can be hidden, if extra attack windows exist, or if payoffs change by day, the equalization equation must be rebuilt.

Test Whether Future Punishment Can Hold A High-Price Path Together

Problem: Two sellers have kept prices high for months. Either seller can secretly cut price today and win extra customers, but if cheating is discovered the other seller will cut prices in every later period.

Ordinary setup: The problem exists because today's temptation is close and concrete while tomorrow's punishment is delayed. Repetition matters only if future losses are heavy enough to change today's choice.

Model choice: Use an infinitely repeated game with a proposed cooperative path and a punishment path. Name the cooperative payoff, the one-time cheating payoff, the punishment payoff, and the discount factor that measures how much future payoffs count today.

Worked solution: Compare cooperating forever with cheating once and then being punished. If cooperation gives 6 each period, cheating gives 10 today, and punishment gives 2 afterward, cooperation survives when 6 plus the discounted future value of more 6s is at least 10 plus the discounted future value of 2s. A patient seller can prefer the cooperative path because losing the future stream is worse than the one-time gain.

Math check: The mathematical object is an incentive constraint. In words: value of staying on the path must be at least value of deviating today plus punishment tomorrow. The discount factor multiplies future terms, so a higher discount factor makes future punishment more powerful.

Assumption audit: Check monitoring, punishment credibility, and the length of the relationship. If cheating cannot be detected, punishment cannot target the deviator. If the game has a known final period, backward induction can unravel cooperation.

Common wrong answer: The wrong answer says repeated interaction automatically creates cooperation. The course logic is stricter: repetition supplies a possible future cost, but the incentive inequality still has to pass.

Transfer rule: When a model claims cooperation is sustained by the future, find the one-shot deviation and the continuation loss. Without those two numbers, the claim is only a story about patience.

Reasoning trace: The solution builds the inequality in words before symbols. First, name the cooperative continuation value if the seller keeps the high price. Second, name the immediate extra gain from secret undercutting. Third, name the punishment continuation value after cheating is detected. Fourth, apply the discount factor to future terms and compare the two paths. Cooperation survives only if the lost future is large enough today. Then check the first period in which punishment starts, because delayed detection reduces the present value of discipline.

Overclaim audit: This answer does not prove collusion is likely or desirable. It proves that a proposed high-price path can be incentive-compatible under strong monitoring, patience, and punishment assumptions. If sellers are impatient, cheating is noisy, punishment is costly to the punisher, or a final period is known, the same repeated-game vocabulary may fail to support cooperation.

Decide Whether A Warranty Is A Signal Or Just Advertising

Problem: A seller can offer a warranty on a used device. High-quality sellers expect few repairs. Low-quality sellers expect many repairs. Buyers see the warranty but not the true device quality.

Ordinary setup: The problem exists because buyers cannot directly see the hidden type. A warranty can help only if the action is cheaper or more valuable for one type than another; otherwise every type can copy it.

Model choice: Use a signaling game with types, actions, buyer beliefs, and prices. The seller type is private information, the warranty is an observed action, and the buyer's willingness to pay depends on what the action says about type.

Worked solution: Suppose a warranty raises the sale price by 5. A high-quality seller expects repair cost 1, so the warranty adds net value 4. A low-quality seller expects repair cost 8, so the same warranty loses net value 3. Then offering the warranty can separate high quality from low quality. Buyers can rationally believe warranty means high quality only because low-quality imitation is unattractive.

Math check: The mathematical move is an incentive-compatibility check plus belief updating. Each type must prefer the action assigned to it, and buyers' beliefs after seeing the action must match the behavior predicted by the model.

Assumption audit: Check whether repair costs really differ by type, whether buyers observe the warranty, and whether the price premium is large enough for good sellers but not bad sellers. If warranties are cheap for everyone, the signal collapses.

Common wrong answer: The wrong answer says the warranty proves quality because it sounds reassuring. Game theory asks why a bad seller would not make the same reassuring promise if buyers rewarded it.

Transfer rule: When a paper calls something a signal, look for a separating cost or benefit. If all types can mimic cheaply, the action may still advertise, but it does not reveal hidden type by itself.

Reasoning trace: The solution works by comparing types, not by trusting the warranty label. First, compute the net warranty payoff for the high-quality seller. Second, compute the same net payoff for the low-quality seller. Third, ask whether the action separates because one type wants it and the other does not. Fourth, let buyer beliefs update only after the incentive comparison explains why the observed action came from one type. If both types choose warranty, the action pools types and should not move beliefs the same way.

Overclaim audit: This answer does not say warranties always prove quality. It says this warranty can signal quality only under the stated repair-cost and price-premium assumptions. If low-quality sellers can mimic cheaply, if buyers ignore the warranty, or if warranties are legally standardized, the same observed action may become pooled advertising rather than separating evidence.

Separate Winning, Paying, And Value In An Ad Auction Rule

Problem: An ad platform ranks ads by bid times predicted quality. A bidder asks whether raising its bid is profitable when the higher bid may improve rank but also changes payment if the ad wins.

Ordinary setup: The everyday problem is that one action changes several things at once. A higher bid can increase the chance of getting shown, alter the price paid, and interact with quality. Treating the winning bid as the whole story hides the mechanism.

Model choice: Use an auction model with private value, allocation rule, payment rule, and expected payoff. The bidder does not only compare prices; it compares value from clicks minus expected payment under each possible bid.

Worked solution: Break the bid change into pieces. First ask how the bid changes rank or probability of winning. Second ask what payment is made if the ad wins. Third multiply by the value of clicks or impressions. A bid is attractive only if the extra expected value from better allocation exceeds the extra expected payment and any quality tradeoff.

Math check: The expected payoff has the shape: chance of winning times value if winning minus chance of paying times payment if winning. Each term must be read separately because auction rules can change probability and payment in different directions.

Assumption audit: Check whether bidders know their values, whether quality scores are fixed or strategically changeable, and whether the payment rule is first-price, second-price, or generalized. Revenue-equivalence conclusions require a narrow environment, not just the word auction.

Common wrong answer: The wrong answer says the highest bid always wins and pays that bid. That misses quality weighting, allocation probability, and payment rules, which are exactly the design levers in ad auctions.

Transfer rule: When reading a mechanism paper, translate the rule into allocation and payment before judging incentives. Rules are not labels; they are machines that turn private values and actions into consequences.

Reasoning trace: The solution separates the mechanism into moving parts. First, identify the bidder's private value for clicks. Second, identify how bid and quality affect rank or probability of winning. Third, identify the payment conditional on winning. Fourth, compare the expected surplus before and after the bid change. The answer is not the highest visible bid; it is the private payoff created by allocation probability and payment together. A useful scratch calculation writes old expected surplus on one line and new expected surplus on the next.

Overclaim audit: This answer does not prove the platform's auction is optimal or truthful. It only gives the incentive calculation a bidder must run under the stated rule. If quality scores are manipulable, budgets bind, reserve prices change, click predictions are wrong, or the payment rule differs from the model, the expected-payoff comparison has to be rebuilt from the actual mechanism.

Decide Whether A Warning Changes Beliefs Or Only Creates Noise

Problem: A project lead privately knows whether a launch is risky. The lead sends a message saying delay is wise. Engineers must decide whether the message should change their launch decision.

Ordinary setup: The problem exists because words can be cheap. A message can carry information only if the speaker's incentives make some messages more likely in some states than others.

Model choice: Use an information game with hidden state, sender incentives, receiver beliefs, and receiver action. The message is not automatically evidence; it becomes evidence only through the link between state, incentives, and speech.

Worked solution: Ask what the lead would say when launch is safe and what the lead would say when launch is risky. If the lead always wants delay because delay avoids blame, then the same warning appears in both states and carries little information. If the lead wants launch when safe and delay when risky, then the message can separate states and rationally move engineers' beliefs. The receiver's job is therefore not to judge whether the sentence sounds serious; it is to compare the sender's incentives across hidden states.

Math check: The mathematical move is belief updating over messages. The receiver asks: how likely is this message in each state? A message shifts beliefs only when those likelihoods differ for strategic reasons.

Assumption audit: Check sender incentives, message observability, and whether lying or exaggerating is costly. If the message is public, common-knowledge effects may matter too; if it is private, coordination may still fail even when the content is believed.

Common wrong answer: The wrong answer treats all warnings as truthful information or all cheap talk as useless. The course distinction is sharper: cheap talk can matter, but only through incentives and beliefs.

Transfer rule: When a model includes communication, ask what the sender would say in every state. The informational content of a message is the pattern of incentives behind it, not the surface sentence, so communication has to be read as behavior chosen under incentives.

Reasoning trace: The solution treats the message as an action chosen by an informed sender. First, list the hidden states: safe launch and risky launch. Second, ask what message the lead wants to send in each state. Third, compare whether the warning is more likely in one state than another. Fourth, let engineers update beliefs only if the sender's incentives make the message state-dependent rather than automatic.

Overclaim audit: This answer does not say cheap talk is always useless or always informative. It says the warning's content depends on alignment between sender incentives and receiver action. If lying is costly, career incentives differ, the message is public, or engineers can verify the risk independently, the communication game changes and the belief update may become stronger or weaker. The conclusion should name which messages separate states and which messages pool them.

Explain Why Public Confirmation Matters After Everyone Privately Heard The Rumor

Problem: Every trader has privately heard that a policy change is coming, but no trader knows whether the others heard it. Later, the regulator publicly announces the same policy change.

Ordinary setup: The factual content may be old for each individual, but the public event changes what everyone can assume about everyone else. Strategic action depends on other people's expectations, not only on private facts.

Model choice: Use a common-knowledge model rather than a simple private-information update. Track the fact, who knows the fact, who knows that others know, and whether that recursive awareness is shared enough to coordinate action.

Worked solution: Before the announcement, a trader may hesitate because selling is costly if others do not also sell. After the public announcement, each trader knows the others heard it and knows that the others can reason from the same public event. The same policy fact can therefore support a different equilibrium behavior because the belief hierarchy changed. The announcement works like a shared starting line: it gives everyone a reason to expect that everyone else is solving from the same premise.

Math check: The mathematical move is knowledge recursion. The relation is not just probability of the policy change; it is layers of belief about other players' beliefs. Common knowledge compresses those layers into a public event that everyone can use.

Assumption audit: Check that the announcement was public, that the audience understood it similarly, and that coordinated action is actually payoff-relevant. If the announcement is ambiguous or seen by only some traders, the common-knowledge conclusion is too strong.

Common wrong answer: The wrong answer says the announcement adds nothing because everyone already knew the policy. That treats information as private facts only and misses the strategic role of shared awareness.

Transfer rule: When a paper says a public signal matters, ask whether the mechanism is new facts, shared interpretation, or higher-order belief. Those are different reasons for behavior to change, and confusing them makes private information look stronger than it is.

Reasoning trace: The solution writes two knowledge states side by side. Before the announcement, each trader may privately know the fact but lack confidence about others' knowledge. After the announcement, each trader knows others received the same public event and can reason about their reasoning. The solution therefore does not count new factual content; it counts the change in the shared belief hierarchy that makes coordinated action safer.

Overclaim audit: This answer does not prove every public announcement changes behavior. It proves why public confirmation can matter when payoffs depend on coordinated expectations. If the announcement is ambiguous, distrusted, privately received, or irrelevant to coordination, the same factual message may fail to become common knowledge or may fail to affect equilibrium behavior. The audit question is whether publicness changes strategic expectations, not merely whether it repeats a fact.
