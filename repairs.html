Misconception Repairs

A correction map for common wrong turns. Start from the mistaken belief, then follow the repair into concepts, limits, drills, and transcript evidence.

Misconception repair

A payoff or utility number is a direct measurement of happiness, so a bigger number always means more real-world well-being in a literal unit.

Why it is tempting: The course writes utilities as numbers, and numbers feel like measurements. It is natural to read them like dollars, inches, or points on a scoreboard, especially once the same numbers start appearing inside payoff tables and expected-value calculations.

First-principles repair: Utility is usually a model's way to preserve choice order and support calculation. The number is meaningful inside the chosen representation, but it does not automatically measure psychological happiness or compare welfare across people. The mathematical object exists because the model needs a stable way to say this chooser prefers one outcome to another before strategy begins.

Diagnostic question: Am I using this number to preserve a ranking inside a model, or am I making a stronger real-world welfare claim that the model has not justified?

What breaks if ignored: If this mistake is ignored, the analyst may compare payoffs across people as if the units were naturally shared, treat a risk-neutral utility model as a claim about emotion, or turn a convenient payoff table into an unsupported welfare conclusion.

Worked correction: When a payoff table says player A gets 4 and player B gets 2, first read those numbers within each player's representation. For A, 4 beats A's lower-numbered outcomes. For B, 2 has meaning inside B's ranking. Nothing in those two entries alone proves A is twice as happy as B or that society should prefer A's gain.

Transfer test: In a new paper, circle every payoff or objective. Ask what real-world preference it represents, whether the scale matters only up to ranking, and whether any interpersonal or welfare comparison has been separately justified.

Wrong shortcut pattern: The shortcut appears whenever a reader treats the payoff column as if it were a ruler for human welfare. The language often shifts from 'player i prefers this' to 'this is objectively better' without adding a welfare theorem, a social objective, or a reason the units are comparable across people.

Repair in plain steps: Repair it in three steps. First, name whose preference the number represents. Second, ask whether only the ranking matters or whether the spacing between numbers matters. Third, separate prediction from welfare: the payoff may explain what a player chooses without proving what a planner should want.

Boundary warning: The repair is not saying welfare analysis is impossible. It says welfare analysis needs extra structure. If the paper explicitly defines a social welfare function, transferable utility, money-metric payoffs, or risk preferences, then the numbers may carry more meaning than a bare ordinal ranking.

Evidence note: The early decision-making evidence supports utility as a representation used before later payoff-table and expected-utility calculations.

Misconception repair

A strategy can be deleted as dominated because it looks bad in the case that seems most likely.

Why it is tempting: Students often bring an informal prediction into the comparison. Once a move looks unattractive under that favorite prediction, deletion feels safe. The mistake is especially tempting when the likely case has vivid real-world detail and the other cases feel like edge conditions.

First-principles repair: Dominance is not a prediction about what the opponent will probably do. It is a universal comparison across every relevant opponent action in the model. If the supposedly bad move is useful in even one relevant case, dominance has not been shown. The mathematical principle is strong precisely because it does not need a belief about the opponent.

Diagnostic question: Did I compare this action against the alternative under every opponent action, or only under the scenario I expect?

What breaks if ignored: If likelihood replaces universal comparison, the deletion step quietly changes from dominance to belief-based expected payoff. That can erase actions that matter later for equilibrium, rationalizability, or signaling, and the reader may never notice that a belief assumption entered the proof.

Worked correction: Suppose high price beats low price if the rival is calm, but low price beats high price if the rival is aggressive. High price may be attractive under your favorite forecast, but low price is not dominated. Keep both actions until the model supplies beliefs or a best-response calculation.

Transfer test: When a paper deletes actions, look for the word every in the comparison. If the text says usually, likely, empirically rare, or intuitively unattractive, the argument is no longer a dominance proof without an added belief model.

Wrong shortcut pattern: The shortcut appears when a solution skips from 'this action seems unattractive' to 'delete it.' It often hides in words like implausible, unlikely, unreasonable, or dominated-looking. Those words may be useful intuition, but they are not the dominance test unless every relevant contingency has been compared.

Repair in plain steps: Repair it by making a comparison grid. Put the action you want to delete beside the action that supposedly beats it. Then walk through each opponent action and mark the payoff comparison. Only after every row or column passes can deletion be called dominance rather than a belief-based shortcut.

Boundary warning: The repair is stricter than many applied arguments. A paper may deliberately use empirical likelihood or equilibrium beliefs to prune actions, but then the evidence should be labeled as a belief or estimation assumption, not as dominance. Weak dominance needs even more care because deletion order can matter.

Evidence note: The dominance and rationalizability evidence anchors support the distinction between strict pruning and belief-based plausibility.

Misconception repair

If an outcome is a Nash equilibrium, it must be good, fair, efficient, or what the group collectively wants.

Why it is tempting: The word equilibrium sounds like balance or social order. It is easy to import a positive meaning that the mathematical test does not contain. Many textbook examples also present equilibrium as the answer, which can make answer feel like recommendation.

First-principles repair: Nash equilibrium is a unilateral deviation test. It says no single player wants to move alone, holding the others fixed. It does not say the outcome is socially best, fair, stable against coalitions, or morally desirable. The concept matters because a bad outcome can still be hard to escape when each individual move away is punished by the fixed behavior of others.

Diagnostic question: Am I checking individual incentives to deviate, or am I smuggling in a welfare judgment about the whole outcome?

What breaks if ignored: If equilibrium is treated as goodness, prisoner-dilemma-style outcomes, aggressive pricing, inefficient coordination failures, and bad conventions become falsely sanitized. The reader loses the course's central distinction between prediction and evaluation.

Worked correction: If two firms both price aggressively, each may prefer mutual softness but still undercut if the other stays soft. The aggressive pair can pass the unilateral-deviation test even though both firms would like a coordinated alternative. The equilibrium claim is about stability, not praise.

Transfer test: In new work, separate three questions: is this outcome stable against unilateral deviations, is it efficient for the group, and is it normatively acceptable? A Nash label answers only the first unless the paper adds more machinery.

Wrong shortcut pattern: The shortcut appears when equilibrium language gets moralized. Watch for a paper or explanation that moves from 'this is the equilibrium' to 'this is the right outcome,' 'the market works,' or 'players are satisfied' without checking efficiency, surplus, distribution, or alternative institutions.

Repair in plain steps: Repair it by splitting the claim into three boxes. Box one is stability: can any one player profitably move alone? Box two is welfare: are total gains high or low? Box three is distribution: who gets those gains? Nash equilibrium fills box one, and the other boxes need separate arguments.

Boundary warning: The repair does not make equilibrium useless. Stability is a powerful prediction filter. The warning is that stability is local to the modeled deviations and payoffs. Add coalitions, repeated play, communication, or mechanism design, and a bad equilibrium may become avoidable. Also check whether the model has multiple equilibria; selecting one stable outcome still needs an explanation.

Evidence note: The best-response and Nash evidence anchors support equilibrium as mutual consistency, while imperfect-competition examples show stable outcomes can still be socially unattractive.

Misconception repair

A threat should be treated as real because it is announced before the other player chooses.

Why it is tempting: Announcements happen early in the story, and early statements can feel like fixed constraints on later behavior. Human conversation also treats promises and threats as socially meaningful, so it is easy to forget that the model has to test the later incentive separately.

First-principles repair: A future action is credible only if the player would still want to take it when the future decision point arrives. Sequential reasoning asks the later self, not only the earlier announcement. The mathematical idea is backward recursion: solve the future node under the payoffs that exist then, and only afterward interpret the earlier move.

Diagnostic question: At the node where the threat must be carried out, is carrying it out still better for the player who made the threat?

What breaks if ignored: If announced threats are accepted without later checks, a model can predict deterrence from empty speech. Entry threats, bargaining ultimatums, and platform enforcement rules all become too powerful because the analyst never asks whether carrying them out is costly later.

Worked correction: Draw the tree. If the entrant enters, the incumbent chooses fight or accommodate. If fighting gives the incumbent -1 and accommodation gives 2, the later node selects accommodation. The entrant should reason from that continuation, not from the earlier aggressive announcement.

Transfer test: In a paper or business case, jump to the moment when the threat must be executed. Ignore sunk speeches and compare the remaining payoffs. If execution is not best there, look for a commitment device before treating the threat as credible.

Wrong shortcut pattern: The shortcut appears when the first announcement is treated like a binding law of nature. The story may say 'the incumbent threatens,' 'the platform promises,' or 'the buyer commits,' but never revisits the moment when that action must actually be chosen.

Repair in plain steps: Repair it by jumping to the future node. Ignore the earlier speech for a moment and ask what the threatening player gets from carrying out the threat versus backing down. Then pull that later choice backward into the earlier decision. The earlier player should believe payoffs, not just announcements.

Boundary warning: The repair leaves room for real commitment. A threat can become credible through contracts, sunk investments, reputation, automation, legal penalties, or repeated-game consequences. If one of those devices changes the later payoff, then the backward-induction calculation must include it. The question is not whether the threat sounds serious, but what payoff table the later self faces.

Evidence note: The extensive-form, backward-induction, and subgame-perfection evidence anchors support the course's credibility test for dynamic games.

Misconception repair

If players interact repeatedly, cooperation should follow because people care about the relationship and expect future contact.

Why it is tempting: Repeated interaction feels socially richer than a one-shot encounter, so it is tempting to treat the future as automatically disciplining behavior. Everyday relationship language also makes patience sound moral rather than mathematical.

First-principles repair: Repetition only helps when future consequences are strong enough. The model compares today's deviation gain with the discounted future loss from punishment or lost cooperation. The future is a force in the model only after monitoring, punishment, and patience turn it into an incentive that changes the present best response.

Diagnostic question: What is the one-time gain from cheating, what future loss follows, and how much does the player value that future?

What breaks if ignored: If repetition is treated as automatic cooperation, finite endings, weak monitoring, low patience, and noncredible punishments disappear from the analysis. The folk-theorem intuition then becomes a slogan instead of a conditional result about payoffs, feasible punishments, and continuation value.

Worked correction: If cheating gives 10 today instead of 6, but triggers future payoffs of 2 instead of 6, compare the one-time gain of 4 with the discounted stream of future losses. A patient player may cooperate; an impatient player may cheat. Repetition alone does not decide the answer.

Transfer test: When a paper says cooperation is sustained by repeated interaction, find the incentive constraint. Identify the deviation payoff, cooperative continuation, punishment continuation, monitoring assumption, and discount factor before accepting the claim as more than a repeated-contact story.

Wrong shortcut pattern: The shortcut appears when repetition is used as a magic word. A text may say firms interact often, workers have relationships, or platforms see users repeatedly, and then infer cooperation without writing the deviation gain, monitoring process, punishment path, or discount factor.

Repair in plain steps: Repair it by writing the incentive inequality in words. What does the player gain by cheating today? What future payoff is lost afterward? How likely and how soon is punishment? How much does the player value that future? Cooperation needs the future loss to beat the current gain. Then ask whether the punisher wants to punish after cheating is observed.

Boundary warning: The repair does not deny that repeated relationships can sustain cooperation. It says they do so only under conditions. Noisy monitoring, known final periods, low patience, weak punishments, legal limits, or too many possible equilibria can make repeated contact a weak predictor. Repetition creates a possibility, not a guarantee.

Evidence note: The repeated-game, folk-theorem, and cartel evidence anchors support future discipline as an incentive calculation, not a general claim about relationships.

Misconception repair

In Bayesian or signaling models, beliefs can be chosen freely to make the desired story work.

Why it is tempting: Beliefs are not directly observed, so they can feel like adjustable narrative glue. It is also common for solution writeups to state beliefs compactly, hiding the type distribution, observed action, and equilibrium-path logic that made those beliefs legitimate.

First-principles repair: Beliefs are part of the model's discipline. They must be tied to types, priors, observed actions, Bayes' rule where applicable, and the incentives that make different types choose different actions. In signaling, the receiver's belief and the sender's action have to support each other.

Diagnostic question: Does this belief follow from the modeled type distribution and observed behavior, or is it being inserted just to rescue the conclusion?

What breaks if ignored: If beliefs are unconstrained, any story can be made to look Bayesian. A warranty can reveal quality without costly imitation checks, a message can persuade without incentive alignment, and a perfect-Bayesian-equilibrium claim can hide arbitrary off-path assumptions.

Worked correction: List high and low types. Ask what each type would do if buyers reward the observed action. If both types cheaply choose the same warranty, seeing a warranty should not strongly raise the buyer's belief that the seller is high quality. The belief must follow from differential behavior.

Transfer test: In new work, trace every posterior back to a prior, a signal or action, and a likelihood under each type. Then check whether the type assigned to an action actually wants to take that action instead of imitating another type.

Wrong shortcut pattern: The shortcut appears when a belief is inserted exactly where the story needs it. The model says buyers believe a seller is high quality, receivers believe a sender is honest, or employers believe education means ability, but the action and type incentives that would create that belief are not checked.

Repair in plain steps: Repair it by tracing the belief backward. Start with the prior type probabilities. Ask how likely each type is to take the observed action. Use that comparison to form the posterior when Bayes' rule applies. Then check whether each type actually wants to behave in the way the belief assumes.

Boundary warning: The repair is hardest off the equilibrium path. Some beliefs are not pinned down by Bayes' rule after events assigned zero probability. In those cases, the paper should state the refinement, restriction, or robustness argument rather than pretending the belief was mechanically forced. If a tiny prior change overturns the result, mark the conclusion as belief-sensitive.

Evidence note: The type, perfect-Bayesian-equilibrium, signaling, and screening evidence anchors support beliefs as disciplined responses to hidden information and observed behavior.

Misconception repair

Auction analysis is just about who bids the most and how much the winner pays after bidding ends.

Why it is tempting: Many everyday auctions are described by the visible winning bid, so the rule behind allocation and payment can disappear from view. Ad auctions make the problem worse because the public may see rankings but not quality weights, reserve prices, or payment formulas.

First-principles repair: Auction rules are incentive machines. A bidder responds to the probability of winning, payment if winning, private value, information about rivals, and any quality or ranking rule built into the mechanism. The mathematical principle matters because the same bid can change allocation and payment through different channels.

Diagnostic question: How does the rule change allocation, payment, winning probability, and the reason to reveal or shade private value under strategic pressure?

What breaks if ignored: If the rule is reduced to highest bid wins, the reader misses bid shading, quality scoring, expected payment, revenue-equivalence assumptions, and strategic changes induced by mechanism design. The model becomes a price story instead of an incentive story.

Worked correction: Separate the event of winning from the payment conditional on winning. If raising a bid increases winning probability but also raises expected payment, the bidder compares the extra value from better allocation with the extra payment. In an ad auction, quality can shift that comparison again.

Transfer test: When reading any auction or platform rule, write two lines before solving: who gets allocated, and what do they pay in each event? Then ask how a participant can change each line by bidding, shading, improving quality, entering, or leaving.

Wrong shortcut pattern: The shortcut appears when an auction is described only by the winning bid. That view skips the strategic machinery: how a bid changes allocation probability, what payment rule applies after winning, what information bidders have, and whether platform scores or reserves alter the rule.

Repair in plain steps: Repair it by decomposing the mechanism. Write the allocation rule, the payment rule, the bidder's private value, and the bidder's belief about rivals. Then ask what happens if the bidder raises or lowers the bid. The incentive lives in that comparison, not in the final winning number alone. For ad auctions, repeat the decomposition for quality scores and click probabilities.

Boundary warning: The repair matters even more in platforms. Quality scores, click predictions, repeated advertiser learning, budgets, and reserve prices can all change incentives. A clean auction theorem transfers only after those institutional features are either modeled or shown to be irrelevant to bidding behavior.

Evidence note: The auction, revenue-equivalence, and ad-auction evidence anchors support treating auction formats as rule systems that shape incentives.

Misconception repair

If every player privately knows the same fact, then the fact is automatically common knowledge for coordination.

Why it is tempting: The phrase everyone knows sounds complete. It hides the higher-order question of whether everyone knows that everyone knows. In ordinary speech, shared truth and public awareness are often collapsed into one phrase, so the extra recursive requirement feels pedantic until coordination fails.

First-principles repair: Common knowledge is recursive publicness. Players need not only the fact, but confidence that others know it, know that others know it, and so on. The mathematical object exists because coordination can depend on beliefs about other people's beliefs, not only on the underlying fact.

Diagnostic question: Is the fact merely true, privately known by all, mutually known, or publicly anchored through the recursive chain needed for coordination?

What breaks if ignored: If private shared knowledge is treated as common knowledge, public announcements look useless when they may be strategically decisive. Coordination failures, market runs, and communication games become misread because the reader ignores the belief hierarchy.

Worked correction: Every trader may privately hear a policy rumor, yet hesitate because selling alone is costly. A public announcement changes the situation by making each trader confident that others heard the same fact and know that others heard it too. The factual content can stay fixed while strategic meaning changes.

Transfer test: In new work, separate first-order information from publicness. Ask whether the model needs a fact, everyone knowing the fact, everyone knowing everyone knows it, or a public event that anchors the whole recursive chain and lets players safely reason from the same premise.

Wrong shortcut pattern: The shortcut appears when a model says everyone knows the fact and then treats coordination as solved. The hidden missing step is whether each player knows that others know, and knows that others know that others know, far enough to support the strategic action.

Repair in plain steps: Repair it by climbing the knowledge ladder. First ask whether the fact is true. Then ask who privately knows it. Then ask whether the event that revealed it was public enough for each player to reason from other players' awareness. Coordination needs the public event, not only the fact.

Boundary warning: The repair does not mean every model needs infinite notation on the page. Often a public announcement, shared meeting, or institutional rule can carry the common-knowledge assumption. The danger is using that assumption when the actual information arrived privately, noisily, or unevenly. If players can doubt who else heard it, coordination may still fail.

Evidence note: The common-knowledge and cheap-talk evidence anchors support the distinction between private information, publicness, and coordination through shared awareness.
